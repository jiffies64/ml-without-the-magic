Calculus for ML

The chain rule is not a dusty calculus footnote. It is backpropagation — the exact rule that lets a network of millions of numbers figure out how to improve itself.

32 min read
You'll learn
  • derivatives
  • partial derivatives
  • gradients
  • the chain rule

Last section we learned to represent data as vectors and to push it through matrices. But a model that never changes is useless. Learning means nudging the numbers inside those matrices, over and over, in the direction that makes the model a little less wrong. Calculus is the mathematics of change — it is the tool that tells us which way, and how hard, to nudge. This one section contains the single most important idea in the whole course, so we'll go slowly and derive everything.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

A derivative tells you which way to push a number to make an output go up or down. A neural network learns by asking, for every one of its numbers, “if I wiggle you a tiny bit, does the error get better or worse?” — then wiggling accordingly. That question is a derivative, and answering it efficiently for millions of numbers at once is the entire trick behind training. Everything below builds to that.

1Derivatives: the slope at a single instant

Intuition first

A derivative measures how fast something is changing right now. If a quantity $y$ depends on an input $x$, the derivative answers: “if I increase $x$ by a tiny amount, how much does $y$ move, and in which direction?” Geometrically it is the slope of the curve at one point — how steeply it rises or falls at that exact spot. A positive slope means “going up”; a negative slope means “going down”; a slope of zero means “flat, for a moment.”

☂ Everyday parallel

Look at your car's speedometer. The odometer tells you total distance travelled; the speedometer tells you how fast that distance is changing at this instant — that is a derivative. Sixty km/h means “if this instant held, you'd cover 60 km in the next hour.” You don't have to wait an hour to know your speed now; the derivative reads the rate of change in the moment. Speed is the derivative of distance.

Now the math

Here is the honest problem. Slope is “rise over run” — change in output divided by change in input — but at a single point there is no run to divide by. The clever fix: measure the slope over a tiny step of width $h$, then shrink $h$ toward zero and see what number the slope settles on. That settling-down process is a limit (the value an expression approaches as you push a variable toward something). This gives the definition of the derivative:

$$ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

In words: take two points on the curve a tiny distance h apart — one at x, one at x + h. The fraction is (change in output) ÷ (change in input): the slope of the little line joining them. As h shrinks to nothing (h → 0), that little line hugs the curve tighter and tighter, until its slope becomes the slope at the single point x. The result f'(x) (read “f-prime of x”) is the derivative.

Let's actually compute one from scratch, so the limit stops being abstract. Take $f(x) = x^2$ and grind the definition:

$$ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \, (2x + h) \\[4pt] &= 2x \end{aligned} $$

In words: we expanded (x + h)², the two lone x² terms cancelled, then we divided every surviving term by h. That left 2x + h. Now let h fall to zero and the leftover h simply vanishes, leaving 2x. So the slope of x² at any point is 2x: at x = 3 the curve rises with slope 6; at x = 0 the slope is 0 (the bottom of the bowl, momentarily flat). We didn't guess — we derived it.

Definition

The derivative of a function is another function that reports the original's slope at every point. We write it $f'(x)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$ (read “dee-f dee-x”), which literally means “a tiny change in f per tiny change in x.” Both notations mean the same thing.

The same thing in PyTorch

PyTorch never runs the limit by hand — it applies known rules like our $2x$ automatically. You mark an input as something to track, compute an output, and call .backward() to get the derivative:

the derivative of x² at x = 3

import torch

x = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)   # "watch this number"
y = x ** 2                                   # f(x) = x^2

y.backward()                                 # compute df/dx at x = 3
print(x.grad)                                # tensor(6.)  -> 2*x = 2*3 = 6

In words: requires_grad=True tells PyTorch to remember how y was built from x. backward() walks that record and fills x.grad with the derivative — exactly the 2x = 6 we derived. You'll rarely differentiate by hand again, but you must know what the machine is computing.

Check yourself Using the limit definition, what is the derivative of the constant function f(x) = 5? What does the answer mean? Reveal

Plug in: f(x + h) = 5 and f(x) = 5, so the top of the fraction is 5 − 5 = 0. That gives 0 / h = 0 for every non-zero h, so the limit is 0. Meaning: a flat, horizontal line has slope zero everywhere — a constant never changes, so its rate of change is nothing. This is why bias terms don't affect the slope with respect to an input, and why constants drop out when you differentiate.

2Partial derivatives: change one input, freeze the rest

Intuition first

Real models don't depend on a single number — they depend on thousands. A function like $f(x, y)$ has two inputs, and “the slope” is ambiguous: slope as we change $x$, or as we change $y$? A partial derivative answers one at a time. It measures how the output changes as you nudge one single input, while holding all the others perfectly still. Nothing new to fear — it's the ordinary derivative from the start of this section, just with everything except the chosen variable treated as a frozen constant.

☂ Everyday parallel

Your shower has two taps: hot and cold. The water temperature depends on both. To learn what the hot tap alone does, you leave the cold tap exactly where it is and turn only the hot one, feeling how the temperature responds. That is a partial derivative: the effect of one input while the other is held fixed. Turn only the cold tap instead and you're measuring the other partial. Each tap has its own separate influence, and you probe them one at a time.

Now the math

We swap the straight $d$ for a curly $\partial$ (“partial”) to signal “there are other inputs, but I'm holding them still.” To take $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, treat every other variable as a plain number. Take $f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3 $$

In words: for ∂f/∂x we treat y as a frozen constant. Then x²y differentiates like (constant)·x², giving 2xy, and the 3y term has no x in it at all, so it's constant and drops to zero. For ∂f/∂y we freeze x instead: x²y is (constant)·y, whose slope in y is just that constant x², and 3y contributes 3. Same rules as before — only one variable moves at a time.

The same thing in PyTorch

two partials at once

import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.tensor(5.0, requires_grad=True)

f = x**2 * y + 3*y        # f(x, y) = x^2 * y + 3y
f.backward()              # fills in BOTH partials

print(x.grad)   # tensor(20.)  -> 2*x*y = 2*2*5 = 20
print(y.grad)   # tensor(7.)   -> x^2 + 3 = 4 + 3 = 7

In words: one call to backward() computes the partial with respect to every tracked input in a single sweep. That's the whole reason training is feasible: the machine gets the influence of all the knobs together, not one slow experiment per knob.

Check yourself For f(x, y) = 4x + y³, what is ∂f/∂x? Why does the y³ term disappear? Reveal

∂f/∂x = 4. When we differentiate with respect to x, we freeze y — so y³ is just some fixed constant number, and the derivative of any constant is zero (we saw this at the start of this section). The 4x term has slope 4 in x. So ∂f/∂x = 4. The y³ term only shows up if you ask for ∂f/∂y, which would be 3y².

3The gradient: the direction of steepest climb

Intuition first

If a function has many inputs, collect all its partial derivatives into one vector. That vector is the gradient, written $\nabla f$ (the symbol $\nabla$ is called “nabla” or just “del”). The gradient is more than bookkeeping: it points in the direction where the function increases fastest, and its length says how steep that increase is. Flip its sign and you get the direction of steepest decrease — which, when the function is our error, is exactly the way we want to move.

☂ Everyday parallel

You're on a mountainside in thick fog, trying to get down. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the ground under your feet. You sense which way tilts most steeply downward, take a step that way, then feel again and repeat. The steepest direction under your feet is the (negative) gradient; the fog is your model's ignorance of the whole landscape. You never see the map — you only ever feel the local slope, and that is enough to walk downhill.

Now the math

For a function of $n$ inputs, the gradient is the column of every partial derivative, one per input:

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\[4pt] \vdots \\[4pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: slot i of the gradient is “how much the output changes when I nudge input i.” Stacked together, these slots form an arrow in input-space. That arrow aims at the fastest uphill direction — big values mean inputs that matter a lot right now, near-zero values mean inputs that barely move the output at this point.

Definition

Gradient descent is the recipe for minimizing a function: from wherever you stand, compute the gradient, then take a small step in the opposite direction (downhill). Repeat. Because the negative gradient is the steepest way down, each step lowers the value a little, and enough steps carry you toward a minimum. In ML the function being minimized is the loss (a number measuring how wrong the model is — smaller is better), so descending it means the model gets better.

The step itself is one short update rule, applied again and again:

$$ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \alpha\,\nabla L $$

In words: θ (“theta”) stands for the model's parameters — all the numbers inside those weight matrices from Section 1. ∇L is the gradient of the loss L with respect to those parameters: the uphill-toward-more-error direction. We subtract it, so we move toward less error. α (“alpha”) is the learning rate: a small positive number setting how big each step is. The arrow ← means “compute the right side, then overwrite θ with it.” Do this thousands of times and the parameters drift to values that make the loss small.

▲ Watch out

The learning rate $\alpha$ is the step size, and it is delicate. Too small and training crawls — thousands of tiny steps to get anywhere. Too large and you overshoot the valley on every step, bouncing from wall to wall or flying off to infinity (the loss becomes NaN — “not a number”). Picking $\alpha$ is the first knob you'll tune on almost every model.

The same thing in PyTorch

gradient descent on f(x) = x², walking to the bottom

import torch

x = torch.tensor(10.0, requires_grad=True)   # start far from the minimum
lr = 0.1                                      # learning rate (alpha)

for step in range(5):
    loss = x ** 2                 # we want to minimize this
    loss.backward()              # grad = df/dx = 2x
    with torch.no_grad():        # don't track the update itself
        x -= lr * x.grad         # theta <- theta - alpha * grad
    x.grad.zero_()               # clear grad before the next step
    print(f"x = {x.item():.4f}")

# x marches 10 -> 8 -> 6.4 -> ... toward 0, the minimum of x^2

In words: each loop reads the slope at the current spot, steps against it by lr * grad, and the value slides down the bowl toward zero. Clearing x.grad matters because PyTorch adds new gradients onto old ones; forget it and last step's slope contaminates this one.

Check yourself You're minimizing a loss and the gradient with respect to a weight is +4. With learning rate 0.1, does the weight go up or down, and by how much? Reveal

The update is θ ← θ − α·∇L = θ − 0.1·(+4) = θ − 0.4. The weight goes down by 0.4. A positive gradient means “increasing this weight increases the loss,” so to reduce the loss we push the weight the other way — downward. The minus sign in the update rule is precisely what turns “which way is uphill” into “step downhill.”

4The chain rule: this is backpropagation

Intuition first

Neural networks are compositions: functions wrapped inside functions inside functions. The input feeds layer 1, whose output feeds layer 2, whose output feeds layer 3, and so on to the loss at the end. To train, we need the derivative of that final loss with respect to a weight buried deep inside — many layers away from the output. The chain rule is the tool that reaches back through the whole stack. Its idea is beautifully simple: the derivative of a chain of functions is the product of the little derivatives along the chain. Each link contributes its own local rate, and you multiply them together.

☂ Everyday parallel

You're changing money: US dollars → euros → pounds. Say 1 dollar buys 0.9 euros, and 1 euro buys 0.85 pounds. How many pounds does 1 extra dollar get you? You multiply the rates: 0.9 × 0.85 = 0.765 pounds per dollar. Each conversion is a link in a chain, and the overall rate is the product of the link rates. Gears mesh the same way: if one gear turns twice as fast as the next, and that one twice as fast as the last, the first spins four times the last — rates multiply along the chain. That multiplying-of-rates is the chain rule.

Now the math

Suppose $y$ depends on $u$, and $u$ depends on $x$ — a two-link chain $x \to u \to y$. The chain rule says:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} $$

In words: “how y changes as x moves” equals “how y changes as u moves” times “how u changes as x moves.” The middle variable u cancels visually, like a fraction — a handy way to remember it. Each factor is a local derivative, easy on its own; the chain rule glues them by multiplication.

Let's work a full single-variable example. Take $y = (3x + 1)^2$. We split it into the inner and outer pieces:

$$ u = 3x + 1, \qquad y = u^2 $$

Now the two local derivatives, each trivial on its own:

$$ \frac{dy}{du} = 2u, \qquad \frac{du}{dx} = 3 $$

Multiply them, then substitute $u = 3x+1$ back in:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \\[4pt] &= 2u \cdot 3 \\[4pt] &= 6u \\[4pt] &= 6(3x + 1) \\[4pt] &= 18x + 6 \end{aligned} $$

In words: we found the outer function's slope (2u, the derivative of a square), the inner function's slope (3, the derivative of 3x + 1), and multiplied them. Then we put u = 3x + 1 back so the answer is in terms of the original x. We can check it: expanding (3x + 1)² = 9x² + 6x + 1, whose ordinary derivative is 18x + 6 — a perfect match. The chain rule gave the same answer without ever expanding the square.

The multivariable chain rule: sum over every path

In a network a variable usually influences the output through several routes at once. When that happens, the chain rule gains one addition: differentiate along each path, then add the paths up. If $L$ depends on $a$ and $b$, and both of those depend on $x$:

$$ \frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{da}{dx} \;+\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\cdot\frac{db}{dx} $$

In words: x reaches L two ways — through a and through b. Each route is a chain-rule product (multiply the rates along it), and because both routes carry influence, we sum them. More paths, more terms in the sum. Every knob's total effect is the sum of its effects along all routes to the output. That's the whole rule.

◆ Why this is the beating heart of ML

Backpropagation is nothing but the chain rule applied to a neural network. The network is a long composition of layers; the loss sits at the far end. To learn, we need the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight, however deep. Backprop computes it by starting at the loss and multiplying local derivatives layer by layer as it walks backward — exactly $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$, chained across the whole network, summing wherever paths merge. When PyTorch's .backward() ran in the code above, this is precisely what it did. We build it by hand and see every multiplication in Section 10.

The same thing in PyTorch

the chain rule, computed for us

import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
y = (3*x + 1) ** 2           # the composed function from above

y.backward()                 # chain rule runs automatically
print(x.grad)                # tensor(42.)  -> 18*x + 6 = 18*2 + 6 = 42

In words: PyTorch recorded that y was built as a square of (3x + 1). backward() walked that chain outer-to-inner, multiplying 2u by 3 just as we did by hand, and delivered 42 — matching our derived 18x + 6 at x = 2. The rule you just learned is literally the engine under the hood.

Check yourself Let y = (x² + 1)³. Using the chain rule with inner u = x² + 1, find dy/dx. Reveal

Outer: y = u³ gives dy/du = 3u². Inner: u = x² + 1 gives du/dx = 2x. Multiply them: dy/dx = 3u² · 2x = 6x·u² = 6x(x² + 1)². Notice how the answer stays compact — the chain rule spared us from expanding a cube. This is exactly why networks can be deep: each layer just contributes one more local derivative to multiply into the chain.

5The Jacobian: a gradient for every output at once

Intuition first

So far the output was a single number. But a layer usually maps a vector of inputs to a vector of outputs, so there are many partial derivatives to track: each output slot has its own slope against each input slot. Arrange all of them in a grid — one row per output, one column per input — and you get the Jacobian matrix. It is simply the gradient idea scaled up: where a single-output function has one gradient vector, a multi-output function has a whole matrix of partials. The multivariable chain rule for vectors then becomes plain matrix multiplication of Jacobians — which is why Section 1's shape rules and this section's chain rule meet inside every real network, and why backprop is, at bottom, a sequence of matrix products.

6What to carry forward

  • A derivative is an instantaneous rate of change — the slope at a single point, defined by shrinking a step $h$ to zero. The slope of $x^2$ is $2x$.
  • A partial derivative is the ordinary derivative with respect to one input while all others are frozen — the hot tap while the cold stays put.
  • The gradient $\nabla f$ stacks every partial into a vector that points uphill fastest. Gradient descent steps the other way to shrink a loss: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\nabla L$, with $\alpha$ the learning rate.
  • The chain rule multiplies local derivatives along a composition, and sums over paths when they merge. This is backpropagation — the exact mechanism we build in Section 10.
  • The Jacobian generalizes the gradient to many outputs, turning the vector chain rule into matrix multiplication.

You now hold the two halves of ML's foundation: linear algebra to move data, and calculus to improve the model. Next we bring in the third — probability — so a model can express not just an answer, but how sure it is.
