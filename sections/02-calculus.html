Calculus for ML

The chain rule is not a small side topic. It is backpropagation: the rule that lets a network of millions of numbers work out how to improve itself.

46 min read
You'll learn
  • derivatives
  • partial derivatives
  • gradients
  • the chain rule
  • the Jacobian

In the last section we learned to represent data as vectors and to push it through matrices. But a model that never changes is useless. Learning means changing the numbers inside those matrices, again and again, in the direction that makes the model a little less wrong. Calculus is the mathematics of change. It tells us which way to change each number, and by how much. The ideas here are central to the whole course, so we go slowly and derive everything.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

A derivative tells you which way to push a number to make an output go up or down. A neural network learns by asking each of its numbers: “if I change you a tiny bit, does the error get better or worse?” Then it changes each number accordingly. That question is a derivative. Training is the job of answering it efficiently for millions of numbers at once.

1Derivatives: the slope at a single point

Intuition first

A derivative measures how fast something changes right now. Suppose a quantity $y$ depends on an input $x$. The derivative answers: “if I increase $x$ by a tiny amount, how much does $y$ move, and in which direction?” In a graph, it is the slope of the curve at one point: how steeply the curve rises or falls there. A positive slope means “going up.” A negative slope means “going down.” A slope of zero means “flat, at this point.”

☂ Everyday parallel

Look at a car's speedometer. The odometer shows the total distance traveled. The speedometer shows how fast that distance changes at this moment, and that is a derivative. 60 km/h means “at this rate, you would cover 60 km in the next hour.” You do not have to wait an hour to know your speed now. Speed is the derivative of distance with respect to time.

Now the math

Here is the problem. Slope is “rise over run”: change in output divided by change in input. But at a single point there is no run to divide by. The fix: measure the slope over a small step of width $h$, then shrink $h$ toward zero and see which number the slope settles on. This settling process is a limit (the value an expression approaches as a variable approaches some value). It gives the definition of the derivative:

$$ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

In words: take two points on the curve, one at $x$ and one at $x+h$. The fraction is (change in output) ÷ (change in input). It is the slope of the short line that joins the two points, called a secant. As $h$ shrinks to zero ($h \to 0$), that line fits the curve more and more closely. Its slope becomes the slope at the single point $x$. The result $f'(x)$ (read “f-prime of x”) is the derivative.

Let's compute one from scratch, so the limit stops being abstract. Take $f(x) = x^2$ and apply the definition step by step:

$$ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \, (2x + h) \\[4pt] &= 2x \end{aligned} $$

In words: we expanded $(x+h)^2$, and the two lone $x^2$ terms canceled. Then we divided every remaining term by $h$, which left $2x + h$. Now let $h$ go to zero: the leftover $h$ disappears, and $2x$ remains. So the slope of $x^2$ at any point is $2x$. At $x = 3$ the curve rises with slope 6. At $x = 0$ the slope is 0 (the bottom of the bowl, where the curve is flat). We did not guess; we derived it.

The definition also gives a rule we will use many times. For a small step $h$, the fraction is already close to $f'(x)$. Multiply both sides by $h$:

$$ f(x+h) \;\approx\; f(x) + f'(x)\,h \qquad \text{(for small } h\text{)} $$

In words: near a point, a smooth curve looks like a straight line with slope $f'(x)$. Move the input by $h$, and the output moves by about $f'(x)$ times $h$. For $x^2$ at $x=3$: $f(3.01) = 9.0601$, and the rule predicts $9 + 6 \times 0.01 = 9.06$.

Definition

The derivative of a function is another function that gives the original's slope at every point. We write it $f'(x)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$ (read “dee-f dee-x”). The second form means “a tiny change in $f$ per tiny change in $x$.” Both notations mean the same thing.

The same thing in PyTorch

PyTorch never computes the limit numerically. It applies known rules, like our $2x$, for you. You mark an input as something to track, compute an output, and call .backward() to get the derivative:

the derivative of x² at x = 3

import torch

x = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)   # "track this number"
y = x ** 2                                   # f(x) = x^2

y.backward()                                 # compute df/dx at x = 3
print(x.grad)                                # tensor(6.)  -> 2*x = 2*3 = 6

In words: requires_grad=True tells PyTorch to record how $y$ was built from $x$. backward() walks back through that record and stores the derivative in x.grad. It is the $2x = 6$ we derived. You will rarely differentiate by hand again, but you must know what the machine computes.

Check yourself Use the limit definition: what is the derivative of the constant function $f(x) = 5$? And for $y = wx + b$, what is $dy/dx$, and why does $b$ not appear in it? Reveal

For $f(x)=5$: $f(x+h) - f(x) = 5 - 5 = 0$. The fraction is $0/h = 0$ for every non-zero $h$, so the limit is 0. A flat line has slope zero everywhere; a constant never changes. For $y = wx + b$: the $wx$ part has slope $w$, and the constant $b$ adds 0. So $dy/dx = w$. The bias moves the line up or down but does not tilt it. (This is true for this straight line. Once a curved function is applied after $wx+b$, the slope can depend on $b$.)

2Partial derivatives: change one input, freeze the rest

Intuition first

Real models depend on thousands of numbers, not one. A function like $f(x, y)$ has two inputs, so “the slope” is unclear: the slope as we change $x$, or as we change $y$? A partial derivative answers one at a time. It measures how the output changes as you change one input while you hold all the others still. It is the ordinary derivative from Section 1. Every variable except the chosen one is treated as a fixed constant.

☂ Everyday parallel

A shower has two taps: hot and cold. The water temperature depends on both. To learn what the hot tap alone does, leave the cold tap where it is. Turn only the hot one, and feel how the temperature responds. That is a partial derivative: the effect of one input while the other is held fixed. Turn only the cold tap instead, and you measure the other partial derivative.

Now the math

We replace the straight $d$ with a curly $\partial$ (“partial”). It signals: “there are other inputs, but I am holding them still.” To compute $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, treat every other variable as a plain number. Take $f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3 $$

In words: for $\partial f/\partial x$ we treat $y$ as a constant. Then $x^2y$ behaves like (constant) × $x^2$, which gives $2xy$. The term $3y$ has no $x$ in it, so it is a constant, and its derivative is zero. For $\partial f/\partial y$ we freeze $x$ instead. Now $x^2y$ is (constant) × $y$, whose slope in $y$ is the constant $x^2$. The term $3y$ adds 3. The rules are the same as before; only one variable moves at a time.

The same thing in PyTorch

two partial derivatives at once

import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.tensor(5.0, requires_grad=True)

f = x**2 * y + 3*y        # f(x, y) = x^2 * y + 3y
f.backward()              # fills in BOTH partial derivatives

print(x.grad)   # tensor(20.)  -> 2*x*y = 2*2*5 = 20
print(y.grad)   # tensor(7.)   -> x^2 + 3 = 4 + 3 = 7

In words: one call to backward() computes the partial derivative for every tracked input in a single pass. This is what makes training possible: the machine gets the effect of all the inputs together, not one slow experiment per input.

Check yourself For $f(x, y) = 4x + y^3$, what is $\partial f/\partial x$? At the point $(1, 2)$, which input changes $f$ faster, $x$ or $y$? Reveal

$\partial f/\partial x = 4$. When we differentiate with respect to $x$, we freeze $y$. So $y^3$ is a fixed number, and its derivative is zero. The other partial is $\partial f/\partial y = 3y^2$, which is $12$ at $y = 2$. So at $(1, 2)$, a small change in $y$ moves $f$ three times as much as the same change in $x$. At $y = 0.5$ the answer would flip: $3y^2 = 0.75 < 4$. Partial derivatives depend on the point.

3The gradient: the direction of steepest climb

Intuition first

If a function has many inputs, collect all its partial derivatives into one vector. That vector is the gradient, written $\nabla f$ (the symbol $\nabla$ is called “nabla” or “del”). The gradient points in the direction in which the function increases fastest. Its length says how steep that increase is. Flip its sign, and you get the direction of steepest decrease. When the function is our error, that is the way we want to move.

☂ Everyday parallel

You are on a mountainside in thick fog, and you want to get down. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel the ground under your feet. You feel which way slopes down most steeply, take a step that way, then feel again and repeat. The steepest downhill direction under your feet is the negative gradient. Like the model, you never see the whole map. You only feel the local slope, and that is enough to walk downhill.

Now the math

For a function of $n$ inputs, the gradient is the column of all partial derivatives, one per input:

$$ \nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\[4pt] \vdots \\[4pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: slot $i$ of the gradient is “how much the output changes when I change input $i$.” Together, these slots form an arrow in input space. Large values mark inputs that matter a lot at this point. Values near zero mark inputs that barely move the output here.

Why does this arrow point uphill fastest? Take a small step of length $\varepsilon$ in some direction $\mathbf{u}$ (a vector of length 1). The straight-line rule from Section 1, applied to each input, says the output changes by about the sum of (partial derivative × step) over all inputs. That sum is a dot product:

$$ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x}) &\approx \varepsilon \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,u_i = \varepsilon\,\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} \\[4pt] &= \varepsilon\,\lVert\nabla f\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\cos\theta = \varepsilon\,\lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\theta$ is the angle between your step and the gradient. We used the geometric form of the dot product from Section 1. The change is largest when $\cos\theta = 1$, that is, when you step along $\nabla f$. It is most negative when $\cos\theta = -1$: step along $-\nabla f$. It is zero when you step at a right angle to the gradient. That is the direction along a contour line, where the height stays the same.

Definition

Gradient descent is a method for minimizing a function. From where you stand, compute the gradient, then take a small step in the opposite direction (downhill). Repeat. If the step size is small enough, each step lowers the value, and enough steps carry you toward a minimum. In ML the function we minimize is the loss $L$ (a number that measures how wrong the model is; smaller is better). So going down the loss means the model gets better.

The step is one short update rule, applied again and again:

$$ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \alpha\,\nabla L $$

In words: $\theta$ (“theta”) stands for the model's parameters, all the numbers inside the weight matrices from Section 1. $\nabla L$ is the gradient of the loss $L$ with respect to those parameters: the direction toward more error. We subtract it, so we move toward less error. $\alpha$ (“alpha”) is the learning rate, a small positive number that sets the size of each step. The arrow $\leftarrow$ means “compute the right side, then replace $\theta$ with it.”

Why “small enough”? Use the dot-product result above with the step $-\alpha\nabla L$:

$$ L(\theta - \alpha\nabla L) \;\approx\; L(\theta) - \alpha\,\nabla L\cdot\nabla L \;=\; L(\theta) - \alpha\,\lVert\nabla L\rVert^2 $$

In words: the straight-line estimate says the loss drops by $\alpha\lVert\nabla L\rVert^2$, which is never negative. But the estimate holds only for small steps. With a large $\alpha$, the curve bends away from the straight line before you land, and the loss can go up instead.

▲ Watch out

The learning rate $\alpha$ is the step size, and it needs care. Too small, and training is very slow: thousands of tiny steps to get anywhere. Too large, and you overshoot the valley on every step. You bounce from wall to wall, or the values grow without limit until the loss becomes NaN (“not a number”). In the code below, lr = 1.1 instead of 0.1 sends $x$ to −12, 14.4, −17.28, and further out on every step. Choosing $\alpha$ is the first setting you will tune on almost every model.

The same thing in PyTorch

gradient descent on f(x) = x², walking to the bottom

import torch

x = torch.tensor(10.0, requires_grad=True)   # start far from the minimum
lr = 0.1                                      # learning rate (alpha)

for step in range(5):
    loss = x ** 2                 # we want to minimize this
    loss.backward()              # grad = df/dx = 2x
    with torch.no_grad():        # don't track the update itself
        x -= lr * x.grad         # theta <- theta - alpha * grad
    x.grad.zero_()               # clear grad before the next step
    print(f"x = {x.item():.4f}")
# x = 8.0000
# x = 6.4000
# x = 5.1200
# x = 4.0960
# x = 3.2768   -> heading toward 0, the minimum of x^2

In words: each pass reads the slope at the current point and steps against it by lr * grad. Here each step multiplies $x$ by $1 - 2\alpha = 0.8$, so the value slides down the bowl toward zero. Clearing x.grad matters because PyTorch adds new gradients to old ones. Forget it, and the previous step's slope mixes into this one.

Check yourself You minimize a loss, and the gradient with respect to one weight is $+4$. With learning rate $0.1$, does the weight go up or down, and by how much? In the code above, which learning rate would reach $x = 0$ in one step? Reveal

The update is $\theta - 0.1 \times 4 = \theta - 0.4$. The weight goes down by 0.4. A positive gradient means “increasing this weight increases the loss,” so we push the weight the other way. For $x^2$ each step multiplies $x$ by $1 - 2\alpha$. That is zero when $\alpha = 0.5$, so one step lands exactly on the minimum. Above $\alpha = 1$, the factor $|1-2\alpha|$ is bigger than 1, and $x$ grows: the overshoot in the Watch-out box.

4The chain rule: this is backpropagation

Intuition first

Neural networks are compositions: functions inside functions inside functions. The input feeds layer 1. Its output feeds layer 2, whose output feeds layer 3, and so on to the loss at the end. To train, we need the derivative of that final loss with respect to a weight deep inside, many layers away from the output. The chain rule reaches back through the whole stack. Its idea is simple: the derivative of a chain of functions is the product of the small derivatives along the chain. Each link contributes its own local rate, and you multiply them.

☂ Everyday parallel

You change money: US dollars → euros → pounds. The rates change every day. Today 1 dollar buys 0.9 euros, and 1 euro buys 0.85 pounds. How many pounds does 1 extra dollar get you today? You multiply today's rates: $0.9 \times 0.85 = 0.765$ pounds per dollar. Tomorrow the rates are different, and so is the product. In the chain rule, too, each local rate depends on where you are (the slope of $u^2$ is $2u$). You multiply the rates at the current point.

Now the math

Suppose $y$ depends on $u$, and $u$ depends on $x$: a two-link chain $x \to u \to y$. The chain rule says:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} $$

In words: “how $y$ changes as $x$ moves” equals “how $y$ changes as $u$ moves” times “how $u$ changes as $x$ moves.” The middle variable $u$ seems to cancel, like in a fraction, which helps you remember the rule. Each factor is a local derivative, easy on its own. The chain rule joins them by multiplication.

Where does the rule come from? Use the straight-line rule from Section 1 twice. A small change $\Delta x$ changes $u$ by about $\frac{du}{dx}\Delta x$. That change in $u$ changes $y$ by about $\frac{dy}{du}$ times itself:

$$ \Delta u \approx \frac{du}{dx}\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta y \approx \frac{dy}{du}\,\Delta u \approx \frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx}\,\Delta x $$

In words: divide by $\Delta x$ and let it shrink to zero. The ratio $\Delta y/\Delta x$ becomes $dy/dx$, and the right side becomes the product of the two local derivatives. (A fully careful proof handles the limits more precisely, but this is the idea.)

Let's work a full example. Take $y = (3x + 1)^2$ and split it into an inner and an outer piece:

$$ u = 3x + 1, \qquad y = u^2 $$

Now the two local derivatives, each easy on its own:

$$ \frac{dy}{du} = 2u, \qquad \frac{du}{dx} = 3 $$

Multiply them, then put $u = 3x+1$ back in:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \\[4pt] &= 2u \cdot 3 \\[4pt] &= 6u \\[4pt] &= 6(3x + 1) \\[4pt] &= 18x + 6 \end{aligned} $$

In words: first we found the outer function's slope ($2u$, the derivative of a square). Then we found the inner function's slope (3, the derivative of $3x + 1$). We multiplied them. Then we put $u = 3x + 1$ back, so the answer is in terms of $x$. Check it: expand $(3x + 1)^2 = 9x^2 + 6x + 1$, whose derivative is $18x + 6$. It matches, and the chain rule never needed the expansion.

The multivariable chain rule: sum over every path

In a network, a variable usually affects the output through several routes at once. Then the chain rule gains one addition: differentiate along each path, then add the paths up. Suppose $L$ depends on $a$ and $b$, and both of those depend on $x$:

$$ \frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{da}{dx} \;+\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\cdot\frac{db}{dx} $$

In words: $x$ reaches $L$ in two ways, through $a$ and through $b$. Each route is a chain-rule product (multiply the rates along it). Both routes carry an effect, so we add them. More paths mean more terms in the sum. The reason is the same straight-line rule, now with two inputs: $\Delta L \approx \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\Delta a + \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\Delta b$. Replace $\Delta a$ and $\Delta b$ with their own straight-line estimates, and divide by $\Delta x$.

◆ Why this matters for ML

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a neural network. The network is a long composition of layers, and the loss is at the far end. To learn, we need the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight, however deep. Backpropagation starts at the loss and multiplies local derivatives layer by layer as it walks backward. It adds the products wherever paths meet. When PyTorch's .backward() ran in the code above, this is what it did. We build it by hand, with every multiplication visible, in Section 10.

The same thing in PyTorch

the chain rule, computed for us

import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
y = (3*x + 1) ** 2           # the composed function from above

y.backward()                 # the chain rule runs automatically
print(x.grad)                # tensor(42.)  -> 18*x + 6 = 18*2 + 6 = 42

In words: PyTorch recorded that $y$ is the square of $3x + 1$. backward() walked that chain from the outside in. It multiplied $2u$ by 3, as we did by hand, and returned 42. That matches $18x + 6$ at $x = 2$. The rule you learned is the rule PyTorch runs.

Check yourself Let $y = (x^2 + 1)^3$. Use the chain rule with inner $u = x^2 + 1$ to find $dy/dx$. Then imagine a chain of 30 links, each with local derivative 0.5. What is the derivative of the whole chain? Reveal

Outer: $y = u^3$ gives $dy/du = 3u^2$. Inner: $u = x^2 + 1$ gives $du/dx = 2x$. Multiply: $dy/dx = 3u^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2 + 1)^2$. The chain rule saved us from expanding a cube. For 30 links: $0.5^{30} \approx 9 \times 10^{-10}$, almost zero. This is why gradients in deep networks can be computed at all (each layer adds one factor to a product). It is also why they can vanish or explode when many factors are below or above 1. We return to this in Section 12.

5The Jacobian: a gradient for every output at once

Intuition first

So far the output was a single number. But a layer usually maps a vector of inputs to a vector of outputs. Each output has its own slope with respect to each input. Put all of these slopes in a grid, with one row per output and one column per input. That grid is the Jacobian matrix. It extends the gradient idea. A function with one output has one gradient vector. A function with many outputs has a matrix of partial derivatives.

☂ Everyday parallel

A mixing desk in a music studio has several input sliders (drums, bass, voice) and several output speakers. Each speaker's volume responds to each slider by some amount. Write those amounts in a table: one row per speaker, one column per slider. That table is a Jacobian. Row $i$ tells you how speaker $i$ responds to every slider.

Now the math

For a function $\mathbf{f}$ with $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs, the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ has shape $(m \times n)$. Its entry in row $i$, column $j$ is

$$ J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j} $$

In words: row $i$ is the gradient of output $i$, laid on its side. Column $j$ shows how all outputs respond to input $j$.

An example with two inputs and two outputs: $\mathbf{f}(x, y) = \big[\,x^2 y,\; 5x + \sin y\,\big]$.

$$ \mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2xy & x^2 \\ 5 & \cos y \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\;x=1,\ y=2\;}\; \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 5 & -0.416 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now chain two such functions: $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$, then $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})$. Each input $x_j$ reaches each output $y_i$ through every middle variable $u_k$. The multivariable chain rule sums over those paths:

$$ \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j} = \sum_k \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_j} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}\,\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}} $$

In words: the sum on the left is “row $i$ of $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{g}$ dotted with column $j$ of $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{f}$.” That is the rule for matrix multiplication from Section 1. So the chain rule for vector functions is a matrix product of Jacobians. This is where Section 1's shape rules and this section's chain rule meet. Backpropagation is a sequence of such products (in practice, it never builds the full matrices, as Section 10 shows).

The same thing in PyTorch

the Jacobian of a two-output function

import torch
from torch.autograd.functional import jacobian

def f(v):                                   # f(x, y) = [x^2 * y,  5x + sin(y)]
    x, y = v[0], v[1]
    return torch.stack([x**2 * y, 5*x + torch.sin(y)])

J = jacobian(f, torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0]))   # at the point x = 1, y = 2
print(J)
# tensor([[ 4.0000,  1.0000],
#         [ 5.0000, -0.4161]])

In words: jacobian computes every partial derivative of every output. Row 0 is the gradient of $x^2y$, and row 1 is the gradient of $5x + \sin y$. The numbers match the matrix we derived by hand.

Check yourself $\mathbf{f}$ maps 3 numbers to 2 numbers, and $\mathbf{g}$ maps 2 numbers to 4 numbers. What are the shapes of $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{f}$, $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{g}$, and the Jacobian of $\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}$? Why must the product be $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{g}\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{f}$ and not $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{f}\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{g}$? Reveal

$\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{f}$ is $(2 \times 3)$: 2 outputs, 3 inputs. $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{g}$ is $(4 \times 2)$. The chain $\mathbf{g}\circ\mathbf{f}$ maps 3 numbers to 4, so its Jacobian is $(4 \times 3)$. The shape rule confirms the order: $(4\times 2)(2\times 3) = (4\times 3)$, with the inner 2s matching. The other order, $(2\times 3)(4\times 2)$, has inner numbers 3 and 4, so it is not even defined.

6What to carry forward

  • A derivative is a rate of change at one point: the slope, defined by shrinking a step $h$ to zero. The slope of $x^2$ is $2x$. Near a point, $f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h$.
  • A partial derivative is the ordinary derivative with respect to one input while all others are held fixed: the hot tap while the cold tap stays put.
  • The gradient $\nabla f$ stacks all partial derivatives into a vector that points uphill fastest. Gradient descent steps the other way to shrink a loss: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\nabla L$, with a learning rate $\alpha$ that is small enough.
  • The chain rule multiplies local derivatives along a composition, and adds over paths when they meet. This is backpropagation, which we build in Section 10.
  • The Jacobian extends the gradient to many outputs, $J_{ij} = \partial f_i/\partial x_j$. The chain rule for vector functions becomes a matrix product of Jacobians.

You now have two of the three foundations of ML: linear algebra to move data, and calculus to improve the model. Next comes the third, probability, so that a model can give not only an answer but also how sure it is.
