Logistic Regression

One S-shaped curve turns a raw number into a probability. One loss function turns wrong probabilities into a clear, learnable signal.

36 min read
You'll learn
  • sigmoid
  • probabilities
  • decision boundaries
  • binary cross-entropy

Linear regression predicts a number: a price, a temperature, a score. Many real questions want a category instead. Is this email spam? Will this patient get sick again? Does this photo contain a cat? Logistic regression uses almost the same machinery as linear regression. It adds one extra part that turns the output into a probability.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Compute a plain linear score, as in linear regression. Then pass that score through a curve whose output is always between 0 and 1, so you can read it as “probability of class 1”. The rest of this section builds that curve and the loss that trains it.

1Classification vs. regression

Intuition first

Regression predicts a continuous number, anywhere on a scale. Classification predicts which category, from a fixed set, an example belongs to. The output is not “how much” but “which one”.

☂ Everyday parallel

A weather forecast does both jobs. “Tomorrow’s high will be 22 °C” is regression: a number on a continuous scale, and missing by half a degree is a small mistake. “Will it rain tomorrow, yes or no?” is classification. There is no “half a rain”. There are two categories, and the model says which one is more likely.

Check yourself You want to predict how many stars (1 to 5) a customer will give a product. Is this regression or classification? Reveal

It can be either, and the choice matters. As classification, the model treats the five ratings as unrelated categories, so predicting 4 when the truth is 5 is as wrong as predicting 1. As regression, the model knows that 4 is close to 5, but it may predict values like 3.7 that no customer can give. Many people choose regression here and round the result. This shows that the problem does not decide the type alone. How you score mistakes decides it.

2The sigmoid: turning any number into a probability

Intuition first

A linear score $wx+b$ can be any real number: huge, tiny, or negative. A probability must be between 0 and 1. The sigmoid function is an S-shaped curve. It takes any real number and returns a number between 0 and 1. So we can give it a raw linear score and get back something we may call a probability.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think about a student’s chance of passing an exam as their study hours grow. With almost no study, the chance is near 0%. In the middle range, each extra hour helps a lot. After many hours, the chance is already close to 100%, so a few more hours change it very little. The chance rises in a smooth S-shape and never quite reaches 0% or 100%. Hours play the role of the score $z$, and the chance plays the role of $\sigma(z)$.

Now the math

For any real number $z$, the sigmoid is:

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} $$

In words: $e^{-z}$ shrinks toward 0 as $z$ grows. It grows toward infinity as $z$ becomes very negative. So the fraction moves toward $1/(1+0)=1$ for large positive $z$, and toward 0 for very negative $z$. That is the squashing we wanted.

The input $z$ is called the logit. The name comes from the inverse of the sigmoid. Solve $p = \sigma(z)$ for $z$:

$$ \begin{aligned} p\,(1+e^{-z}) &= 1 \\ e^{-z} &= \frac{1-p}{p} \\ z &= \log\frac{p}{1-p} \end{aligned} $$

In words: $p/(1-p)$ is the odds: the probability that something happens divided by the probability that it does not. So the logit is the log-odds. A logit of 0 means odds of 1 to 1. Each step of +1 in the logit multiplies the odds by $e \approx 2.7$.

We will need two more properties later. First, the midpoint:

$$ \sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1+e^{0}} = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac12 $$

In words: a logit of zero gives a probability of one-half. Both classes are equally likely.

Second, the sigmoid’s derivative. We need it for the gradient of the loss. Write $\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ and apply the chain rule (Section 2):

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\sigma}{dz} &= -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z}) \\ &= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} \\ &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} \\ &= \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) \end{aligned} $$

In words: the last line uses $1-\sigma(z) = \tfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}$ (subtract the fraction from 1 and simplify). So the slope of the sigmoid is $\sigma(z)$ times $1-\sigma(z)$. Both factors are positive, so the slope is always greater than 0. The slope is largest at $z=0$, where it equals $0.25$. It shrinks toward 0 at both ends, where the curve is almost flat.

The same thing in PyTorch

the sigmoid at three logits

import torch

z = torch.tensor([-5.0, 0.0, 5.0])
print(torch.sigmoid(z))   # tensor([0.0067, 0.5000, 0.9933])
Check yourself $\sigma(3) \approx 0.953$. Without a calculator, what is $\sigma(-3)$? Reveal

About $0.047$, because $\sigma(-z) = 1-\sigma(z)$ for every $z$. To see why, multiply the top and bottom of $\sigma(-z) = \tfrac{1}{1+e^{z}}$ by $e^{-z}$. You get $\tfrac{e^{-z}}{e^{-z}+1}$, which is the expression for $1-\sigma(z)$ from the derivation above. The output above shows the same symmetry: 0.0067 and 0.9933 add up to 1.

3From logit to probability to decision boundary

Intuition first

Logistic regression computes the same linear score as linear regression, then passes it through the sigmoid. At some places the model changes from predicting class 0 to predicting class 1. Together these places form the decision boundary. For logistic regression, the boundary is a straight line (or a flat plane in higher dimensions). Nothing about it is curved.

Now the math

The logit is the linear-regression formula:

$$ z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b $$

Pass it through the sigmoid to get the probability of class 1:

$$ p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b)}} $$

In words: $z$ can be any real number, depending on $\mathbf{x}$. $p$ is that number squashed into a probability between 0 and 1.

The prediction rule is: predict class 1 when $p\ge 0.5$. We showed that $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)) > 0$ everywhere. So the sigmoid is strictly increasing: a larger $z$ always gives a larger $p$. We also showed $\sigma(0)=\tfrac12$. Together:

$$ p \ge \tfrac12 \iff \sigma(z)\ge\sigma(0) \iff z\ge 0 $$

In words: the sigmoid always goes up, and it passes one-half at $z=0$. So these two questions always have the same answer: “is the probability at least one-half?” and “is the logit at least zero?” The curved probability test becomes a straight-line test on the score.

The boundary is where $p = 0.5$ exactly, so it is the set of points where $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b=0$. With two features $x_1,x_2$, rearrange this into the equation of a line:

$$ \begin{aligned} w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b &= 0 \\ x_2 &= -\frac{w_1}{w_2}x_1 - \frac{b}{w_2} \end{aligned} $$

In words: this is a line in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane with slope $-w_1/w_2$ and intercept $-b/w_2$ (assuming $w_2 \ne 0$). With three features the boundary is a flat plane. With more, it is a flat hyperplane. It is always flat, even though the probability changes along a curve as you cross it.

Check yourself With $\mathbf{w} = [2, -1]$ and $b = -3$, where is the point $\mathbf{x} = [2, 1]$, and what does the model predict for it? What if you double both $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$? Reveal

$z = 2\cdot 2 + (-1)\cdot 1 - 3 = 0$, so the point is on the boundary and $p = \sigma(0) = 0.5$. The model is equally unsure about both classes. The rule “predict 1 when $p \ge 0.5$” still gives an answer: class 1. Doubling $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ does not move the boundary, because $2z = 0$ exactly where $z = 0$. It does make the probabilities change faster: the dashed lines in Figure 6.2 move closer to the boundary.

4The loss: binary cross-entropy

Intuition first

You might reuse Mean Squared Error on the probability: predicted probability minus true label, squared. That choice makes training slow and unreliable. Classification uses binary cross-entropy instead, a loss built for comparing probabilities.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine scoring a weather forecaster. Each day, look at the probability they gave to what really happened, and charge them $-\log$ of it. If they said 90% rain and it rained, the charge is small ($-\log 0.9 \approx 0.1$). If they said 1% rain and it rained, the charge is large ($-\log 0.01 \approx 4.6$). A forecaster who says “0%” and is wrong pays an unlimited price. This is binary cross-entropy.

Definition

A function is convex when its graph is bowl-shaped: the straight segment between any two points on the graph never goes below the graph. A convex function has no false valleys. Every local minimum is a global minimum, so gradient descent (with a small enough learning rate) cannot get stuck in a bad dip. A strictly convex function has at most one lowest point. A convex function can still have no minimum at all (think of $e^{-z}$, which keeps going down toward 0), and this matters below.

Now the math

First, where binary cross-entropy comes from. The label $y$ is 0 or 1, so it is a Bernoulli variable (Section 3). The model says “$y = 1$ with probability $\hat y$”. The probability the model gives to the true label can be written in one formula:

$$ P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \hat y^{\,y}\,(1-\hat y)^{1-y} $$

In words: if $y = 1$, the formula gives $\hat y$. If $y = 0$, it gives $1-\hat y$. The exponents only select the right factor.

Maximum likelihood says: make this probability large. That is the same as making its negative log small:

$$ \begin{aligned} L &= -\log P(y\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ &= -\big[y\log \hat y + (1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\big] \end{aligned} $$

In words: for each example, only one term is active. If $y=1$, the loss is $-\log\hat y$. It is small when $\hat y$ is near 1 and grows without limit as $\hat y\to 0$. If $y=0$, the loss is $-\log(1-\hat y)$, with the roles swapped. This is also the cross-entropy $H(p, q)$ from Section 4, with true distribution $p = (y,\, 1-y)$ and model distribution $q = (\hat y,\, 1-\hat y)$.

Now, why does squared error behave badly on top of a sigmoid? Let $L_{sq}=(\hat y - y)^2$ with $\hat y=\sigma(z)$. By the chain rule, with $\sigma'(z)=\hat y(1-\hat y)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L_{sq}}{\partial z} &= 2(\hat y-y)\,\sigma'(z) \\ &= 2(\hat y - y)\,\hat y(1-\hat y) \end{aligned} $$

In words: the gradient contains the sigmoid’s slope $\hat y(1-\hat y)$. Suppose the true label is $y=1$, but the model is confident and wrong: $\hat y\approx 0$. Then $\hat y(1-\hat y)\approx 0$, so the whole gradient is almost zero. The model gets almost no correction exactly when it needs a large one. Training slows down, sometimes for a very long time.

There is a second, separate problem: squared error on a sigmoid is not convex. For $y = 1$ the loss is $f(z) = (1-\sigma(z))^2$. A convex function has a slope that never decreases as $z$ grows. Here $f'(0) = -2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14 = -\tfrac14$. But as $z \to -\infty$, the curve flattens out, so $f'(z) \to 0$. The slope therefore decreases from about 0 far to the left down to $-\tfrac14$ at $z=0$. That is impossible for a convex function. Figure 6.3 shows the flat shelf on the left.

Now derive the gradient of binary cross-entropy with respect to the logit $z$. First differentiate with respect to $\hat y$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} &= -\frac{y}{\hat y} + \frac{1-y}{1-\hat y} \\ &= \frac{-y(1-\hat y) + (1-y)\hat y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)} \\ &= \frac{-y+y\hat y+\hat y-y\hat y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)} \\ &= \frac{\hat y - y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)} \end{aligned} $$

Then apply the chain rule through $\hat y=\sigma(z)$, using $\partial\hat y/\partial z=\hat y(1-\hat y)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial z} \\ &= \frac{\hat y-y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)}\cdot \hat y(1-\hat y) = \hat y - y \end{aligned} $$

In words: the $\hat y(1-\hat y)$ factors cancel. What is left is the simplest possible gradient: predicted probability minus true label. It does not vanish when the model is confidently wrong. Its size is proportional to how wrong the prediction is.

Finally, $z=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$, so $\partial z/\partial w_j = x_j$ and $\partial z/\partial b = 1$. One more chain rule gives:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = (\hat y - y)\,\mathbf{x}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \hat y - y $$

In words: the gradient for each weight is (error) × (that feature’s value). This has the same shape as linear regression’s gradient, although the loss is different. That is why the training loop for logistic regression looks almost the same as the one for linear regression.

Is binary cross-entropy convex in $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$? Yes. As a function of $z$, its second derivative is $\partial(\hat y - y)/\partial z = \hat y(1-\hat y) > 0$, so it curves upward everywhere. A convex function of a linear function of the weights is convex in the weights, and a sum of convex functions is convex. So there are no false valleys. But convex does not guarantee that a minimum exists, as the next section shows.

Check yourself True label $y = 1$. Compare a prediction of $\hat y = 0.2$ with $\hat y = 0.01$. For each, what is $\partial L/\partial z$, and which way does gradient descent push the logit? Reveal

For $\hat y = 0.2$: $\partial L/\partial z = 0.2 - 1 = -0.8$. For $\hat y = 0.01$: $-0.99$. Gradient descent subtracts a positive multiple of the gradient, so in both cases the logit moves up and $\hat y$ moves toward 1. The more wrong prediction gets the larger push. With squared error, the pushes would be $2(0.2-1)(0.2)(0.8) = -0.256$ and $2(0.01-1)(0.01)(0.99) \approx -0.0196$: the more wrong prediction would get the smaller push.

5Training logistic regression in PyTorch

The same thing in PyTorch

train on four students: hours studied, passed or failed

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

X = torch.tensor([[2.0], [4.0], [6.0], [8.0]])   # hours studied
y = torch.tensor([0., 0., 1., 1.])                # failed / passed

w = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)
b = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)
lr = 0.1

# BCEWithLogitsLoss = sigmoid + binary cross-entropy in one stable function.
# It takes the raw logit z, not a probability.
loss_fn = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()

for step in range(1000):
    z = X.squeeze() * w + b        # the logit: no sigmoid before this loss
    loss = loss_fn(z, y)

    loss.backward()
    with torch.no_grad():
        w -= lr * w.grad
        b -= lr * b.grad
        w.grad.zero_()
        b.grad.zero_()

probs = torch.sigmoid(X.squeeze() * w + b)   # logits to probabilities, for reporting
print(probs, loss.item())
# tensor([0.0301, 0.2764, 0.8246, 0.9830], grad_fn=<SigmoidBackward0>) 0.14109164476394653
print(w.item(), b.item())
# 1.2552133798599243 -5.98319673538208

The model predicts “pass” for 6 and 8 hours and “fail” for 2 and 4 hours. Its boundary is at $-b/w \approx 4.77$ hours.

▲ Watch out

Give nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss the raw logit. Do not apply torch.sigmoid yourself and then use nn.BCELoss. The combined function computes $\log\sigma(z)$ in a numerically stable way (with the log-sum-exp trick), so large logits cause no problems. The two-step version fails when the sigmoid saturates. In float32, $\sigma(-200)$ is exactly 0. nn.BCELoss then clamps $\log 0$ to −100, so the loss stays finite, but the gradient is 0 and learning stops.

the same confident mistake, two ways

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

target = torch.tensor([1.])

z = torch.tensor([-200.], requires_grad=True)
loss = nn.BCELoss()(torch.sigmoid(z), target)
loss.backward()
print(loss.item(), z.grad)   # 100.0 tensor([-0.])

z = torch.tensor([-200.], requires_grad=True)
loss = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()(z, target)
loss.backward()
print(loss.item(), z.grad)   # 200.0 tensor([-1.])

The stable version reports the true loss (200) and the correct gradient $\hat y - y = -1$. The two-step version reports a clamped loss and no gradient at all.

◆ When the classes can be separated perfectly

Our four students can be split perfectly: everyone below 5 hours failed, everyone above passed. For such separable data, the loss has no minimum. Making $w$ and $b$ larger (while keeping $-b/w$ near 5) always makes every probability more confident and the loss smaller. So training never stops growing the weights:

w, b and loss as training continues

steps     w       b       loss
1000      1.26   -5.98   0.1411
10000     3.00  -14.81   0.0248
100000    5.27  -26.15   0.0026

The model becomes more and more certain, even though four examples cannot justify that certainty. The usual fixes are regularization (a penalty on large weights, Section 7) or stopping training early.

Check yourself Add a fifth student who studied 7 hours but failed. Does training still grow $w$ and $b$ forever? Reveal

No. Now no single threshold separates the classes: the student at 7 hours failed, but the one at 6 hours passed. If the weights grew without limit, the model would become certain that the 7-hour student passed. That student’s loss $-\log(1-\hat y)$ would grow without limit too. So at some finite $w$ and $b$ the total loss is smallest, and gradient descent settles there. Overlapping classes give the loss a real minimum.

6What to carry forward

  • Classification predicts a category, not a number. Logistic regression handles the two-category case.
  • The sigmoid $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ squashes any logit into a valid probability. $\sigma(0)=0.5$, and the slope $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ is always positive. The logit is the log-odds.
  • The decision boundary ($p=0.5$) is where the logit $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$ crosses zero. It is always flat: a line or hyperplane.
  • Binary cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli label. It replaces squared error, whose gradient vanishes on confidently wrong predictions. Its gradient is $(\hat y-y)\mathbf{x}$.
  • The loss is convex, but on separable data it has no minimum. Regularization or early stopping keeps the weights finite.

In Section 5 we solved a regression problem in two ways. Here we reused the same hypothesis–loss–optimization triad for classification, with a squashing function and a different loss.
