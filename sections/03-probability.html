Probability & Statistics

Models don't output answers, they output beliefs. Probability is the language we use to reason carefully about things we are not sure of — and, quietly, it's where every loss function is born.

30 min read
You'll learn
  • distributions
  • expectation
  • variance
  • Bayes' rule
  • maximum likelihood

Machine learning lives in a world of uncertainty. Will this email turn out to be spam? What digit is in this blurry photo? A good model never bangs its fist on the table with a single certain answer — it hands you a degree of belief: “90% spam,” “probably a 7, maybe a 1.” Probability is the mathematics of those graded beliefs, and statistics is how we tune them from data. This section builds that toolkit from the ground up.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

A model outputs a distribution — a spread of belief across the possible answers, not one answer. Everything in this section is either a way to describe such a distribution (expectation, variance), a common shape one takes (Bernoulli, Gaussian), or a way to update or fit one from evidence (Bayes, maximum likelihood). Hold that map in your head and the symbols fall into place.

1Random variables & distributions

Intuition first

A random variable is just a number whose value is uncertain until you look — the outcome of some process you can't predict exactly. Roll a die and call the result $X$: before the roll, $X$ could be any of 1–6. A distribution is the full accounting of that uncertainty: it tells you, for every possible value, how likely that value is. If a random variable is the question (“what will come up?”), the distribution is the complete answer key with the odds written next to each option.

Random variables come in two flavours, and the difference matters for every formula below:

  • Discrete — the values come in separate, countable lumps: 1, 2, 3… You can list them. A die, a coin, the number of emails in your inbox.
  • Continuous — the values slide smoothly along a range with no gaps: any real number in some interval. A temperature, a height, a waiting time.
☂ Everyday parallel

A die roll versus tomorrow's temperature. The die is discrete: six neat outcomes, each with a clean $1/6$ chance, and it makes sense to ask “what's the probability of exactly a 4?” The temperature is continuous: it could be 21.0°, 21.3°, 21.3007°… Asking “what's the chance it's exactly 21.30000…°?” is the wrong question — there are infinitely many values, so any single one has essentially zero chance. Instead you ask “what's the chance it lands between 21° and 22°?” That shift — from exact values to ranges — is the whole difference between the two worlds.

Now the math

For a discrete variable we use a probability mass function, written $P(X = x)$ — the amount of probability sitting on each specific value. Two honest rules hold: every mass is between 0 and 1, and all the masses add up to exactly 1 (something must happen).

$$ 0 \le P(X = x) \le 1, \qquad \sum_{x} P(X = x) = 1 $$

In words: each outcome gets a share of belief between “impossible” (0) and “certain” (1), and the shares of all outcomes together make one whole. For a fair die, $P(X = x) = 1/6$ for each of the six faces, and $6 \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 1$. The function is called “mass” because you can picture a lump of probability weight parked on each value.

For a continuous variable, single values have zero probability, so we use a probability density function $p(x)$ instead. Density is not probability itself — it's probability per unit of x, like grams per centimetre. To get an actual probability you must sum the density over a range, which for a smooth curve means taking an integral (the area under the curve):

$$ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} p(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = 1 $$

In words: the chance that $X$ falls between a and b is the area under the density curve over that stretch. The $\int$ sign is just a continuous cousin of the $\sum$ sign — “add up over a smooth range” instead of “add up over a list.” And the total area under the whole curve is 1, the continuous version of “the masses sum to 1.”

Definition

A distribution is the complete description of how likely each value of a random variable is. For discrete variables it's given by a PMF (mass on each value); for continuous variables by a PDF (a density curve whose area gives probability). Both always sum/integrate to 1.

The same thing in PyTorch

a discrete distribution (a loaded die) and sampling from it

import torch

# a PMF over 6 faces — six numbers that must sum to 1
probs = torch.tensor([0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.50])  # face 6 is loaded
print(probs.sum())                 # tensor(1.0000)  -> a valid distribution

die = torch.distributions.Categorical(probs=probs)

# draw 10,000 rolls and see the loaded face show up ~half the time
samples = die.sample((10_000,))
print((samples == 5).float().mean())   # ~0.50   (face index 5 == the '6')
Check yourself Someone hands you a continuous PDF and asks “what is P(X = 3.0) exactly?” Why is the honest answer 0, and what should they have asked instead? Reveal

For a continuous variable there are infinitely many possible values packed into any interval, so the probability piled on any single exact point is zero — the area under a curve over a zero-width slice is zero. The density p(3.0) can be large, but density is not probability. The right question is about a range, e.g. P(2.9 ≤ X ≤ 3.1), which is the area under the curve between those bounds. This is the single most common confusion when people first meet PDFs.

2Expectation and variance

Intuition first

Once you have a distribution, the first thing you want is a one-number summary: “on average, what value should I expect?” That's the expectation (also called the mean or expected value), written $\mathbb{E}[X]$. It is the long-run average you'd converge to if you repeated the random process forever. Crucially, it's a weighted average: outcomes that are more likely pull the average toward them harder.

☂ Everyday parallel

A slot machine pays 0 dollars most spins, but 100 dollars one time in fifty. What do you win per spin on average? Not 100 dollars (that's rare), not 0 dollars (you do sometimes win). It's each payout weighted by how often it happens: $\tfrac{49}{50}\times\$0 + \tfrac{1}{50}\times\$100 = \$2$ per spin. That 2 dollars is the expectation. The casino sets the machine so your expected win is less than what you paid to play — the “house edge” is just an expectation calculation running in their favour, spin after spin.

Now the math

For a discrete variable, expectation is the probability-weighted sum of the values:

$$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} x \, P(X = x) $$

In words: go through every possible value $x$, multiply it by how likely it is, and add all those products up. Likely values contribute more; rare ones barely nudge the total. The result is the balance point of the distribution — the spot where it would sit level on a seesaw.

Let's derive one concretely. A fair die: values 1–6, each with probability $1/6$.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= 1\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 4\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 5\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 6\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} \\[4pt] &= \tfrac{1}{6}\,(1+2+3+4+5+6) \\[4pt] &= \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot 21 \;=\; 3.5 \end{aligned} $$

In words: the expected roll is 3.5. Note you can never actually roll a 3.5 — the expectation is not a possible outcome, it's the long-run average. Roll ten thousand times and the running average will settle very close to 3.5.

The mean tells you where a distribution sits. The next question is how spread out it is — do outcomes cluster tightly around the mean, or scatter wildly? That's the variance, written $\mathrm{Var}[X]$. We measure each outcome's distance from the mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$, square it (so that being too high and too low both count as spread, and never cancel out), and take the expected value of that:

$$ \mathrm{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] $$

In words: variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Big variance means outcomes routinely land far from the average (a wild, unpredictable variable); small variance means they hug it (a steady, reliable one). We square the distances so that a swing of $+3$ and a swing of $-3$ both count as spread rather than erasing each other. The square root of variance, $\sigma$, is the standard deviation — the same idea back in the original units.

☂ Everyday parallel

Two basketball players both average 20 points a game — same expectation. But one scores 19, 21, 20, 20, 20 (a reliable player, tiny variance), while the other scores 2, 40, 5, 38, 15 (a streaky player, huge variance). The mean can't tell them apart; variance is exactly the number that does. A coach picking who to trust in the final seconds cares about variance as much as the average.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch

# simulate the fair die and check both summaries empirically
rolls = torch.randint(1, 7, (100_000,)).float()   # 100k rolls of 1..6

print(rolls.mean())                 # ~3.50   matches E[X] we derived by hand
print(rolls.var(unbiased=False))    # ~2.92   the spread around 3.5

# reliable vs streaky: same mean, very different variance
reliable = torch.tensor([19., 21., 20., 20., 20.])
streaky  = torch.tensor([ 2., 40.,  5., 38., 15.])
print(reliable.mean(), streaky.mean())   # both 20.0
print(reliable.var(),  streaky.var())    # ~0.5   vs   ~326.5
Check yourself A bet pays +$10 with probability 0.2 and −$3 with probability 0.8. What is the expected payout, and should you take the bet? Reveal

E[payout] = (0.2)(+10) + (0.8)(−3) = 2.0 − 2.4 = −$0.40. On average you lose 40 cents every time you play, even though the win is bigger than the loss — because the loss happens four times as often. A rational one-number decision says skip it. (In real life you'd also weigh variance and how much you can afford to lose, but the expectation is the first thing to check.)

3Two distributions you'll meet everywhere

Most of ML leans on a small handful of standard distribution shapes. Two of them show up so often they're worth knowing by heart.

The Bernoulli: one yes-or-no event

Intuition first

The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest one there is: a single event with exactly two outcomes, 1 (“success”) or 0 (“failure”), governed by one number $p$ — the probability of the 1. A coin flip, a click-or-no-click, a spam-or-not-spam decision. Whenever a model answers a single yes/no question, a Bernoulli is hiding underneath.

☂ Everyday parallel

A single coin flip with a possibly-bent coin. If the coin comes up heads 70% of the time, then $p = 0.7$: heads (1) has probability 0.7, tails (0) has probability 0.3. That one number $p$ is the entire distribution — nothing else to know.

Now the math

Its PMF can be written as a single tidy line that works for both outcomes:

$$ P(X = x) = p^{\,x}\,(1-p)^{\,1-x}, \qquad x \in \{0, 1\} $$

In words: plug in $x = 1$ and the formula collapses to $p$ (the chance of a 1); plug in $x = 0$ and it collapses to $1 - p$ (the chance of a 0). The exponents are a clever switch: whichever outcome you ask about, the other term's exponent becomes 0 and vanishes (anything to the power 0 is 1). Its mean is $\mathbb{E}[X] = p$ and its variance is $p(1-p)$ — biggest at $p = 0.5$, the most unpredictable coin.

The Gaussian: the bell curve

Intuition first

The Gaussian (also called the Normal distribution) is the famous bell curve: a smooth hump, tall in the middle and tailing off symmetrically on both sides. It's the default shape for a continuous quantity that clusters around a typical value with random wobble either side — heights, measurement errors, exam scores, sensor noise.

☂ Everyday parallel

The heights of adults in a city. Most people are near the average, say 170 cm. Fewer are 185 cm, fewer still 200 cm; the same thinning-out happens toward the short end. Plot how many people sit at each height and you get a bell: crowded in the middle, sparse in the tails, roughly symmetric. That's a Gaussian in the wild.

Now the math

The Gaussian PDF is controlled by just two numbers — its mean $\mu$ and its standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) $$

In words: $\mu$ (mu) is the centre — where the peak sits, the typical value. $\sigma$ (sigma) is the width — how fat the bell is, i.e. how much things spread around the centre. The heart of the formula is $-(x-\mu)^2$: the further $x$ is from the centre, the more negative that gets, and $\exp$ of a big negative number is tiny — so probability density falls off fast as you move away from $\mu$. The $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ out front is just a scaling factor that forces the total area to equal 1.

◆ Why the Gaussian is everywhere

The deep reason is the Central Limit Theorem: when you add up (or average) lots of small, independent random effects, the total tends toward a Gaussian — almost regardless of the shape of the individual effects. A person's height is the sum of countless small genetic and environmental nudges; measurement error is the sum of many tiny disturbances. Because so much of the world is “many little random pushes added together,” the bell curve appears again and again — and that's why ML so often assumes noise is Gaussian, and models it that way.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch

# a Gaussian centred at mu=170 with spread sigma=10 (think: heights in cm)
normal = torch.distributions.Normal(loc=170.0, scale=10.0)

samples = normal.sample((100_000,))
print(samples.mean(), samples.std())   # ~170.0, ~10.0  -> recovers mu, sigma

# about 68% of a Gaussian lies within one sigma of the mean:
within_1sigma = ((samples > 160) & (samples < 180)).float().mean()
print(within_1sigma)                   # ~0.68  the famous 68% rule
Check yourself A Bernoulli has p = 0.5 and another has p = 0.9. Which one is more uncertain — harder to predict on any single trial — and how does the variance p(1−p) confirm it? Reveal

The p = 0.5 coin is more uncertain: it's a genuine coin flip, no way to guess. The p = 0.9 one is easy — just guess “1” and you're right 90% of the time. The variance confirms it: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 versus 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09. Variance p(1−p) peaks exactly at p = 0.5 and shrinks toward the extremes, which matches the intuition that a near-certain event carries little surprise.

4Conditional probability & Bayes' rule

Intuition first

Conditional probability, written $P(A \mid B)$ and read “the probability of A given B,” is how likely A is once you already know B has happened. New information shrinks the world of possibilities, and the odds shift accordingly. Learning that it's cloudy raises the chance of rain; the raw chance of rain and the chance-of-rain-given-clouds are two different numbers.

☂ Everyday parallel

The chance a stranger is over six feet tall is modest. But given that you're told they play professional basketball, that chance rockets up. The fact didn't change the person — it changed your information, and so it changed the probability you should assign. That's all a conditional probability is: an updated belief in the light of a known fact.

Now the math

Start from the joint probability — the chance that both A and B happen, written $P(A, B)$. There are two equally valid ways to build that joint up out of a condition, depending on which event you condition on:

$$ P(A, B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) $$

In words: the chance of both A and B is the chance of B, times the chance of A once B is known — “B happens, and then A happens given B.” You could equally tell the story the other way round (A first, then B given A). Both routes reach the same joint, so the two products are equal.

Now set those two expressions for the same joint equal to each other and divide by $P(B)$. That single step of algebra is Bayes' rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A \mid B)\,P(B) &= P(B \mid A)\,P(A) \\[4pt] P(A \mid B) &= \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)} \end{aligned} $$

In words: Bayes' rule lets you flip a conditional. If you know how likely the evidence B is when A is true, plus how common A is to begin with, you can work out how likely A is once you've seen B. It's the formula for updating a belief in the light of evidence.

The three words of Bayes

The prior $P(A)$ is your belief in A before the evidence. The likelihood $P(B \mid A)$ is how well A explains the evidence B — how probable the evidence would be if A were true. The posterior $P(A \mid B)$ is your updated belief in A after seeing the evidence. Bayes is the machine that turns prior + likelihood into posterior.

☂ Everyday parallel

A medical test for a rare disease. You take it; it comes back positive. Your gut screams “I'm sick.” But Bayes says: hold on — factor in how rare the disease is to begin with (the prior), and how often the test cries wolf on healthy people (false positives). The frightening number and the true number can be very far apart. Let's put real figures on it.

A concrete number: the base-rate effect

Suppose a disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test is quite good: if you have the disease it comes back positive 99% of the time (the likelihood), and if you're healthy it wrongly comes back positive just 5% of the time (the false-positive rate). You test positive. What's the real chance you're sick?

Let $D$ = “has disease,” $+$ = “tests positive.” We want the posterior $P(D \mid +)$. Bayes needs the total chance of a positive result, $P(+)$, which is positives from sick people plus positives from healthy people:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(+) &= \underbrace{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}_{\text{true positives}} + \underbrace{P(+\mid \lnot D)\,P(\lnot D)}_{\text{false positives}} \\[4pt] &= (0.99)(0.001) + (0.05)(0.999) \\[4pt] &= 0.00099 + 0.04995 \;=\; 0.05094 \\[10pt] P(D \mid +) &= \frac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{(0.99)(0.001)}{0.05094} \;\approx\; 0.0194 \end{aligned} $$

In words: even after a positive result on a 99%-accurate test, your chance of actually being sick is only about 2%. Why so low? Because the disease is rare (the prior $P(D) = 0.001$ is tiny). Out of a positive pile, the vast majority come from the huge healthy population's 5% false-positive rate, which swamps the few true positives. This is the base-rate effect: ignore how rare something is to begin with, and you'll wildly overreact to evidence. Bayes forces you to keep the prior in the picture.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch

p_disease   = torch.tensor(0.001)   # prior: 1 in 1000
p_pos_sick  = torch.tensor(0.99)    # likelihood: true-positive rate
p_pos_well  = torch.tensor(0.05)    # false-positive rate

# total probability of a positive test (sick + healthy sources)
p_pos = p_pos_sick * p_disease + p_pos_well * (1 - p_disease)

posterior = (p_pos_sick * p_disease) / p_pos
print(posterior)     # tensor(0.0194)  -> ~2%, not 99%
Check yourself If the disease were far more common — say 1 in 10 people (prior 0.1) instead of 1 in 1000 — would a positive test make you more or less confident you're sick? Roughly what happens to the posterior? Reveal

Much more confident. Redo the sum with P(D) = 0.1: P(+) = (0.99)(0.1) + (0.05)(0.9) = 0.099 + 0.045 = 0.144, so P(D | +) = 0.099 / 0.144 ≈ 0.69 — about 69%, versus 2% before. The test never changed; only the prior did. When the base rate is high, the true positives no longer get drowned out by false positives. This is exactly why context matters: the same evidence means very different things depending on how common the thing was to begin with.

5Maximum likelihood: fitting a distribution to data

Intuition first

So far the distributions came with their numbers already filled in. But in real ML you have data and you need to find the numbers yourself. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is the workhorse method for that, and its idea is beautifully simple: pick the parameters that make the data you actually observed as probable as possible. Of all the coins that could have produced your flips, MLE picks the one that would produce them most readily.

☂ Everyday parallel

You hear a muffled band playing through a wall and try to guess the song. You run through candidates and pick the one that would most naturally produce the notes you're hearing — the song under which “these notes” are least surprising. MLE does the same with numbers: it scans the possible parameter values and keeps the one under which your observed data is least surprising, i.e. most probable.

Now the math

Let's derive it fully for a coin. You flip a coin with unknown heads-probability $p$ and observe $H$ heads out of $N$ flips. Each flip is a Bernoulli, and because the flips are independent, the probability of the whole sequence is the product of the individual probabilities. That product, viewed as a function of the unknown $p$, is the likelihood:

$$ L(p) = p^{\,H}\,(1-p)^{\,N-H} $$

In words: to get exactly this data you need $H$ heads (each contributing a factor of $p$) and $N - H$ tails (each contributing $1 - p$). Multiply them all together and you get the chance of the observed sequence for a given $p$. We want the $p$ that makes this as large as possible.

Multiplying many probabilities gives a tiny, awkward number, and products are painful to differentiate. So we take the logarithm first. Two reasons the log is a free win: it turns products into sums (much easier calculus), and because $\log$ is a steadily increasing function, the $p$ that maximizes $\log L$ is the exact same $p$ that maximizes $L$ — we lose nothing by working with the log.

$$ \ell(p) = \log L(p) = H \log p + (N - H)\log(1 - p) $$

In words: the $\log$ pulled the exponents down into multipliers and turned the product into a sum. This $\ell(p)$ is the log-likelihood, and it's what we'll actually maximize.

To find the maximum, use calculus from Section 2: take the derivative with respect to $p$, set it to zero (the slope is flat at a peak), and solve. Recall $\frac{d}{dp}\log p = \frac{1}{p}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\ell}{dp} &= \frac{H}{p} - \frac{N - H}{1 - p} = 0 \\[6pt] \frac{H}{p} &= \frac{N - H}{1 - p} \\[6pt] H\,(1 - p) &= (N - H)\,p \\[6pt] H - Hp &= Np - Hp \\[6pt] H &= Np \\[6pt] \hat{p} &= \frac{H}{N} \end{aligned} $$

In words: the best estimate is $\hat{p} = H/N$ — the fraction of flips that came up heads. Flip 100 times, see 70 heads, and MLE says the most likely coin is one with $p = 0.7$. The little hat on $\hat{p}$ marks it as an estimate from data, not the true hidden value. What feels like plain common sense — “just count the heads” — turns out to be exactly what the maximum-likelihood principle prescribes. That's reassuring: the fancy method agrees with your intuition on the easy case, which is why you can trust it on the hard ones.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch

# 100 flips, 70 heads: recover p by maximizing the log-likelihood with gradient ascent
H, N = 70.0, 100.0
p = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)   # start with a fair-coin guess

for _ in range(500):
    log_lik = H * torch.log(p) + (N - H) * torch.log(1 - p)
    log_lik.backward()                       # d(log_lik)/dp, from Section 2
    with torch.no_grad():
        p += 0.001 * p.grad                  # step UP-hill (maximize)
        p.grad.zero_()

print(p)          # tensor(0.70...)  -> matches the closed-form p_hat = H/N = 0.7
◆ Where loss functions come from

Notice what we maximized: a log-likelihood. Now flip the sign. Maximizing $\log L$ is the same as minimizing $-\log L$, and that quantity, negative log-likelihood, is precisely the loss function most classifiers are trained on — you'll meet it as cross-entropy in Section 4. The Gaussian version, where you do this same derivation for a Normal instead of a Bernoulli, collapses into the mean-squared-error loss behind Section 5's linear regression. Training a model is maximum likelihood in disguise: “find the parameters that make the data most probable” is the same sentence as “find the parameters that minimize the loss.”

Check yourself You flip a coin 10 times and get 10 heads. MLE says p̂ = 10/10 = 1.0 — a coin that never lands tails. Why is that suspicious, and what does it hint about the limits of pure MLE? Reveal

MLE only listens to the data in front of it, and with a small sample that data can be misleading. Ten heads in a row is perfectly plausible from a fair coin (about a 0.1% chance), yet MLE confidently declares p = 1.0, meaning it now assigns probability zero to ever seeing a tail — a reckless certainty from ten flips. The fix is to fold in a prior belief (“coins are usually roughly fair”) and use Bayes, which pulls the estimate back toward 0.5 until enough data overwhelms it. This tug-of-war between what the data says and what you believed beforehand is the bridge from maximum likelihood to full Bayesian learning.

6What to carry forward

  • A model outputs a distribution — belief spread over outcomes. Discrete ones use a PMF (mass on each value); continuous ones use a PDF (area under a curve gives probability).
  • Expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the probability-weighted average — where the distribution balances. Variance $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]$ is its spread — reliable versus streaky.
  • The Bernoulli models one yes/no event via a single $p$; the Gaussian is the bell curve set by mean $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$, and it appears everywhere because sums of many small random effects tend toward it.
  • Bayes' rule $P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}$ updates a prior belief with likelihood into a posterior. Never ignore the base rate.
  • MLE picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable; for a coin it gives the plain count $\hat{p} = H/N$. Minimizing negative log-likelihood is where loss functions come from.

Next we turn probability into a way to measure information and surprise — which leads straight to entropy, cross-entropy, and the loss functions we just previewed.
