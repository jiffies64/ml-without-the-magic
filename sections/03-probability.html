Probability & Statistics

Models do not output answers; they output beliefs. Probability is the language for reasoning carefully about things we are not sure of. It is also where the common loss functions come from.

48 min read
You'll learn
  • distributions
  • expectation
  • variance
  • Bayes' rule
  • maximum likelihood

Machine learning lives in a world of uncertainty. Is this email spam? Which digit is in this blurry photo? A good model does not give a single answer as if it were certain. It gives a degree of belief: “90% spam,” “probably a 7, maybe a 1.” Probability is the mathematics of those graded beliefs. Statistics is how we adjust them to fit data. This section builds that toolkit from the start.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

A model outputs a distribution: belief spread across the possible answers, not one answer. Everything in this section does one of three things. It describes a distribution (expectation, variance). It names a common shape (Bernoulli, Gaussian). Or it updates or fits a distribution from evidence (Bayes, maximum likelihood). Keep that map in mind and the symbols will fall into place.

1Random variables and distributions

Intuition first

A random variable is a number whose value is uncertain until you look. It is the outcome of some process you cannot predict exactly. Roll a die and call the result $X$. Before the roll, $X$ could be any of 1 to 6. A distribution is the full description of that uncertainty. It tells you, for every possible value, how likely that value is. If the random variable is the question (“what will come up?”), the distribution is the answer sheet, with the chance written next to each option.

Random variables come in two kinds, and the difference matters for every formula below:

  • Discrete: the values are separate and countable, like 1, 2, 3. You can list them. Examples: a die, a coin, the number of emails in your inbox.
  • Continuous: the values can be any real number in some range, with no gaps. Examples: a temperature, a height, a waiting time.
☂ Everyday parallel

Compare a die roll with tomorrow's temperature. The die is discrete: six outcomes, each with chance $1/6$. It makes sense to ask “what is the chance of a 4?” The temperature is continuous: it could be 21.0°, 21.3°, 21.3007°, and so on. Asking “what is the chance it is exactly 21.30000…°?” is the wrong question. There are infinitely many values, so any single one has zero chance. Instead you ask “what is the chance it lands between 21° and 22°?” This shift from single values to ranges is the main difference between the two kinds.

Now the math

For a discrete variable we use a probability mass function (PMF), written $P(X = x)$. It is the amount of probability that sits on each value. Two rules always hold. Every mass is between 0 and 1. And all the masses add up to 1 (something must happen).

$$ 0 \le P(X = x) \le 1, \qquad \sum_{x} P(X = x) = 1 $$

In words: each outcome gets a share of belief between “impossible” (0) and “certain” (1). The shares of all outcomes together make one whole. For a fair die, $P(X = x) = 1/6$ for each of the six faces, and $6 \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 1$. It is called “mass” because you can picture a lump of probability placed on each value.

For a continuous variable, single values have zero probability. So we use a probability density function (PDF) $p(x)$ instead. Density is not probability. It is probability per unit of $x$, like grams per centimeter. To get a probability, you add up the density over a range. For a smooth curve, that means taking an integral (the area under the curve):

$$ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} p(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = 1 $$

In words: the chance that $X$ falls between $a$ and $b$ is the area under the density curve over that range. The $\int$ sign is the continuous version of the $\sum$ sign: “add up over a smooth range” instead of “add up over a list.” The total area under the whole curve is 1, the continuous version of “the masses add up to 1.”

Definition

A distribution is the complete description of how likely each value of a random variable is. For discrete variables it is a PMF (a mass on each value). For continuous variables it is a PDF (a density curve whose area gives probability). The masses add up to 1, and the total area is 1.

The same thing in PyTorch

a discrete distribution (a loaded die) and sampling from it

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# a PMF over 6 faces: six numbers that must add up to 1
probs = torch.tensor([0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.50])  # face 6 is loaded
print(probs.sum())                 # tensor(1.)  -> a valid distribution

die = torch.distributions.Categorical(probs=probs)

# draw 10,000 rolls and count how often the loaded face appears
samples = die.sample((10_000,))
print((samples == 5).float().mean())   # tensor(0.5046)   (index 5 is the face '6')

In words: Categorical is PyTorch's name for a distribution over a list of outcomes. It numbers the faces from 0, so the face “6” is index 5. The share of 6s in 10,000 rolls is close to 0.5, as the PMF says. A different seed gives a slightly different number, near 0.50.

Check yourself A PDF has the value $p(3.0) = 2$ at one point. Is that a mistake, since probabilities cannot be above 1? What is $P(X = 3.0)$, and what should you ask instead? Reveal

It is not a mistake. A density is probability per unit, so it can be larger than 1 on a narrow range, as long as the total area is 1. $P(X = 3.0)$ is 0: the area over a slice of zero width is zero. The right question is about a range, for example $P(2.9 \le X \le 3.1)$. If the density stays near 2 over that range, the answer is about $2 \times 0.2 = 0.4$.

2Expectation and variance

Intuition first

Once you have a distribution, you first want a one-number summary: “on average, what value should I expect?” That is the expectation (also called the mean or expected value), written $\mathbb{E}[X]$. It is the long-run average you would reach if you repeated the random process many, many times. It is a weighted average: outcomes that are more likely pull the average toward them more strongly.

☂ Everyday parallel

A slot machine pays 0 dollars on most spins, but 100 dollars one time in fifty. What do you win per spin, on average? Not 100 dollars (that is rare), and not 0 dollars (you do win sometimes). You weight each payout by how often it happens: $\tfrac{49}{50}\times 0 + \tfrac{1}{50}\times 100 = 2$ dollars per spin. Those 2 dollars are the expectation. The casino sets the machine so that your expected win is less than the price of a spin. The casino's profit is an expectation calculation in its favor.

Now the math

For a discrete variable, the expectation is the probability-weighted sum of the values:

$$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} x \, P(X = x) $$

In words: go through every possible value $x$, multiply it by its probability, and add all the products. Likely values contribute more; rare ones barely change the total. The result is the balance point of the distribution: the place where it would balance on a seesaw.

Let's compute one. A fair die has values 1 to 6, each with probability $1/6$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= 1\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 4\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 5\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} + 6\cdot\tfrac{1}{6} \\[4pt] &= \tfrac{1}{6}\,(1+2+3+4+5+6) \\[4pt] &= \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot 21 \;=\; 3.5 \end{aligned} $$

In words: the expected roll is 3.5. You can never roll a 3.5. The expectation does not have to be a possible outcome; it is the long-run average. Roll ten thousand times, and the running average will be very close to 3.5.

The mean tells you where a distribution sits. The next question is how spread out it is. Do outcomes stay close to the mean, or scatter widely? That is the variance, written $\mathrm{Var}[X]$. We measure each outcome's distance from the mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and square it. Then we take the expected value of that:

$$ \mathrm{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] $$

In words: variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Large variance means outcomes often land far from the average (an unpredictable variable). Small variance means they stay close to it (a steady one). We square the distances so that a swing of $+3$ and a swing of $-3$ both count as spread, instead of canceling. The square root of the variance, $\sigma$ (sigma), is the standard deviation. It is the same idea, back in the original units.

A second form of the variance is often easier to compute. Expand the square and use the fact that an expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}[X] &= \mathbb{E}\big[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2\big] \\[4pt] &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 \\[4pt] &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2 \end{aligned} $$

In words: the mean of the squares, minus the square of the mean. (The last step uses $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$.) For the fair die, $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \tfrac{1}{6}(1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) = \tfrac{91}{6}$. So $\mathrm{Var}[X] = \tfrac{91}{6} - 3.5^2 = \tfrac{35}{12} \approx 2.92$.

☂ Everyday parallel

Two basketball players both average 20 points a game: same expectation. One scores 19, 21, 20, 20, 20 (a reliable player, tiny variance). The other scores 2, 40, 5, 38, 15 (a streaky player, huge variance). The mean cannot tell them apart; the variance can. A coach who chooses a player for the last seconds of a close game cares about variance as much as about the average.

The same thing in PyTorch

mean and variance, from samples and from lists

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# simulate the fair die and check both summaries
rolls = torch.randint(1, 7, (100_000,)).float()   # 100k rolls of 1..6

print(rolls.mean())                 # tensor(3.4991)  close to E[X] = 3.5
print(rolls.var(correction=0))      # tensor(2.9190)  close to 35/12 = 2.917

# reliable vs streaky: same mean, very different variance
reliable = torch.tensor([19., 21., 20., 20., 20.])
streaky  = torch.tensor([ 2., 40.,  5., 38., 15.])
print(reliable.mean(), streaky.mean())                    # tensor(20.) tensor(20.)
print(reliable.var(correction=0), streaky.var(correction=0))  # tensor(0.4000) tensor(259.6000)

In words: correction=0 divides by $N$, the number of values. That is the average squared distance, as in our formula. PyTorch's default is correction=1, which divides by $N-1$. This “sample variance” is slightly larger (0.5 and 324.5 here). It corrects for the fact that the mean was estimated from the same few values. With 100,000 rolls, the two versions are almost equal.

Check yourself A bet pays $+10$ dollars with probability 0.2 and $-3$ dollars with probability 0.8. What is the expected payout? Now add 5 dollars to every payout. How do the mean and the variance change? Reveal

$\mathbb{E} = (0.2)(10) + (0.8)(-3) = 2.0 - 2.4 = -0.40$. On average you lose 40 cents per play. The win is larger than the loss, but the loss happens four times as often. If you add 5 dollars to every payout, the mean rises by 5, to 4.60. The variance does not change: every outcome and the mean move together, so all distances from the mean stay the same.

3Two distributions you will meet everywhere

Most of ML relies on a small number of standard distribution shapes. Two of them appear so often that they are worth knowing well.

The Bernoulli: one yes-or-no event

Intuition first

The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest one there is. It describes a single event with two outcomes, 1 (“success”) or 0 (“failure”). One number $p$ controls it: the probability of a 1. Examples: a coin flip, a click or no click, spam or not spam. Whenever a model answers a single yes/no question, a Bernoulli is behind it.

☂ Everyday parallel

Flip a bent coin that comes up heads 70% of the time. Then $p = 0.7$: heads (1) has probability 0.7, and tails (0) has probability 0.3. That one number $p$ is the entire distribution. There is nothing else to know.

Now the math

Its PMF can be written as one line that works for both outcomes:

$$ P(X = x) = p^{\,x}\,(1-p)^{\,1-x}, \qquad x \in \{0, 1\} $$

In words: put in $x = 1$ and the formula becomes $p$ (the chance of a 1). Put in $x = 0$ and it becomes $1 - p$ (the chance of a 0). The exponents work as a switch: for whichever outcome you ask about, the other factor gets exponent 0 and becomes 1.

Its mean and variance follow from Section 2. Because $X$ is only ever 0 or 1, $X^2 = X$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= 0\cdot(1-p) + 1\cdot p = p \\[4pt] \mathbb{E}[X^2] &= 0^2\cdot(1-p) + 1^2\cdot p = p \\[4pt] \mathrm{Var}[X] &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2 = p - p^2 = p(1-p) \end{aligned} $$

In words: the mean of a 0/1 variable is the chance of a 1. The variance $p(1-p)$ is 0 when $p$ is 0 or 1 (no randomness at all). It is largest at $p = 0.5$, where it equals 0.25.

The same thing in PyTorch

a Bernoulli coin: sampled and exact

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

coin = torch.distributions.Bernoulli(probs=0.7)
flips = coin.sample((100_000,))     # 100k flips, each 0.0 or 1.0

print(flips.mean())                 # tensor(0.7007)  close to p = 0.7
print(flips.var(correction=0))      # tensor(0.2097)  close to p(1-p) = 0.21
print(coin.mean, coin.variance)     # tensor(0.7000) tensor(0.2100)  the exact formulas

In words: the sample mean and variance of many flips come close to the formulas we derived. The distribution object also knows the exact values, $p$ and $p(1-p)$.

The Gaussian: the bell curve

Intuition first

The Gaussian (also called the normal distribution) is the well-known bell curve. It is a smooth hump, tall in the middle, and it falls off equally on both sides. It is the default shape for a continuous quantity that stays near a typical value with random variation on either side. Examples: heights, measurement errors, exam scores, sensor noise.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of the heights of adults in a city. Most people are near the average, say 170 cm. Fewer are 185 cm, and even fewer 200 cm. The same thinning happens toward the short end. Plot how many people have each height and you get a bell: crowded in the middle, sparse at the edges, roughly symmetric. That is a Gaussian in real life.

Now the math

Two numbers control the Gaussian PDF: its mean $\mu$ and its standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) $$

In words: $\mu$ (mu) is the center: where the peak is, the typical value. $\sigma$ (sigma) is the width: how far values spread around the center. The main part of the formula is $-(x-\mu)^2$. The farther $x$ is from the center, the more negative this gets, and $\exp$ of a large negative number is tiny. So the density falls quickly as you move away from $\mu$. The factor $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ in front makes the total area equal 1. (We will not prove that area here; it needs a calculus trick with a double integral.)

◆ Why the Gaussian is everywhere

The main reason is the central limit theorem. When you add up (or average) many small, independent random effects, the total tends toward a Gaussian. This happens almost regardless of the shape of each single effect. We will not prove it here. A person's height is the sum of many small genetic and environmental effects. A measurement error is the sum of many tiny disturbances. Many quantities in the world are “many small random pushes added together,” so the bell curve appears again and again. That is why ML often assumes that noise is Gaussian.

The same thing in PyTorch

sampling heights from a Gaussian

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# a Gaussian centered at mu = 170 with spread sigma = 10 (heights in cm)
normal = torch.distributions.Normal(loc=170.0, scale=10.0)

samples = normal.sample((100_000,))
print(samples.mean(), samples.std())   # tensor(169.9756) tensor(10.0124)  close to mu, sigma

# about 68% of a Gaussian lies within one sigma of the mean:
within_1sigma = ((samples > 160) & (samples < 180)).float().mean()
print(within_1sigma)                   # tensor(0.6811)  the 68% rule

In words: the samples recover the two numbers we chose. The share of samples between 160 cm and 180 cm is the shaded area in Figure 3.1. For any Gaussian, the area within one $\sigma$ of the mean is about 0.683.

Check yourself One Bernoulli has $p = 0.5$ and another has $p = 0.9$. If you always guess the more likely outcome, how often are you wrong for each? How does the variance $p(1-p)$ match that? Reveal

For $p = 0.5$ there is no better guess, so you are wrong half the time. For $p = 0.9$ you guess “1” and are wrong only 10% of the time. The variance agrees: $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ versus $0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.09$. The variance $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$ and shrinks toward the ends. So the harder a single outcome is to predict, the larger its variance. (Section 4 measures this unpredictability in a different way, with entropy.)

4Conditional probability and Bayes' rule

Intuition first

Conditional probability, written $P(A \mid B)$ and read “the probability of A given B,” is how likely A is when you already know that B happened. New information makes the set of possibilities smaller, and the chances change. If you learn that the sky is cloudy, the chance of rain goes up. The plain chance of rain and the chance of rain given clouds are two different numbers.

☂ Everyday parallel

The chance that a stranger is over 190 cm tall is small. But if you learn that they play professional basketball, that chance rises sharply. The fact did not change the person. It changed your information, and so it changed the probability you should give. That is all a conditional probability is: a belief updated with a known fact.

Now the math

Start from the joint probability: the chance that both A and B happen, written $P(A, B)$. There are two equally valid ways to build it from a condition, depending on which event you condition on:

$$ P(A, B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) $$

In words: the chance of both A and B is the chance of B, times the chance of A once B is known: “B happens, and then A happens given B.” You can also tell the story the other way round (A first, then B given A). Both ways reach the same joint probability, so the two products are equal.

Now set those two expressions equal and divide by $P(B)$. That one step of algebra is Bayes' rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A \mid B)\,P(B) &= P(B \mid A)\,P(A) \\[4pt] P(A \mid B) &= \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)} \end{aligned} $$

In words: Bayes' rule lets you reverse a conditional. Suppose you know how likely the evidence B is when A is true, and how common A is to begin with. Then you can compute how likely A is once you have seen B. It is the formula for updating a belief with evidence.

The three words of Bayes

The prior $P(A)$ is your belief in A before the evidence. The likelihood $P(B \mid A)$ is how probable the evidence B would be if A were true. The posterior $P(A \mid B)$ is your updated belief in A after seeing the evidence. Bayes' rule turns prior and likelihood into posterior.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a medical test for a rare disease. You take it, and it comes back positive. Your first thought is “I am sick.” But Bayes says: first consider how rare the disease is (the prior). Then consider how often the test gives a false alarm for healthy people (false positives). The number that feels right and the true number can be very far apart. Let's put real numbers on it.

A concrete number: the base-rate effect

Suppose a disease affects 1 in 1000 people. The test is quite good. If you have the disease, it is positive 99% of the time (the likelihood). If you are healthy, it is wrongly positive only 5% of the time (the false-positive rate). You test positive. What is the real chance that you are sick?

Let $D$ = “has the disease” and $+$ = “tests positive.” We want the posterior $P(D \mid +)$. Bayes' rule needs the total chance of a positive result, $P(+)$. That is the positives from sick people plus the positives from healthy people:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(+) &= \underbrace{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}_{\text{true positives}} + \underbrace{P(+\mid \lnot D)\,P(\lnot D)}_{\text{false positives}} \\[4pt] &= (0.99)(0.001) + (0.05)(0.999) \\[4pt] &= 0.00099 + 0.04995 \;=\; 0.05094 \\[10pt] P(D \mid +) &= \frac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{(0.99)(0.001)}{0.05094} \;\approx\; 0.0194 \end{aligned} $$

In words: the test catches 99% of real cases. Even so, after a positive result your chance of being sick is only about 2%. The reason is that the disease is rare: the prior $P(D) = 0.001$ is tiny. Most positive results come from the large healthy group and its 5% false-positive rate. These far outnumber the few true positives. This is the base-rate effect. If you ignore how rare something is, you will overreact to evidence. Bayes' rule keeps the prior in the calculation.

The same thing in PyTorch

Bayes' rule for the medical test

import torch

p_disease   = torch.tensor(0.001)   # prior: 1 in 1000
p_pos_sick  = torch.tensor(0.99)    # likelihood: true-positive rate
p_pos_well  = torch.tensor(0.05)    # false-positive rate

# total probability of a positive test (sick + healthy sources)
p_pos = p_pos_sick * p_disease + p_pos_well * (1 - p_disease)

posterior = (p_pos_sick * p_disease) / p_pos
print(posterior)     # tensor(0.0194)  -> about 2%, not 99%

In words: the code follows the calculation line by line. First the total chance of a positive result, then the share of it that comes from sick people.

Check yourself Suppose the disease is much more common: 1 in 10 people (prior 0.1) instead of 1 in 1000. Predict: does a positive test now make you more or less sure that you are sick? Then compute the posterior. Reveal

Much more sure. With $P(D) = 0.1$: $P(+) = (0.99)(0.1) + (0.05)(0.9) = 0.099 + 0.045 = 0.144$. So $P(D \mid +) = 0.099 / 0.144 \approx 0.69$, about 69%, compared with 2% before. The test did not change; only the prior did. When the base rate is high, the false positives no longer outnumber the true positives. The same evidence means very different things depending on how common the condition was to begin with.

5Maximum likelihood: fitting a distribution to data

Intuition first

So far the distributions came with their numbers already known. In real ML you have data, and you must find the numbers yourself. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the standard method for that. Its idea is simple: choose the parameters that make the observed data as probable as possible. Of all the coins that could have produced your flips, MLE chooses the one under which those flips were most likely.

☂ Everyday parallel

Someone rolled one of three dice: a fair die, a die loaded toward 6 (a 6 half the time), or a die loaded toward 1. You see the rolls 6, 6, 3, 6, 5, 6. Which die best explains them? For each die, compute the probability of these exact rolls. The fair die gives $(1/6)^6 \approx 0.00002$. The 6-loaded die gives $0.5^4 \times 0.1^2 = 0.000625$, about 29 times more. The 1-loaded die gives $0.1^6$, far less. MLE picks the 6-loaded die. With a coin, the “dice” are all the values of $p$ between 0 and 1.

Now the math

Let's derive it fully for a coin. You flip a coin with unknown heads probability $p$ and observe $H$ heads in $N$ flips. Each flip is a Bernoulli. The flips are independent, so the probability of the whole sequence is the product of the single probabilities. That product, viewed as a function of the unknown $p$, is the likelihood. We write it $\mathcal{L}(p)$, with a curly L, because plain $L$ means the loss in this book:

$$ \mathcal{L}(p) = p^{\,H}\,(1-p)^{\,N-H} $$

In words: to get exactly this data, you need $H$ heads (each contributes a factor $p$) and $N - H$ tails (each contributes a factor $1 - p$). Multiply them all, and you get the chance of the observed sequence for a given $p$. We want the $p$ that makes this as large as possible.

A product of many probabilities is a tiny number, and products are hard to differentiate. So we take the logarithm first. This helps in two ways. First, it turns products into sums, which makes the calculus easier. Second, $\log$ is an increasing function. So the $p$ that maximizes $\log \mathcal{L}$ is the same $p$ that maximizes $\mathcal{L}$. We lose nothing by working with the log. (In this book, $\log$ means the natural log.)

$$ \ell(p) = \log \mathcal{L}(p) = H \log p + (N - H)\log(1 - p) $$

In words: the $\log$ brought the exponents down as multipliers and turned the product into a sum. This $\ell(p)$ is the log-likelihood, and it is what we maximize.

To find the maximum, use calculus from Section 2. Take the derivative with respect to $p$, set it to zero (the slope is flat at a peak), and solve. Recall that $\frac{d}{dp}\log p = \frac{1}{p}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\ell}{dp} &= \frac{H}{p} - \frac{N - H}{1 - p} = 0 \\[6pt] \frac{H}{p} &= \frac{N - H}{1 - p} \\[6pt] H\,(1 - p) &= (N - H)\,p \\[6pt] H - Hp &= Np - Hp \\[6pt] H &= Np \\[6pt] \hat{p} &= \frac{H}{N} \end{aligned} $$

In words: the best estimate is $\hat{p} = H/N$, the fraction of flips that came up heads. Flip 100 times, see 70 heads, and MLE says the most likely coin has $p = 0.7$. The hat on $\hat{p}$ marks it as an estimate from data, not the true hidden value. Common sense says “count the heads,” and the maximum-likelihood principle gives the same answer. (The slope $H/p - (N-H)/(1-p)$ is positive left of $H/N$ and negative right of it, so this flat point is a peak, as Figure 3.3 shows.)

The same thing in PyTorch

finding p by gradient ascent on the log-likelihood

import torch

# 100 flips, 70 heads: recover p by maximizing the log-likelihood with gradient ascent
H, N = 70.0, 100.0
p = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)   # start with a fair-coin guess

for _ in range(500):
    log_lik = H * torch.log(p) + (N - H) * torch.log(1 - p)
    log_lik.backward()                       # d(log_lik)/dp, from Section 2
    with torch.no_grad():
        p += 0.001 * p.grad                  # step UPHILL (maximize)
        p.grad.zero_()

print(p)          # tensor(0.7000, requires_grad=True)  -> matches p_hat = H/N = 0.7

In words: this is gradient descent from Section 2 with the sign flipped. We add the gradient because we want to go up the log-likelihood, not down. After 500 small steps, $p$ reaches the peak at 0.7.

◆ Where loss functions come from

Look at what we maximized: a log-likelihood. Now flip the sign. Maximizing $\log \mathcal{L}$ is the same as minimizing $-\log \mathcal{L}$. That quantity, the negative log-likelihood, is the loss function most classifiers are trained on. You will meet it as cross-entropy in Section 4. Do the same derivation for a Gaussian instead of a Bernoulli, and you get the mean-squared-error loss behind Section 5's linear regression. So training a model is maximum likelihood in another form. “Find the parameters that make the data most probable” means the same as “find the parameters that minimize the loss.”

Check yourself You flip a coin 10 times and get 10 heads. What does MLE give for $\hat{p}$? Why is that suspicious, and what does it tell you about the limits of pure MLE? Reveal

$\hat{p} = 10/10 = 1.0$: a coin that never lands tails. MLE uses only the data in front of it, and a small sample can mislead. Ten heads in a row from a fair coin is unlikely (about 1 in 1000), but possible. Yet MLE now gives probability zero to ever seeing a tail, after only ten flips. The fix is to include a prior belief (“coins are usually close to fair”) and use Bayes' rule. That pulls the estimate back toward 0.5 until there is enough data to outweigh the prior. This balance between the data and the prior belief leads from maximum likelihood to Bayesian learning.

6What to carry forward

  • A model outputs a distribution: belief spread over outcomes. Discrete variables use a PMF (a mass on each value). Continuous variables use a PDF (area under a curve gives probability).
  • Expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the probability-weighted average, the balance point. Variance $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2$ is the spread: reliable versus streaky.
  • The Bernoulli models one yes/no event with a single $p$ (mean $p$, variance $p(1-p)$). The Gaussian is the bell curve with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. It appears everywhere because sums of many small random effects tend toward it.
  • Bayes' rule $P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}$ updates a prior belief with a likelihood into a posterior. Never ignore the base rate.
  • MLE chooses the parameters that make the observed data most probable. For a coin it gives the plain count $\hat{p} = H/N$. Minimizing the negative log-likelihood is where loss functions come from.
