Backpropagation by Hand

Backprop is just the chain rule with good bookkeeping. We'll compute every gradient in a real network by hand, with actual numbers — nothing left as “it can be shown.”

34 min read You'll learn: the computational graph · the chain rule flowing backward · a full worked numerical example · the local-gradient rule

A neural network learns by nudging its weights in the direction that lowers the loss. To nudge a weight the right way, we need one number for it: the gradient — how much the loss would change if we wiggled that weight a hair. A real network has millions of weights, so we need millions of these numbers, and we need them fast, over and over. Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes all of them in a single sweep backward through the network. This is the engine room of deep learning: once it clicks, most of what follows is variations on this one idea.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Backprop is the chain rule from Section 2 applied to a graph, with the intermediate results stored and reused instead of recomputed. That reuse is the whole trick: it turns “millions of expensive experiments” into “one cheap backward pass.” Everything below is just careful bookkeeping of that idea.

1The goal, and why the obvious method fails

Intuition first

Suppose you want to know how each weight affects the loss. The most obvious idea: try it. Take one weight, add a tiny amount to it, run the whole network again, and see how much the loss moved. Divide the change in loss by the change in weight — that ratio is (approximately) the gradient for that one weight. Then put the weight back and do the next one. It works, and it needs no calculus at all. The problem is speed.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine a mixing desk with a million volume sliders feeding one final loudspeaker, and you want to know how much each slider affects the total loudness. The brute-force way: nudge slider one, replay the whole song, measure the loudness, write it down, reset. Then slider two. Replay the entire song again. A million replays for one reading of the desk. You'd never finish. Backprop is the sound engineer who, in a single playthrough, works out every slider's influence at once — by listening backward from the speaker to each source.

Now the math

Write the loss as $L$ (a single number telling us how wrong the network is) and a weight as $w$. The brute-force (“finite difference”) estimate of the gradient is:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \;\approx\; \frac{L(w+\varepsilon) - L(w)}{\varepsilon} $$

In words: $\partial L/\partial w$ means “how fast the loss $L$ changes as we change the weight $w$.” We estimate it by adding a tiny bump $\varepsilon$ (epsilon, a small number like $10^{-5}$) to the weight, seeing how much $L$ moves, and dividing. The catch: computing $L(w+\varepsilon)$ means running the entire forward pass of the network.

Now count the cost. If the network has $P$ weights, and one forward pass costs $C$ units of work, then estimating every gradient this way costs about:

$$ P \times C \quad\text{units of work.} $$

In words: one full forward pass per weight. For a small model with a million weights ($P = 10^{6}$), that is a million forward passes just to take a single learning step. Modern models have billions of weights and take hundreds of thousands of steps. The brute-force method is not slow — it is impossible.

◆ What backprop buys you

Backprop computes all $P$ gradients in the cost of roughly one extra pass — a backward one — no matter how many weights there are. It does this by reusing partial results: the gradient at a later layer is built from work already done, so nothing is recomputed from scratch. This is a classic case of dynamic programming (solving a big problem by storing and reusing the answers to smaller overlapping pieces).

Check yourself A network has 5 million weights and one forward pass takes 2 milliseconds. Roughly how long does one gradient step cost with brute force, versus with backprop? Reveal

Brute force: one forward pass per weight, so $5{,}000{,}000 \times 2\text{ ms} = 10{,}000{,}000$ ms $\approx$ 2.8 hours for a single learning step — and you need thousands of steps. Backprop: one forward pass plus one backward pass, roughly $2 \times 2\text{ ms} = $ 4 ms. That is the difference between “trains overnight” and “never finishes.” Same gradients, wildly different cost, because backprop reuses intermediate results instead of throwing them away.

2The computational graph

Intuition first

To organise the bookkeeping we draw the network as a computational graph: a diagram of little boxes (called nodes), one per simple operation — multiply, add, apply the sigmoid, and so on. Arrows (the edges) carry numbers from one box into the next. Feeding the input in and letting numbers flow left-to-right is the forward pass — it produces the prediction and the loss. Then we go right-to-left, and that return trip is the backward pass — it produces the gradients.

☂ Everyday parallel

A dish comes out too salty, and the head chef wants to assign blame up the kitchen chain. The final saltiness is the loss. Working backward: the plating cook barely touched the salt, so little blame flows to them; the cook who poured the stock added most of it, so most of the blame flows there; and whoever measured the stock's saltiness in the first place inherits blame in proportion to how much the stock mattered. Each station receives blame equal to (blame passed down from above) × (how much this station actually influenced the next step). That single rule, applied station by station backward through the kitchen, is backprop.

Now the math

Here is the exact graph we will compute with. One input $x$ flows through two layers. Each layer does a linear step (multiply by a weight, add a bias) and then a nonlinear step (the sigmoid, written $\sigma$). The final node compares the output to the target $y$ and produces the loss $L$:

$$ x \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{z_1 = w_1 x + b_1}_{\text{linear}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{a_1 = \sigma(z_1)}_{\text{nonlinear}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{z_2 = w_2 a_1 + b_2}_{\text{linear}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{a_2 = \sigma(z_2)}_{\text{nonlinear}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{L = \tfrac12 (a_2 - y)^2}_{\text{loss}} $$

In words: $x$ is the input number. $w_1, b_1$ are the first layer's weight and bias; $z_1$ is its raw output (the pre-activation) and $a_1$ is that value squashed by the sigmoid into the range $(0,1)$ (the activation). $w_2, b_2, z_2, a_2$ are the same for the second layer, and $a_2$ is the network's final prediction. $y$ is the target we wish $a_2$ equalled, and $L$ is the squared-error loss — half the squared gap. The $\tfrac12$ is a convenience that cancels when we differentiate.

Definition

The forward pass runs the graph left-to-right, computing and storing every intermediate value ($z_1, a_1, z_2, a_2, L$). The backward pass runs it right-to-left, computing the gradient of $L$ with respect to every stored value and every weight. The forward pass must happen first because the backward pass reuses those stored values — that is exactly where the speed comes from.

Check yourself Why must we keep the forward-pass values in memory instead of discarding them once we have the loss? Reveal

Because the backward pass needs them. As we'll see, the gradient for $w_2$ turns out to be $\delta_2 \cdot a_1$ — it literally multiplies by the stored activation $a_1$. If we threw $a_1$ away, we'd have to recompute it, defeating the purpose. This is why training uses far more memory than just running a model for predictions (inference): training must hold the whole forward pass in memory, ready for the return trip.

3The core rule at every node

Intuition first

Every node in the graph obeys the same tiny rule, and once you see it you have seen all of backprop. A node receives, from the node just downstream of it, a number that says “here is how much the final loss cares about my output.” The node then knows one purely local fact: “how much does my output change when my input changes?” Multiply those two together and you get “how much the final loss cares about my input” — which is exactly the message to pass one step further back.

☂ Everyday parallel

Money changing hands down a supply chain. The shop tells the wholesaler “each extra unit you send raises my profit by 2 dollars” (the gradient flowing in). The wholesaler knows a purely local fact: “for every extra kilo of raw material I buy, I can make 3 units” (the local gradient). So the wholesaler tells the farmer “each extra kilo you grow is worth \(2 \times 3 = 6\) dollars to the final profit.” Nobody needs the full picture — each link only multiplies the message from downstream by its own local conversion rate and passes it on.

Now the math

This is the chain rule, stated as a rule you can apply mechanically. Consider any node computing an output $u$ from an input $t$. Let the loss be $L$. Then:

$$ \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}}_{\text{gradient sent to input}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}}_{\text{gradient from above}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}}_{\text{local gradient}} $$

In words: (gradient the loss has for my output) × (how my output responds to my input) = (gradient the loss has for my input). The middle term, $\partial u/\partial t$, is the local gradient — it depends only on what this node does, and can be worked out without knowing anything about the rest of the network. That locality is why backprop is modular: each operation just needs to know its own derivative.

◆ The local-gradient rule

gradient in (from above)  ×  local gradient  =  gradient out (to inputs)

Every node applies exactly this. Backprop is nothing more than starting at the loss (whose gradient with respect to itself is $1$) and applying this rule, node by node, all the way back to the weights. If a value feeds several nodes, its gradient is the sum of the messages coming back from each of them — blame from multiple children adds up.

We will lean on the local gradients of the three operations in our graph. Two are immediate; the third is the sigmoid, whose derivative has a famously tidy form:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{multiply } u = wt &: \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = w, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial w} = t \\[4pt] \text{add } u = t + b &: \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial b} = 1 \\[4pt] \text{sigmoid } a = \sigma(z) &: \quad \frac{\partial a}{\partial z} = \sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr) = a(1-a) \end{aligned} $$

In words: a multiply node passes gradient through scaled by the other input (so $z_2 = w_2 a_1$ sends gradient to $a_1$ scaled by $w_2$, and to $w_2$ scaled by $a_1$). An add node passes gradient through unchanged (local gradient $1$). And the sigmoid's local gradient is just $a(1-a)$ — computed straight from the activation $a$ we already stored on the forward pass. No re-evaluating exponentials on the way back.

Check yourself At a node u = w·t, the gradient arriving from above is ∂L/∂u = −3, and the stored inputs are w = 2, t = 5. What gradients does the node send to t and to w? Reveal

Apply the rule twice. To the input $t$: local gradient is $w = 2$, so $\partial L/\partial t = -3 \times 2 = \mathbf{-6}$. To the weight $w$: local gradient is $t = 5$, so $\partial L/\partial w = -3 \times 5 = \mathbf{-15}$. Notice the multiply node “swaps” its inputs on the way back — each branch is scaled by the other value. That swap is worth memorising; it is the single most common node in any network.

4The centrepiece: a full network, by hand

Now we do the real thing. We take the two-layer graph above, plug in concrete numbers, and compute every value forward and every gradient backward. Nothing is skipped; you can reproduce each number with a calculator. Here are the fixed ingredients:

SymbolMeaningValue
$x$the single input$1.0$
$w_1,\; b_1$layer-1 weight and bias$0.5,\; 0.5$
$w_2,\; b_2$layer-2 weight and bias$1.5,\; -1.0$
$y$the target we want $a_2$ to hit$1.0$

We round every result to four decimal places. The sigmoid is $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$.

Forward pass — compute and store every value

Now the math

Node 1 — first linear step. Multiply the input by $w_1$ and add $b_1$:

$$ z_1 = w_1 x + b_1 = (0.5)(1.0) + 0.5 = \mathbf{1.0000} $$

Node 2 — first sigmoid. Squash $z_1$ into $(0,1)$:

$$ a_1 = \sigma(1.0) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-1.0}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.3679} = \mathbf{0.7311} $$

Node 3 — second linear step. Multiply the activation by $w_2$ and add $b_2$:

$$ z_2 = w_2 a_1 + b_2 = (1.5)(0.7311) + (-1.0) = 1.0966 - 1.0 = \mathbf{0.0966} $$

Node 4 — second sigmoid. This is the network's prediction:

$$ a_2 = \sigma(0.0966) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.0966}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.9079} = \mathbf{0.5241} $$

Node 5 — the loss. Compare the prediction $a_2$ to the target $y = 1.0$:

$$ L = \tfrac12 (a_2 - y)^2 = \tfrac12 (0.5241 - 1.0)^2 = \tfrac12 (-0.4759)^2 = \tfrac12(0.2265) = \mathbf{0.1132} $$

In words: the network currently predicts $0.5241$ but we wanted $1.0$, so it is off by $0.4759$, and the loss is $0.1132$. Every one of these five numbers is now stored; the backward pass will reuse them.

☂ Everyday parallel

The forward pass is like following a recipe end to end and tasting the final dish: too bland, a “wrongness” score of $0.1132$. We wrote down the state after every step — how salty the stock was, how it tasted after simmering — precisely so that, when the dish disappoints, we can walk back through our notes and see which step to blame. That walk back is next.

Backward pass — send the gradient back, node by node

Now the math

We start at the loss and apply the local-gradient rule repeatedly. It helps to name the gradient arriving at each pre-activation; the standard name is $\delta$ (delta). So $\delta_2 = \partial L / \partial z_2$ and $\delta_1 = \partial L / \partial z_1$. Watch how each step reuses a stored forward value.

Step A — through the loss node. Differentiate $L = \tfrac12(a_2 - y)^2$ with respect to $a_2$. The $\tfrac12$ cancels the power's $2$:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_2} = (a_2 - y) = 0.5241 - 1.0 = \mathbf{-0.4759} $$

In words: the loss wants $a_2$ to increase (the gradient is negative, and moving against a negative gradient means increasing $a_2$) — which makes sense, since $0.5241$ is below the target $1.0$.

Step B — through the second sigmoid. The local gradient is $a_2(1-a_2)$, built from the stored $a_2$. Multiply it by the gradient from above to get $\delta_2$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial z_2} &= a_2(1-a_2) = (0.5241)(0.4759) = 0.2494 \\[4pt] \delta_2 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_2}\cdot\frac{\partial a_2}{\partial z_2} = (-0.4759)(0.2494) = \mathbf{-0.1187} \end{aligned} $$

In words: we took the message $-0.4759$ and scaled it by the sigmoid's local gradient $0.2494$. That $0.2494$ came for free from the stored $a_2$ — no exponentials recomputed. $\delta_2$ is now the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer-2 pre-activation.

Step C — the layer-2 weight and bias. Recall $z_2 = w_2 a_1 + b_2$. The multiply node sends gradient to $w_2$ scaled by the other input $a_1$ (stored!); the add node sends gradient to $b_2$ unchanged:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} &= \delta_2 \cdot \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2} = \delta_2 \cdot a_1 = (-0.1187)(0.7311) = \mathbf{-0.0868} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} &= \delta_2 \cdot \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial b_2} = \delta_2 \cdot 1 = \mathbf{-0.1187} \end{aligned} $$

In words: two of our four weight gradients are already done. The weight gradient reused the stored activation $a_1 = 0.7311$; the bias gradient is just $\delta_2$ itself, because adding a bias passes gradient through untouched.

Step D — back into layer 1. The multiply node also sends gradient to its other input $a_1$, scaled by $w_2$:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1} = \delta_2 \cdot \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial a_1} = \delta_2 \cdot w_2 = (-0.1187)(1.5) = \mathbf{-0.1780} $$

In words: we have crossed from layer 2 into layer 1. The blame for the loss has been handed to $a_1$, the first layer's activation. Now we push it through the first sigmoid, exactly as we did the second.

Step E — through the first sigmoid. Same rule as Step B, now with the stored $a_1$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial z_1} &= a_1(1-a_1) = (0.7311)(0.2689) = 0.1966 \\[4pt] \delta_1 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1}\cdot\frac{\partial a_1}{\partial z_1} = (-0.1780)(0.1966) = \mathbf{-0.0350} \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\delta_1$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer-1 pre-activation. Notice it is much smaller than $\delta_2$ — the signal shrank as it passed back through the sigmoid (whose local gradient is at most $0.25$). Stack many sigmoid layers and the signal keeps shrinking; that is the vanishing gradient problem in one glance.

Step F — the layer-1 weight and bias. Recall $z_1 = w_1 x + b_1$. Same pattern as Step C, now with the stored input $x$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} &= \delta_1 \cdot \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_1} = \delta_1 \cdot x = (-0.0350)(1.0) = \mathbf{-0.0350} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1} &= \delta_1 \cdot \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial b_1} = \delta_1 \cdot 1 = \mathbf{-0.0350} \end{aligned} $$

In words: all four weight gradients are done. We swept from the loss back to the very first weight, and at each node we did nothing but “gradient from above × local gradient,” reusing stored values. That entire sweep is one backward pass.

◆ All four gradients, in one place

These are the numbers we came for — the slope of the loss along each weight:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b_1}$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b_2}$
$-0.0350$$-0.0350$$-0.0868$$-0.1187$

Every one is negative, meaning: increasing any of these weights would lower the loss. So the update will increase them all.

One gradient-descent step — and the loss really drops

Now the math

Gradient descent moves each weight a small step against its gradient (downhill on the loss). The step size is the learning rate $\alpha$ (alpha). Take $\alpha = 1.0$. The update rule for any weight is $w \leftarrow w - \alpha\,\partial L/\partial w$:

$$ \begin{aligned} w_1 &\leftarrow 0.5 - (1.0)(-0.0350) = \mathbf{0.5350} \\ b_1 &\leftarrow 0.5 - (1.0)(-0.0350) = \mathbf{0.5350} \\ w_2 &\leftarrow 1.5 - (1.0)(-0.0868) = \mathbf{1.5868} \\ b_2 &\leftarrow -1.0 - (1.0)(-0.1187) = \mathbf{-0.8813} \end{aligned} $$

In words: subtracting a negative gradient means adding — every weight grew slightly, exactly as the signs predicted. Now the honest test: does the loss actually fall? Run the forward pass again with the new weights.

New forward pass:

$$ \begin{aligned} z_1 &= (0.5350)(1.0) + 0.5350 = 1.0700, & a_1 &= \sigma(1.0700) = 0.7446 \\ z_2 &= (1.5868)(0.7446) + (-0.8813) = 0.3002, & a_2 &= \sigma(0.3002) = 0.5745 \\ L_{\text{new}} &= \tfrac12(0.5745 - 1.0)^2 = \tfrac12(0.1811) = \mathbf{0.0905} \end{aligned} $$

In words: the loss fell from $0.1132$ to $\mathbf{0.0905}$, and the prediction rose from $0.5241$ toward the target — from $0.5241$ to $0.5745$. One backward pass, one step, and the network is measurably less wrong. Repeat this loop thousands of times and you have training.

☂ Everyday parallel

You are hiking down a foggy hill and can only feel the slope under your feet. The gradient is that felt slope; the learning rate is your stride length. You take one step straight downhill, re-feel the slope, and step again. You never see the whole valley, yet step by step you reach the bottom. Backprop is what tells you the slope; gradient descent is the step.

The same thing in PyTorch (manual, no autograd)

reproduces every number above by hand

import torch

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + torch.exp(-z))

# fixed ingredients
x  = torch.tensor(1.0)
w1, b1 = torch.tensor(0.5), torch.tensor(0.5)
w2, b2 = torch.tensor(1.5), torch.tensor(-1.0)
y  = torch.tensor(1.0)

# ---- forward pass: compute and keep every value ----
z1 = w1 * x + b1        # 1.0000
a1 = sigmoid(z1)        # 0.7311
z2 = w2 * a1 + b2       # 0.0966
a2 = sigmoid(z2)        # 0.5241
L  = 0.5 * (a2 - y)**2  # 0.1132

# ---- backward pass: gradient in x local gradient, node by node ----
dL_da2 = (a2 - y)               # -0.4759
d2     = dL_da2 * a2 * (1 - a2) # delta2 = -0.1187
dL_dw2 = d2 * a1                # -0.0868
dL_db2 = d2                     # -0.1187
dL_da1 = d2 * w2                # -0.1780
d1     = dL_da1 * a1 * (1 - a1) # delta1 = -0.0350
dL_dw1 = d1 * x                 # -0.0350
dL_db1 = d1                     # -0.0350

print(dL_dw1, dL_db1, dL_dw2, dL_db2)

# ---- one gradient-descent step (learning rate 1.0) ----
alpha = 1.0
w1, b1 = w1 - alpha*dL_dw1, b1 - alpha*dL_db1
w2, b2 = w2 - alpha*dL_dw2, b2 - alpha*dL_db2

a2_new = sigmoid(w2 * sigmoid(w1*x + b1) + b2)
print(0.5 * (a2_new - y)**2)    # 0.0905  -> loss dropped
Check yourself Why is ∂L/∂b₁ equal to ∂L/∂w₁ here (both −0.0350), and would they still be equal if the input x were 2.0 instead of 1.0? Reveal

Both are $\delta_1$ times a local gradient. For the bias the local gradient is $1$ (adding passes gradient through), giving $\partial L/\partial b_1 = \delta_1$. For the weight the local gradient is the input $x$, giving $\partial L/\partial w_1 = \delta_1 \cdot x$. They are equal here only because $x = 1.0$, so multiplying by it changes nothing. With $x = 2.0$ the weight gradient would double to $\delta_1 \cdot 2 = -0.0700$ while the bias gradient would stay $-0.0350$. A weight's gradient always scales with the input feeding it; a bias's never does.

5The same rules for a whole layer at once

Intuition first

Real layers have many neurons, so weights form a matrix $\mathbf{W}$ and activations form vectors. The good news: the rules do not change at all. “Multiply node swaps its inputs” becomes “multiply by the transpose of the matrix.” The scalar $\delta$ becomes a vector $\boldsymbol{\delta}$, one entry per neuron. Everything you just did by hand now runs on whole layers in one matrix operation — which is exactly what a GPU is built to do fast.

☂ Everyday parallel

Doing the whole payroll at once instead of one employee at a time. The per-person rule (hours × rate) never changed — you just stacked everyone into a table and ran the arithmetic in one sweep. Vectorising backprop is the same move: identical rule, applied to the entire layer in a single matrix multiply.

Now the math

For a layer $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ followed by an activation $\mathbf{a} = f(\mathbf{z})$, let $\boldsymbol{\delta} = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{z}$ be the gradient arriving at the layer's pre-activations. Then the three gradients we need are:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}} &= \boldsymbol{\delta}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top} &&\text{(gradient for the weight matrix)} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}} &= \boldsymbol{\delta} &&\text{(gradient for the bias vector)} \\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{x}} &= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta} &&\text{(gradient to send to the previous layer)} \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\mathbf{x}^{\top}$ is the input row-vector (the little $\top$ means transpose — flip a column into a row). These are the exact scalar rules from Step C and Step D, wearing matrix clothes: the weight gradient is “$\delta$ times the stored input,” the bias gradient is “just $\delta$,” and the gradient handed to the previous layer is “$\delta$ scaled by the weights,” now with a transpose so the shapes line up.

To keep the chain going one more layer back, push that returned gradient through the previous layer's activation — multiply by its local gradient $f'$, element by element:

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{prev}} = \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}\bigr) \odot f'(\mathbf{z}_{\text{prev}}) $$

In words: $\odot$ means multiply matching entries (the element-wise product). So each neuron's incoming gradient gets scaled by its own local slope $f'$. This one line, repeated layer to layer, is the backward pass for a deep network — the same “gradient in × local gradient = gradient out” you did by hand, vectorised.

The same thing in PyTorch (vectorized layer)

import torch

# a layer: W is (out=3, in=4), one input vector x of length 4
W = torch.randn(3, 4)
x = torch.randn(4)
delta = torch.randn(3)        # gradient arriving at this layer's z

dL_dW = torch.outer(delta, x) # delta @ x^T  -> shape (3, 4), matches W
dL_db = delta                 # -> shape (3,)
dL_dx = W.T @ delta           # -> shape (4,), sent to the previous layer

print(dL_dW.shape, dL_db.shape, dL_dx.shape)  # (3,4) (3,) (4,)
▲ Watch out

The transposes are not decoration — they are what makes the shapes fit. $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{W}$ must have the same shape as $\mathbf{W}$ itself (you subtract it from $\mathbf{W}$ during the update), and $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{x}$ must match $\mathbf{x}$. If a backward pass throws a shape error, a misplaced transpose is the first suspect. When stuck, print shapes on every line, exactly as in Section 1.

6Trust, but verify: gradient checking

Intuition first

Hand-derived gradients are easy to get subtly wrong — a dropped transpose, a sign flip. There is a dead-simple way to catch it: compare your fancy backprop gradient against the crude brute-force estimate from the start of this section. If they agree to several decimals, your backprop is almost certainly correct. This is the numerical gradient check, and it has saved more from-scratch networks than any other single trick.

☂ Everyday parallel

You add up a long shopping bill in your head, then also run it through the till. If both give \$47.30, you trust your arithmetic. If they disagree, you know there is a bug somewhere before you rely on the total. The till is slow, so you don't use it for every bill — just to confirm your method once.

Now the math

Use the centred difference — bump the weight both up and down by $\varepsilon$. It is far more accurate than the one-sided version we opened with:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \;\approx\; \frac{L(w+\varepsilon) - L(w-\varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon} $$

In words: nudge the weight up, nudge it down, and take the average slope between. With $\varepsilon = 10^{-5}$ this typically matches the true gradient to $6$–$8$ decimal places. It is far too slow to train with (one forward pass per weight — the very problem backprop solves), but it is perfect as a one-off check that backprop is wired correctly.

Checking our example's gradient for $w_1$

import torch

def loss(w1, b1, w2, b2, x=torch.tensor(1.0), y=torch.tensor(1.0)):
    a1 = 1/(1 + torch.exp(-(w1*x + b1)))
    a2 = 1/(1 + torch.exp(-(w2*a1 + b2)))
    return 0.5 * (a2 - y)**2

w1, b1, w2, b2 = (torch.tensor(v) for v in (0.5, 0.5, 1.5, -1.0))
eps = 1e-5
num_grad_w1 = (loss(w1+eps, b1, w2, b2) - loss(w1-eps, b1, w2, b2)) / (2*eps)

print(num_grad_w1)   # tensor(-0.0350)  -- matches our hand-computed delta1 * x

In words: the numerical estimate lands on $-0.0350$, the same value we derived by hand in Step F. That agreement is the proof our backward pass is correct. We build this into a proper debugging habit in Section 15.

◆ You will never write this by hand again

Deriving gradients by hand is how you understand backprop — but in practice a library does it for you. Autograd (Section 13) records the computational graph as your forward code runs, then applies the exact local-gradient rule you just used at every node, automatically. And in Section 11 we code a full multi-layer network's backward pass from scratch in NumPy, so you see there is no magic — only the bookkeeping you now understand.

Check yourself Your backprop gradient for some weight is 0.4000, but the centred numerical check gives −0.4000. What kind of bug does that specific mismatch point to? Reveal

A sign error. When the magnitudes match but the signs are opposite, you have almost certainly flipped a subtraction somewhere — a $y - a$ written as $a - y$, or a gradient added where it should be subtracted (or vice-versa) in the update. It is one of the most common backprop bugs, and gradient checking catches it instantly. If instead the magnitudes were wildly different, suspect a missing local-gradient factor (forgot to multiply by $a(1-a)$) or a transpose in the vectorized form.

7What to carry forward

  • Training needs $\partial L/\partial w$ for every weight; brute force costs one forward pass per weight and is hopeless. Backprop gets them all in one backward pass by reusing stored forward values — dynamic programming.
  • The computational graph is the network as nodes (operations) and edges (values). Forward computes and stores values; backward computes gradients.
  • The local-gradient rule at every node: gradient in (from above) × local gradient = gradient out (to inputs). If a value feeds several nodes, sum the incoming gradients.
  • Handy local gradients: multiply swaps its inputs; add passes gradient through unchanged; sigmoid's is $a(1-a)$, free from the stored activation.
  • We computed a whole two-layer network by hand: forward to $L = 0.1132$, backward to four gradients, one step, and the loss fell to $0.0905$ — verified in code.
  • Vectorized, the same rules read $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{W} = \boldsymbol{\delta}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}$, $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{b} = \boldsymbol{\delta}$, $\partial L/\partial\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}$.
  • Gradient checking — compare to $(L(w+\varepsilon)-L(w-\varepsilon))/2\varepsilon$ — is your safety net; a sign flip or dropped factor shows up at once.
