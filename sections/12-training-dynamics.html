Training Dynamics & Optimizers

The same network can fail or fly depending on how you start it and how you step — here's the why behind the knobs.

32 min read You'll learn: weight initialization (Xavier/He) · learning rates · SGD, momentum, Adam · LR schedules

You have a network. You have a loss (a single number saying how wrong the network is) and you have backpropagation to tell you which way each weight should move. So training is done, right? Not quite. Two choices you make before and during that loop decide whether it converges in a minute or never converges at all: the numbers you start the weights at, and the size of the steps you take. This section is about those two knobs — the ones that turn a working design into a working model.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Backprop tells you the direction to move each weight. It says nothing about where to begin or how far to step. Those two decisions — initialization and the optimizer — are what "training dynamics" means, and getting them wrong breaks networks that are otherwise perfectly correct.

1Initialization: where the weights start

Intuition first

Before the first step of training, every weight in the network needs a starting value. It feels like it shouldn't matter much — training will fix them anyway, won't it? But the starting point secretly controls two things at once: whether the neurons can ever become different from each other, and whether the signal can travel through a deep stack of layers without either dying out or blowing up. Get either wrong and the network learns slowly, badly, or not at all.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine a study group where everyone starts with the identical notes and always copies each other's answers. No matter how long they study, they stay perfect clones — the group is no smarter than one person. To actually cover the material, the members have to start from slightly different places so they can specialise. A neural network's neurons are the same: they must start different to end up doing different jobs.

Why not all zeros — the symmetry problem, proven

Now the math

The tempting "safe" choice is to set every weight to zero. Let's prove why it destroys the network. Take one layer with two identical-looking neurons. Each neuron $j$ computes a weighted sum of the same inputs $\mathbf{x}$, then passes it through an activation function $\phi$ (a simple nonlinear squashing function like ReLU or sigmoid):

$$ a_j = \phi\!\left(\sum_i w_{ji}\, x_i + b_j\right) $$

In words: neuron j's output is: multiply each input xᵢ by its weight wⱼᵢ, add them up, add the bias bⱼ, then squash. wⱼᵢ is the weight from input i into neuron j.

Now set every weight and bias to the same value — say zero. Then for every neuron $j$ in the layer, the sum inside is the same number, so every neuron produces the same output:

$$ w_{ji} = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_n = \phi(0) $$

That is already bad, but the killer is what backprop does next. The gradient that flows back into weight $w_{ji}$ depends only on that neuron's input $x_i$ and the error signal arriving at neuron $j$. Because all neurons in the layer are identical, they all receive the identical error signal, so every weight in the layer gets the identical gradient:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{1i}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{2i}} = \dots = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ni}} $$

In words: ∂L/∂wⱼᵢ means "how much the loss L changes if we nudge weight wⱼᵢ." When every neuron is a copy, every one of these numbers is equal — so the optimizer nudges them all by the same amount.

Follow the consequence. If they start equal and always get updated by the same amount, they stay equal forever. The two neurons remain perfect clones for the entire run. A layer of 100 neurons behaves like a layer of one. This is the symmetry problem: nothing in the update rule can ever break the tie, so the neurons never differentiate.

◆ The fix

Break the symmetry by starting the weights at small random numbers. Now each neuron sees a slightly different weighted sum, receives a slightly different gradient, and is free to specialise. Random initialization isn't a nice-to-have — it's the only thing that lets a wide layer be more expressive than a single neuron. (Biases can safely start at zero; the random weights already break the tie.)

Why not too large, and not too small

Intuition first

Fine — random. But how big random? This is the second job of initialization. A deep network passes the signal through many layers in a row. If each layer tends to magnify the signal a little, that magnification compounds layer after layer and the numbers explode to infinity. If each layer shrinks it a little, the signal fades to nothing before it reaches the end. Either way, learning stalls.

☂ Everyday parallel

This is a microphone next to its own speaker. Set the gain slightly too high and every pass through the loop is louder than the last — you get the screaming feedback squeal (exploding). Set it too low and the sound dies out to silence before you can hear it (vanishing). There is a narrow band of gain where the sound carries cleanly. Initialization is setting that gain for the network's signal.

This is exactly the eigenvalue story from Section 1: passing a signal through a stack of layers is repeated multiplication by weight matrices, and repeated multiplication grows or decays according to whether the matrices stretch by more or less than 1. We want each layer to stretch by about 1 — to preserve the size of the signal — so it survives all the way through.

Deriving the right scale: variance preservation

Now the math

Let's turn "stretch by about 1" into an actual formula. Consider one neuron's pre-activation (the weighted sum before the squashing function):

$$ z = \sum_{i=1}^{n_\text{in}} w_i\, x_i $$

In words: z is the sum over all n_in incoming connections of weight times input. n_in (called fan-in) is simply how many inputs feed this neuron.

We want the output $z$ to have roughly the same spread as the inputs, so the signal neither grows nor shrinks. "Spread" is measured by variance (a number saying how far values typically stray from their average). Assume the weights and inputs start centered at zero, are independent, and each input has variance $\mathrm{Var}(x)$. The variance of a sum of independent zero-mean products adds up term by term:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{n_\text{in}} \mathrm{Var}(w_i\, x_i) = n_\text{in}\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathrm{Var}(x) $$

In words: there are n_in terms in the sum, and each contributes Var(w)·Var(x) (the variance of a product of two independent zero-mean numbers). So the output variance is the input variance multiplied by the factor n_in · Var(w).

For the output spread to equal the input spread, that multiplying factor must be exactly 1:

$$ n_\text{in}\,\mathrm{Var}(w) = 1 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\;\mathrm{Var}(w) = \frac{1}{n_\text{in}}\;} $$

In words: initialize each weight from a distribution whose variance is 1 divided by the number of inputs. A neuron with more inputs must start with smaller weights, because it is summing more terms. That single line is the seed of every modern init scheme.

Xavier / Glorot initialization refines this by noticing that signal flows two ways — forward (the activations) and backward (the gradients). The forward pass wants $\mathrm{Var}(w)=1/n_\text{in}$; the backward pass wants $\mathrm{Var}(w)=1/n_\text{out}$. Glorot's compromise averages the two:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}} $$

In words: use the average of the fan-in and fan-out constraints. n_out (fan-out) is how many neurons this one feeds. Xavier is designed for activations that are symmetric around zero, like tanh and sigmoid.

He (Kaiming) initialization fixes Xavier for ReLU. ReLU ($\max(0,z)$ — it zeroes out every negative input) throws away half the signal on average, halving the variance at each layer. He compensates by doubling the scale:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_\text{in}} $$

In words: the extra factor of 2 exactly cancels the halving that ReLU causes, so the variance is preserved again. This is why the numerator is 2, not 1.

Which to use, in one line

Use He (Kaiming) initialization with ReLU and its relatives (the default for almost every modern network). Use Xavier (Glorot) with tanh or sigmoid activations. Both come in a "normal" flavour (draw from a bell curve with the target variance) and a "uniform" flavour (draw from a flat range with the same variance); the difference rarely matters.

The same thing in PyTorch

He init for a ReLU layer — and what PyTorch does by default

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

layer = nn.Linear(256, 128)   # fan_in = 256, fan_out = 128

# He / Kaiming init, the right choice when a ReLU follows this layer
nn.init.kaiming_normal_(layer.weight, nonlinearity='relu')
nn.init.zeros_(layer.bias)     # biases can safely start at zero

# check the empirical variance is about 2 / fan_in = 2/256 ~ 0.0078
print(layer.weight.var().item())   # ~0.0078

# For a tanh/sigmoid layer you'd use Xavier instead:
# nn.init.xavier_normal_(layer.weight)

Good news: nn.Linear and nn.Conv2d already apply a Kaiming-style init out of the box, so you rarely call these by hand. You reach for them when you build a custom layer, or when a network mysteriously refuses to learn and you suspect the starting scale.

▲ Watch out

Two silent killers. All-zero weights — the symmetry trap above; the network trains but never beats a single-neuron layer. Default-scale weights that are too big for a very deep net — the loss shows up as NaN within a few steps because the activations overflowed. If your loss goes to NaN immediately, suspect the initialization (or the learning rate, next) before anything else.

Check yourself You build a 50-layer network with ReLU activations but initialize every weight from a standard normal (variance 1, ignoring fan-in). What happens in the forward pass, and why? Reveal

The activations explode. With variance 1 per weight and a fan-in of, say, 256, each layer multiplies the signal variance by roughly n_in · Var(w) = 256 (before ReLU). Compounded over 50 layers that is an astronomically large factor, so the pre-activations overflow to inf, and the loss becomes NaN on the first forward pass. The fix is to scale the variance down to 2/n_in (He init), which makes each layer's multiplying factor about 1 instead of 256 — the signal then travels all 50 layers intact. This is the eigenvalue "repeated multiplication" story from Section 1 in action.

2The learning rate: how far to step

Intuition first

Gradient descent moves each weight a little bit in the downhill direction that backprop found. The learning rate (written $\alpha$, and sometimes called the step size) is the single number that says how big "a little bit" is. It is the most important hyperparameter you will tune, and it has a real sweet-spot: too big and the steps overshoot and the loss diverges; too small and training crawls, taking forever to reach the bottom.

☂ Everyday parallel

You're walking downhill in thick fog, trying to reach the bottom of a valley, and you can only feel the slope right under your feet. Take giant strides and you'll leap clear over the valley and land higher up the far slope — bouncing back and forth, never settling. Take tiny baby steps and you'll get there eventually, but the sun will set first. The learning rate is the length of your stride in that fog.

Now the math

The update rule for one weight (or the whole parameter vector $\theta$) is just:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \nabla_\theta L $$

In words: the arrow means "replace the left side with the right side." Take the current parameters θ, subtract the gradient ∇L (the uphill direction) scaled by the learning rate α. Subtracting the uphill direction means stepping downhill. α controls the length of that step.

Why does too-large $\alpha$ diverge? On a simple bowl- shaped loss, if the step is larger than the curvature can absorb, each step lands on the opposite wall of the bowl higher than before. The loss then grows every step instead of shrinking — the tell-tale sign that your learning rate is too high.

▲ Watch out

The classic learning-rate signatures, worth memorising: loss going to NaN or rising = too high, lower it 10×. Loss falling but painfully slowly, almost a flat line = too low, raise it. A good first guess for Adam is 1e-3; for plain SGD, 1e-1 to 1e-2. Always tune the learning rate before fiddling with anything fancier.

Check yourself Your training loss zig-zags up and down every step and slowly trends upward. Learning rate too high or too low? Reveal

Too high. The zig-zag is the optimizer overshooting the valley and bouncing off the opposite wall each step, landing higher than it started — exactly the giant-strides-in-fog failure. Divide the learning rate by 10 and the bouncing usually turns into smooth descent. (A loss that falls but very slowly, with no bouncing, would be the opposite problem: too low.)

3Optimizers: smarter ways to step

Plain gradient descent takes the raw gradient and steps. Optimizers are recipes that reshape that step to make training faster and more stable. We'll build up the three you actually meet — SGD, momentum, and Adam — each one fixing a flaw in the last.

SGD and why we use minibatches

Intuition first

The "true" gradient is the average of the gradient over your entire training set. Computing that for every single step is wildly expensive when you have millions of examples. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) — "stochastic" just means "random" — takes a shortcut: estimate the gradient from a small random handful of examples, called a minibatch, and step on that estimate. It's noisy, but it's cheap, and you get to take hundreds of steps in the time one full-dataset step would cost.

☂ Everyday parallel

To learn whether a national election is leaning left or right, you don't phone all 50 million voters — you poll a random 1,000. The poll is a little off from the true number (that's the noise), but it's a thousand times faster and cheap enough to repeat every day. A minibatch is that opinion poll: a small random sample that gives a good-enough read on which way to step.

Now the math

SGD is the update from the last section, computed on a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ instead of the whole set:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \nabla_\theta L_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta) $$

In words: same downhill step as before, but the gradient ∇L_B is measured on just the current minibatch B — a noisy but unbiased estimate of the true gradient. "Unbiased" means that on average, over many batches, it points the right way.

The noise is not purely a cost. Its little random kicks can bump the optimizer out of shallow bad spots (poor local dips in the loss) that a perfectly smooth gradient would get stuck in. Cheap and occasionally helpful.

Momentum: remember where you were going

Intuition first

Pure SGD has a weakness: it only knows the slope right now. In a long narrow valley it wastes energy bouncing across the walls instead of rolling along the floor, and it stalls on flat patches. Momentum fixes this by keeping a running memory of the recent gradients and stepping in that accumulated direction — so consistent downhill directions build up speed, and little side-to-side wobbles cancel out.

☂ Everyday parallel

Roll a heavy bowling ball down a bumpy hill. A tiny dip or pebble doesn't stop it — its built-up momentum carries it straight over. And because it's heavy, random little bumps to the left and right mostly cancel, so it holds a steady line toward the bottom. That accumulated "want to keep going this way" is exactly what momentum adds to the raw gradient.

Now the math

Momentum keeps a velocity vector $v$, which is an exponentially-decaying moving average (EMA) of past gradients — a running average that weights recent gradients more and lets old ones fade:

$$ \begin{aligned} v &\leftarrow \beta\, v + \nabla_\theta L \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, v \end{aligned} $$

In words: first line — the new velocity is β (beta, typically 0.9) times the old velocity, plus the fresh gradient. Because we keep 90% of the old velocity each step, past gradients pile up and decay slowly. Second line — step in the direction of that accumulated velocity, not the raw gradient. A steady downhill direction reinforces itself step after step and gains speed; noisy sideways components average toward zero.

Definition

An EMA (exponentially-decaying moving average) blends a running value with each new observation: $v \leftarrow \beta v + (1-\beta)\,g$. A high β (like 0.9) means a long memory (each value influences many future steps); a low β means a short memory. It shows up three times in this section — in momentum, and twice inside Adam.

Adam: momentum plus a personal step size for every weight

Intuition first

Momentum fixed direction. There's a second problem it leaves alone: different weights need different step sizes. Some sit on steep parts of the loss (small steps, please) and some on nearly flat parts (bigger steps, please), and a single global learning rate can't suit both. Adam ("Adaptive Moment Estimation") solves both at once — it uses momentum for direction and gives every single parameter its own automatically-tuned step size. It's the default optimizer for most of deep learning for a reason.

☂ Everyday parallel

A smart hiker descending a mountain. She remembers the general downhill direction she's been heading and keeps her momentum (that's the first part). But she also adapts her stride to the ground: long confident strides on smooth open slopes, short careful steps on steep rocky bits where a big step would send her tumbling. Different terrain, different step — per foot, per moment. Adam gives every weight that kind of terrain-aware footing.

Now the math

Adam keeps two running averages of the gradient $g = \nabla_\theta L$. Let $t$ be the step count, starting at 1. First, the first moment $m$ — an EMA of the gradient itself, i.e. plain momentum, tracking which way to go:

$$ m \leftarrow \beta_1\, m + (1-\beta_1)\, g $$

In words: blend the running direction m with the fresh gradient. β₁ is usually 0.9. This is the "momentum" half — the smoothed direction.

Second, the second moment $v$ — an EMA of the gradient squared (each component squared, elementwise), tracking how big and bouncy each parameter's gradient has been:

$$ v \leftarrow \beta_2\, v + (1-\beta_2)\, g^2 $$

In words: blend the running squared-gradient v with the fresh squared gradient. β₂ is usually 0.999 (a very long memory). A parameter whose gradient has been large and jumpy builds a large v; a parameter with small steady gradients has a small v. This is the per-parameter terrain sensor.

There's a cold-start problem. Both $m$ and $v$ start at zero, so for the first several steps they're biased toward zero — too small — which would make the early steps wrong. The bias correction scales them back up to their true size:

$$ \hat{m} = \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^{\,t}}, \qquad \hat{v} = \frac{v}{1 - \beta_2^{\,t}} $$

In words: divide by (1 − βᵗ). At step t = 1 this divisor is small (like 0.1), so it inflates the tiny early estimates back to full size. As t grows, βᵗ shrinks toward 0, the divisor approaches 1, and the correction quietly switches off. It's a warm-up patch for the first dozen or so steps.

Finally the update — momentum direction $\hat{m}$, divided by the per-parameter scale $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\,\frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon} $$

In words: step in the smoothed direction m̂, but divide each parameter's step by the square root of its own v̂. A weight with a big, bouncy gradient (large v̂) gets a smaller step; a weight with small steady gradients gets a larger one. ε (epsilon, about 1e-8) is a tiny number added only to avoid dividing by zero. So m̂ answers "which way," and √v̂ hands each parameter its own adaptive step size — that's the whole trick.

◆ Adam in one breath

m is a smoothed direction (momentum). √v is a per-parameter step-size dial (adaptivity). Bias correction fixes the too-small first few steps. Put them together and you get an optimizer that mostly "just works" at learning rate 1e-3 without hand-tuning per-layer — which is why it's everyone's default.

The same thing in PyTorch

the three optimizers, same interface

import torch

model = torch.nn.Linear(64, 10)

# plain SGD
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=1e-2)

# SGD with momentum (beta = 0.9)
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=1e-2, momentum=0.9)

# Adam: betas = (beta1, beta2), eps as derived above
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3,
                       betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8)

# the training step is identical whichever you chose:
loss = model(torch.randn(32, 64)).sum()   # stand-in for a real loss
opt.zero_grad()    # clear last step's gradients (they accumulate otherwise)
loss.backward()    # backprop fills every .grad
opt.step()         # the optimizer applies its update rule

Notice the loop is the same three calls regardless of optimizer — zero_grad, backward, step. The optimizer object hides all the momentum and adaptivity math behind that one step().

▲ Watch out

Forgetting opt.zero_grad() is the most common training bug there is. PyTorch accumulates gradients into .grad by design, so if you skip the reset, this step's gradient is added on top of last step's leftover — your updates quietly go wrong and the model won't converge. Clear the gradients every single step.

Check yourself In Adam, one weight consistently has much larger gradients than the others. Compared to a plain fixed learning rate, does Adam take bigger or smaller steps for that weight, and which term causes it? Reveal

Smaller steps. A consistently large gradient builds up a large second moment v (the EMA of squared gradients), and the update divides by √v̂. Dividing by a big number shrinks that weight's step. This is the per-parameter adaptivity: Adam automatically reins in the jumpy, high-gradient weights while letting the calm, small-gradient weights take relatively larger steps — so a single global learning rate no longer has to suit every parameter at once.

4Learning-rate schedules: change the step over time

Intuition first

Even the best fixed learning rate is a compromise. Early in training you want big steps to cover ground fast; late in training, near the bottom, you want small steps so you can settle precisely into the minimum instead of bouncing around it. A learning-rate schedule changes $\alpha$ as training goes on — usually starting higher and shrinking it over time.

☂ Everyday parallel

Parking a car. You approach the spot quickly (big steps), then slow right down for the final few centimetres so you don't bump the curb (small steps). Or think of cooling metal in blacksmithing: hot and mobile at first so it can reshape, then cooled slowly so it settles into a stable, precise form. Lowering the learning rate is that same "move fast, then finish gently."

Now the math

Three schedules you'll meet constantly. Step decay multiplies the learning rate by a factor $\gamma$ (gamma, e.g. 0.1) every fixed number of epochs (an epoch = one full pass over the training data):

$$ \alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t / s \rfloor} $$

In words: start at α₀; every s epochs, drop the rate by the factor γ. The ⌊ ⌋ (floor) just counts how many full drops have happened so far. Simple staircase: flat, then a sudden cut, then flat again.

Cosine decay lowers the rate smoothly along the shape of a cosine curve, from $\alpha_0$ down to (near) zero over the total training length $T$:

$$ \alpha_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha_0\left(1 + \cos\!\frac{\pi t}{T}\right) $$

In words: at the start (t=0) the cosine is 1, so αₜ = α₀; at the end (t=T) the cosine is −1, so αₜ = 0. In between it glides down a smooth S-shaped curve — no abrupt staircase steps. This is the most popular modern choice.

Warmup is the opposite move, used only at the very start: begin at a tiny learning rate and raise it over the first few hundred or thousand steps before letting a decay schedule take over. Why? Early on, the weights are freshly random and the gradient estimates are wild; a big step right away can throw the model into a bad region it never recovers from. Warmup lets the running averages (Adam's $m$ and $v$) stabilise before you trust them with full-size steps.

◆ Why transformers always warm up

Large transformer models are famously touchy in their first few hundred steps — without warmup the loss often diverges immediately. So the standard recipe is linear warmup then cosine (or inverse-square-root) decay: ramp the learning rate up gently, then glide it back down. You'll see this exact schedule again in Section 18; now you know what every line of it is doing and why.

Optimizer plus a scheduler

Adam with cosine decay — the common modern combo

import torch

model = torch.nn.Linear(64, 10)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

# cosine decay from 1e-3 down to ~0 over the whole run (T_max epochs)
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100)

for epoch in range(100):
    for x, y in train_loader:              # each minibatch
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()                         # update weights
    scheduler.step()                       # lower the LR once per epoch

    # inspect the current learning rate at any time:
    print(epoch, scheduler.get_last_lr()[0])

The rule of thumb: call opt.step() every minibatch, but call scheduler.step() once per epoch (unless the scheduler's docs say otherwise — a few per-step schedulers exist). The scheduler simply overwrites the lr that the optimizer reads on its next step.

Check yourself Your model trains fine with a fixed learning rate but the loss stalls and jitters in the last third of training, never quite settling. What single change is most likely to help, and why? Reveal

Add a decay schedule (step or cosine) so the learning rate shrinks later in training. The late-stage jitter is the optimizer taking steps too large to settle into the narrow minimum — it keeps overshooting by a little, like trying to park at full speed. Lowering $\alpha$ over time lets it inch into the bottom precisely. Cosine decay to near zero is the usual go-to, and it typically buys a small but real bump in final accuracy for free.

5What to carry forward

  • Never initialize weights to all zeros — identical neurons get identical gradients and stay clones forever (the symmetry problem). Use small random weights.
  • Scale that randomness to preserve variance across layers: $\mathrm{Var}(w)\approx 1/n_\text{in}$. Use He ($2/n_\text{in}$) with ReLU, Xavier ($2/(n_\text{in}+n_\text{out})$) with tanh/sigmoid.
  • The learning rate is your most important knob: too high diverges, too low crawls. Tune it first; a good default is 1e-3 for Adam.
  • Optimizers build up: SGD steps on a cheap noisy minibatch gradient; momentum adds an EMA of past gradients for direction and speed; Adam adds a per-parameter step size ($\sqrt{\hat v}$) plus bias correction.
  • Schedules shrink the learning rate over time — step or cosine decay to settle precisely, with warmup at the start for touchy models like transformers.

You now have every moving part of the training loop: a model, a loss, gradients, a starting point, and a stepping rule.
