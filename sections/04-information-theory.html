Information Theory

By the end of this page you will have derived, not been told, why cross-entropy is the loss function for classification. Every step is shown, and where we skip a proof, we say so.

46 min read
You'll learn

  • why cross-entropy is THE classification loss

Every time a model makes a prediction, we need one number that says how wrong it was. That number is the loss. For classification, the loss is almost always cross-entropy. Many courses give you the formula and move on. We will build it from nothing. We start with a question a child could ask: “why is some news surprising and other news boring?” Step by step, we arrive at the loss function that trains most image classifiers and language models today.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Information is surprise, and surprise can be measured as the length of the message you need to report an event. Entropy is your average surprise. Cross-entropy is your average surprise when you hold the wrong beliefs. The gap between them, the wasted message length, is what a classifier learns to shrink.

1Information: surprise, measured

Intuition first

Start with a plain observation about the news. A headline is big news when it tells you something you did not expect. “Dog bites man” is not a story: it happens often, so you learn almost nothing. “Man bites dog” is a story. It is rare, and because it is rare, it carries a lot of information. So information is not about how important an event feels. It is about how unlikely the event was. The rarer the event, the more you learn when it happens.

☂ Everyday parallel

A friend who says “the sun rose this morning” has told you nothing: probability close to 1, no surprise. A friend who says “it snowed in the desert” has told you a lot: tiny probability, large surprise. Both used a few words, but they delivered very different amounts of information. Information is the gap between what you expected and what happened.

Now the math

We want a number, called information content or surprise. It takes the probability $p(x)$ of an event $x$ and grows as that probability shrinks. Instead of guessing a formula, let's list the properties any sensible measure of surprise must have. Then we see which function they lead to.

  1. Certain events are not surprising. If $p(x)=1$, the surprise must be 0. You learn nothing from something that was guaranteed.
  2. Rarer means more surprising. As $p(x)$ goes down, surprise must go up.
  3. Surprises from independent events add up. Suppose a coin lands heads and a die rolls a six. The total surprise should be the surprise of the first plus the surprise of the second. (“Independent” means that the outcome of one tells you nothing about the other.)

The third rule is the strict one. For independent events, probabilities multiply: $p(\text{both}) = p(x)\,p(y)$. But we want the surprises to add. So we need a function that turns multiplication inside into addition outside. The logarithm does this, because $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$. In fact, it is the only choice: every continuous function with this property is a constant times a logarithm. (We will not prove that here.) So surprise must be a log of the probability. To make it decrease as probability rises (rule 2), we put a minus sign in front:

$$ I(x) \;=\; -\log p(x) \;=\; \log\frac{1}{p(x)} $$

In words: the information in an event is “minus the log of how likely it was.” Probabilities are between 0 and 1, so their log is negative or zero, and the minus sign makes $I(x)$ positive or zero. A sure thing ($p = 1$) gives $-\log 1 = 0$: no surprise. A one-in-a-million event gives a large positive number. The second form reads the same idea another way. An event with probability $1/8$ is as surprising as picking one box out of 8 equally likely boxes. Surprise is the log of that number of equally likely options.

Definition

The information content (also called surprise or surprisal) of an outcome $x$ is $I(x) = -\log p(x)$. When the log has base 2, the unit is bits. When it is the natural log $\ln$ (base $e$), the unit is nats. One bit is the surprise of one fair coin flip.

Why is one fair coin flip worth one bit? Put in $p=\tfrac12$ with a base-2 log: $-\log_2 \tfrac12 = \log_2 2 = 1$. One yes/no answer, one bit. Eight independent coin flips carry $8 \times 1 = 8$ bits. That is why a byte (8 bits) can name $2^8 = 256$ equally likely things.

The same thing in PyTorch

surprise in bits and nats

import torch

# a rare event vs a common one
p_rare   = torch.tensor(1/1000)   # one in a thousand
p_common = torch.tensor(0.9)      # happens most of the time

# base-2 log -> answer in BITS
print(-torch.log2(p_rare))    # tensor(9.9658)  about 10 bits of surprise
print(-torch.log2(p_common))  # tensor(0.1520)  about a seventh of a bit

# natural log -> answer in NATS (what PyTorch losses use)
print(-torch.log(p_rare))     # tensor(6.9078)  same event, different unit

In words: the rare event carries about 66 times more surprise than the common one. The two units differ by a constant factor: $9.9658 / 6.9078 \approx 1.44$. We explain that factor in Section 2.

Check yourself A weather app gives rain tomorrow a probability of $1/8$. How many bits of surprise is rain? And how many bits is “rain on both of the next two days,” if the days are independent? Reveal

$-\log_2(1/8) = \log_2 8 = 3$ bits. One option in 8 is like “which of 8 boxes?”, and 3 yes/no questions find one of 8 boxes (8 → 4 → 2 → 1). Two independent rainy days have probability $1/8 \times 1/8 = 1/64$, and $\log_2 64 = 6$ bits. That is $3 + 3$: the surprises add, as rule 3 demands.

2Entropy: your average surprise

Intuition first

Surprise is defined for a single outcome. But usually we care about a whole distribution. Think of a source that keeps producing outcomes, like a coin you keep flipping or a language that keeps producing words. Entropy answers the question “on average, how surprised will I be by each outcome from this source?” It is the mean surprise, weighted by how often each outcome happens.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of the game 20 Questions. Entropy is the smallest average number of yes/no questions you need to guess the answer, if you choose your questions well. For a fair coin, one question settles it: entropy 1. For one of 1,024 equally likely names, you need 10 questions: entropy 10. For a source that always says “heads,” you need no questions, because you already know: entropy 0. Entropy is uncertainty measured in questions.

Now the math

To average the surprise, we weight each outcome's surprise $-\log p(x)$ by its probability $p(x)$ and add them all. That weighted average is written with the expectation symbol $\mathbb{E}$ from Section 3:

$$ H(p) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log p(x) $$

In words: go through every possible outcome $x$. Take its surprise $-\log p(x)$, multiply by how often it happens, $p(x)$, and add over all outcomes. Common outcomes are not very surprising, but they happen often. Rare outcomes are very surprising, but they happen seldom. Entropy combines the two into one number: the surprise you should expect per draw.

Let's compute it for a coin that lands heads with probability $p$ and tails with $1-p$. There are only two outcomes, so the sum has two terms:

$$ H(p) \;=\; -\,p\log_2 p \;-\; (1-p)\log_2(1-p) $$

Now look at some values of $p$:

Coin$p$ (heads)Entropy $H$ (bits)What it means
Two-headed (rigged)10No uncertainty: you always know the result.
Heavily biased0.10.47Usually tails; little uncertainty.
Fair0.51Maximum uncertainty: a full bit per flip.
Two-tailed (rigged)00No uncertainty again: always tails.

(For $p = 0$ or $p = 1$, one term is $0 \log 0$. We define it as 0, because $p \log p$ goes to 0 as $p$ goes to 0.) Notice the shape. Entropy is zero at both ends: a rigged coin holds no surprise. It is largest in the middle, at $p=0.5$. Uncertainty is highest when the outcomes are equally likely, so you have the least idea what comes next.

We can prove that the peak is at $p=0.5$, instead of reading it from the table. Use the natural log for the calculus, and differentiate $H(p) = -p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p)$ with respect to $p$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dH}{dp} &= -\big(\ln p + 1\big) \;-\; \big(-\ln(1-p) - 1\big) \\[2pt] &= -\ln p - 1 + \ln(1-p) + 1 \\[2pt] &= \ln\frac{1-p}{p}. \end{aligned} $$

In words: the slope of the entropy curve is the log of “tails probability over heads probability.” For $p < 0.5$ the fraction is above 1, so its log is positive: the curve rises. For $p > 0.5$ the fraction is below 1, so the log is negative: the curve falls. The slope is zero only where $\frac{1-p}{p}=1$, that is, at $p=0.5$. A curve that rises and then falls has its top at the flat point. So the fair coin is the most uncertain coin.

Definition

Bits vs nats. Entropy is the same quantity whichever log you use; only the unit changes. Base-2 log gives bits (natural for yes/no questions and file sizes). The natural log gives nats. Since $\log_2 x = \ln x / \ln 2$, a value in nats becomes bits when you divide by $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$. So $1\text{ nat} = 1/\ln 2 \approx 1.44\text{ bits}$. Machine learning code uses nats, because derivatives of $\ln$ are simplest.

The same thing in PyTorch

entropy of a coin for several biases

import math
import torch

def coin_entropy_bits(p):
    # entr(t) = -t * ln(t), with entr(0) = 0 built in; divide by ln 2 for bits
    return (torch.special.entr(p) + torch.special.entr(1 - p)) / math.log(2)

for p in [0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 1.0]:
    print(f"p={p:.1f}  H={coin_entropy_bits(torch.tensor(p)):.3f} bits")
# p=0.0  H=0.000 bits
# p=0.1  H=0.469 bits
# p=0.5  H=1.000 bits
# p=0.9  H=0.469 bits
# p=1.0  H=0.000 bits    -> peak in the middle

In words: torch.special.entr computes $-t\ln t$ and returns 0 at $t = 0$, which follows our convention. A naive version, -(p * torch.log(p)), gives nan at $p = 0$, because $0 \times (-\infty)$ is undefined in floating point.

Check yourself A fair six-sided die has entropy $\log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits. Can a yes/no strategy find the face with 2.585 questions on average? What would make the die's entropy lower? Reveal

Not quite. The best strategy splits the six faces into groups of 3 and 3, then 1 and 2. Two faces need 2 questions and four faces need 3, so the average is $(2+2+3+3+3+3)/6 = 8/3 \approx 2.67$. Entropy is a lower limit on the average; a question-by-question strategy reaches it exactly only when the probabilities are powers of $\tfrac12$. For $k$ equally likely options the entropy is $\log_2 k$, the largest possible. To lower it, make the die unfair, for example a 6 half the time. Then “is it a 6?” is often the only question you need. Predictability always lowers entropy.

3Cross-entropy: surprise under the wrong beliefs

Intuition first

Entropy assumed that you know the true probabilities $p$. A model does not know them. It only has a guess, a predicted distribution we call $q$. Cross-entropy asks: if the world follows $p$, but I plan for it as if it follows $q$, how surprised will I be on average? It is your average surprise when your beliefs are $q$ but reality is $p$.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a code like Morse code, built for English. Common English letters, like E and T, get short signals; rare ones, like Q and Z, get long signals. In the ideal code, a letter with frequency $q$ gets a signal about $-\log_2 q$ bits long. Now use this English code to send Polish text. The letters arrive at Polish frequencies $p$, but each one costs the length the English code gave it, $-\log_2 q$. Your average signal length is the cross-entropy. Polish uses Z often, and each Z costs a long English signal, so the average grows.

Now the math

The formula is the same as for entropy (average the surprise, weighted by how often each outcome happens) with one change. The outcomes still happen at the true rate $p(x)$, because reality decides how often things occur. But the surprise you feel comes from your belief $q(x)$, because that is the probability you gave. So we weight by $p$ but take the log of $q$:

$$ H(p, q) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[-\log q(x)\big] \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log q(x) $$

In words: outcomes arrive at their real rate $p(x)$, but each one surprises you by $-\log q(x)$, the surprise your model gave it. If your guess $q$ matched reality $p$ perfectly, this would be the ordinary entropy $H(p)$. Any mismatch makes it larger. Cross-entropy is never smaller than entropy; we prove this in the next section.

◆ Read the two arguments carefully

The order matters. $H(p,q)$ weights by the first argument $p$ (what is true). It takes the log of the second argument $q$ (what you believe). Swap them and you get a different number. A short way to remember it: “weighted by truth, surprised by belief.”

The same thing in PyTorch

cross-entropy between a true and a predicted distribution

import torch

# true distribution p over 3 classes, and two guesses q
p     = torch.tensor([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])   # reality
q_ok  = torch.tensor([0.6, 0.3, 0.1])   # a decent guess
q_bad = torch.tensor([0.1, 0.2, 0.7])   # confidently wrong

def cross_entropy(p, q):
    return -(p * torch.log(q)).sum()    # nats

print(cross_entropy(p, p))      # tensor(0.8018)  = H(p), the lowest possible
print(cross_entropy(p, q_ok))   # tensor(0.8286)  a little above it
print(cross_entropy(p, q_bad))  # tensor(1.9694)  far above -> bad model

In words: the function multiplies each true probability by the log of the guessed one and adds the results. The better the guess, the closer the result is to $H(p)$.

Check yourself In q_bad, the model gives the first class only 0.1, though it really happens 70% of the time. Which single term of the sum contributes most to the cross-entropy? Estimate it. Reveal

The first term: $-0.7 \ln 0.1 = 0.7 \times 2.303 \approx 1.61$ nats. The other two terms add only $-0.2\ln 0.2 - 0.1\ln 0.7 \approx 0.32 + 0.04 = 0.36$. Total: about 1.97, as the code prints. Most of the cost comes from a common outcome that the model thought was rare. Cross-entropy punishes that kind of mistake most.

4KL divergence: the extra cost of being wrong

Intuition first

Cross-entropy $H(p,q)$ is your total surprise with a wrong belief. Entropy $H(p)$ is the surprise you cannot avoid, even with a perfect belief. The difference is the part you could have avoided: the waste caused by believing $q$ instead of $p$. That waste is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, written $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)$.

☂ Everyday parallel

Back to the English code used for Polish. A code built for Polish would send Polish text with an average length of $H(p)$. That is the shortest possible. The English code needs $H(p,q)$. The KL divergence is the extra length per letter that you pay only because the code was built for the wrong language. The asymmetry is also clear here. Sending Polish with an English code wastes a different amount than sending English with a Polish code.

Now the math

By definition, it is the gap between cross-entropy and entropy. Subtract, and the two sums combine term by term into one sum:

$$ \begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) &= H(p,q) - H(p) \\[2pt] &= \Big(-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)\Big) - \Big(-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\Big) \\[2pt] &= \sum_x p(x)\,\big(\log p(x) - \log q(x)\big) \\[2pt] &= \sum_x p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}. \end{aligned} $$

In words: for each outcome, look at the ratio of what is true to what you believed, $p(x)/q(x)$. Take its log, weight it by how often the outcome happens, $p(x)$, and add. Where your belief matched reality, the ratio is 1 and its log is 0: no penalty.

Why KL divergence is never negative (Gibbs' inequality)

We keep saying “cross-entropy ≥ entropy” and “the extra cost is never negative.” Let's prove it, because the whole story depends on it. The claim is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\ge 0$, known as Gibbs' inequality. We work in nats, with the natural log $\ln$. (In bits, every log is divided by the positive constant $\ln 2$, which does not change the sign.)

We need one fact about $\ln$: $\ln t \le t - 1$ for all $t\gt 0$. The line $t-1$ is the tangent to $\ln t$ at $t=1$. The curve $\ln t$ is concave (it bends downward), so it never rises above its tangent line. This fact is true only for the natural log. For example, $\log_2 1.5 \approx 0.585$ is larger than $1.5 - 1 = 0.5$.

Now apply the fact to each term:

$$ \begin{aligned} -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) &= \sum_x p(x)\,\ln\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} &&\text{(flip the ratio; the minus sign flips the log)}\\[4pt] &\le \sum_x p(x)\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1\right) &&\text{(use } \ln t \le t-1 \text{ with } t=\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)})\\[4pt] &= \sum_x \big(q(x) - p(x)\big) \\[4pt] &= \sum_x q(x) - \sum_x p(x) \;=\; 1 - 1 \;=\; 0. \end{aligned} $$

In words: we showed $-D_{\mathrm{KL}} \le 0$, which is the same as $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$. The last line uses the one thing we know for sure about probability distributions: each one adds up to 1. (The sums run over outcomes with $p(x) > 0$. Skipping the others can only make $\sum q$ smaller than 1, so the result still holds.) So the extra cost of a wrong belief is always zero or positive, never a bonus. Equality ($D_{\mathrm{KL}}=0$) needs $\ln t = t-1$ in every term. That happens only at $t=1$, so only when $q(x)=p(x)$ everywhere.

◆ Why this matters for training

Gibbs' inequality is why minimizing cross-entropy is a sensible goal. We have $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)$, and $H(p)$ is a constant that the model cannot change. So pushing cross-entropy down is the same as pushing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ down. That moves your predicted distribution $q$ toward the truth $p$. The lowest value is zero, reached only when $q=p$.

▲ Watch out: KL is not a distance

KL divergence is asymmetric: in general, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)$. The cost of using the wrong code depends on which language is really being sent. So do not call KL a “distance.” A true distance is symmetric, and KL is not. It measures “extra surprise if the truth is $p$ and you planned for $q$.”

The same thing in PyTorch

KL is the gap, and it is asymmetric

import torch

p = torch.tensor([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])
q = torch.tensor([0.4, 0.4, 0.2])

def H(p, q=None):                       # entropy if q is None, else cross-entropy
    q = p if q is None else q
    return -(p * torch.log(q)).sum()

kl_pq = H(p, q) - H(p)                  # extra cost if truth is p, belief is q
kl_qp = H(q, p) - H(q)                  # the reversed question

print(kl_pq)   # tensor(0.1838)
print(kl_qp)   # tensor(0.1920)  -> different number: KL is asymmetric
print(kl_pq >= 0, kl_qp >= 0)  # tensor(True) tensor(True) -> Gibbs holds

In words: the code computes KL as “cross-entropy minus entropy,” in both directions. Here $H(p) = 0.8018$ and $H(p,q) = 0.9856$, the numbers in Figure 4.3.

Check yourself A model's prediction $q$ equals the truth $p$. What are $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)$ and $H(p,q)$? Can the cross-entropy loss ever reach zero when the source is truly uncertain? Reveal

When $q = p$: $D_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0$ (no waste), so $H(p,q) = H(p) + 0 = H(p)$. The cross-entropy can go no lower than the source's own entropy. So its lowest value is $H(p)$, not zero. For a truly uncertain source ($H(p) > 0$), the loss never reaches zero, however good the model is. Some surprise cannot be removed. The loss can reach zero only when the truth itself is certain (one-hot). That is the classification case we turn to now.

5Why cross-entropy is the classification loss

Intuition first

Everything above was general. Now apply it to classification: putting an input into one of several categories (is this image a cat, a dog, or a bird?). Here the truth is not uncertain: each training example belongs to exactly one class. This one fact makes cross-entropy very simple. It shows that “minimize cross-entropy” and “make the correct answer as likely as possible” are the same instruction.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine a multiple-choice test where students must give a probability for each answer, and only one answer is correct. The grader looks only at the probability the student put on the right answer. “100% sure, and correct” costs nothing. “5% on the correct answer” costs a lot. “0% on the correct answer” costs without limit: the penalty $-\log q$ has no upper bound. Cross-entropy grades this way.

Now the math

In classification, the true distribution $p$ for one example is one-hot. It puts probability 1 on the correct class $c$ and 0 on every other class: $p(c)=1$, and $p(x)=0$ for $x\ne c$. (“One-hot” means a vector of zeros with a single 1 that marks the right answer.) Put that into the cross-entropy sum, and every term but one disappears:

$$ \begin{aligned} H(p, q) &= -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log q(x) \\[2pt] &= -\Big[\,p(c)\log q(c) \;+\!\!\sum_{x\ne c} p(x)\log q(x)\Big] \\[2pt] &= -\Big[\,1\cdot\log q(c) \;+\!\!\sum_{x\ne c} 0\cdot\log q(x)\Big] \\[2pt] &= -\log q(c). \end{aligned} $$

In words: the truth is 1 for the correct class and 0 everywhere else. So every wrong-class term is multiplied by 0 and disappears. One term remains: minus the log of the probability your model gave the correct class. The whole cross-entropy of a labeled example is $-\log q(\text{correct class})$: the surprise of the right answer under your model.

◆ Cross-entropy = negative log-likelihood

$-\log q(c)$ is the negative log-likelihood of the correct label. The likelihood is how probable the model thinks the true answer is, and the loss is minus its log. So minimizing cross-entropy is maximizing the likelihood of the correct answers. That is the principle of maximum likelihood estimation from Section 3. Two ideas that looked unrelated, “shortest code” from information theory and “most probable model” from statistics, lead to the same loss.

Take a dataset of $N$ independent examples. Their likelihoods multiply, so their log-likelihoods add. The training loss is the average negative log-likelihood, one cross-entropy term per example:

$$ L \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log q_i(c_i) $$

In words: for each training example $i$, take the model's probability on that example's true class $c_i$. Take its negative log (the surprise), and average over all $N$ examples. When you lower this number, the model becomes less surprised by the right answers, example by example.

Where do the probabilities $q$ come from? The softmax

A neural network's last layer does not output probabilities. It outputs a vector of raw real numbers called logits: one score per class, which can be negative, large, anything. To turn logits $z_1,\dots,z_K$ (for $K$ classes) into a valid probability distribution $q$, we use the softmax function. It exponentiates each score (making it positive) and divides by the total (making the set add up to 1):

$$ q_k \;=\; \operatorname{softmax}(z)_k \;=\; \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} $$

In words: $e^{z_k}$ makes every score positive and makes the gaps larger, so the largest logit dominates. Dividing by the sum turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 1. The class with the largest logit gets the largest probability. Softmax connects the raw network scores to a distribution $q$ that cross-entropy can use.

Put softmax into the loss, and the log cancels the exponential:

$$ -\log q_c \;=\; -z_c + \log \sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j} $$

In words: the loss needs only the logits, never the probabilities themselves. The second term can be computed safely even for very large logits, by subtracting the largest $z_j$ before exponentiating. This is why PyTorch computes the two steps together.

◆ Softmax + cross-entropy: the standard pair

Feed the network's logits through softmax to get $q$, then score $q$ with cross-entropy $-\log q(c)$. This pair is the standard output of classifiers. PyTorch combines both steps in one function, nn.CrossEntropyLoss (or F.cross_entropy), which takes the raw logits directly. You almost never apply softmax yourself before the loss; the loss does it for you, safely.

The same thing in PyTorch

from raw logits to the classification loss, two equivalent ways

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# a batch of 2 examples, 3 classes; these are raw LOGITS from a network
logits = torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.5, -1.0],    # example 0
                       [0.1, 0.2,  3.0]])   # example 1
targets = torch.tensor([0, 2])              # true classes 0 and 2 (given as indices, not one-hot)

# --- by hand: softmax -> pick correct class -> -log -> average ---
q = F.softmax(logits, dim=1)                # logits -> probabilities per row
p_correct = q[range(2), targets]            # q given to each true class
manual = (-torch.log(p_correct)).mean()     # mean negative log-likelihood
print(manual)                               # tensor(0.1755)

# --- the usual way: one combined, numerically stable call on RAW logits ---
builtin = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)  # softmax + negative log-likelihood
print(builtin)                              # tensor(0.1755)  -> identical

In words: the first half follows our derivation step by step. The second half gets the same number from one call. In real code, use the second.

▲ Watch out: the double-softmax bug

nn.CrossEntropyLoss and F.cross_entropy apply softmax internally. If you also call softmax on the model's output first, you apply softmax twice. That squashes the probabilities toward uniform, so the model trains slowly and badly. Rule: give the loss raw logits, never probabilities.

Check yourself Your classifier puts probability $0.9$ on the correct class for one image and $0.1$ for another. Predict which image adds more to the loss, and by how much (in nats). What happens as the second probability goes toward 0? Reveal

The loss per example is $-\ln q_{\text{correct}}$. First image: $-\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$ nats. Second image: $-\ln 0.1 \approx 2.303$ nats, about 22 times more. As the probability on the correct class goes toward 0, $-\ln q$ grows without limit. So cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes very strongly, and this pushes the model to stop being confidently wrong. The same loss, applied to “predict the next word,” trains language models; see Section 19.

6What to carry forward

  • Information (surprise) is $-\log p(x)$. Rare events carry more, and the log makes surprises from independent events add up.
  • Entropy $H(p) = -\sum p\log p$ is your average surprise. It is the lower limit on the average number of yes/no questions, and it is largest when outcomes are equally likely.
  • Cross-entropy $H(p,q) = -\sum p\log q$ is your average surprise when reality is $p$ but you believe $q$: weighted by truth, surprised by belief.
  • KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \ge 0$ is the extra cost of a wrong belief (Gibbs' inequality, proved with $\ln t \le t-1$). It is asymmetric, so it is not a distance.
  • The main result: for one-hot labels, cross-entropy becomes $-\log q(\text{correct class})$, the negative log-likelihood. So minimizing it is maximum likelihood (Section 3). Softmax turns logits into $q$. Cross-entropy returns in binary form in Section 6, and as the softmax + cross-entropy pair in Section 11 and Section 19.

You now have the loss function that trains most classifiers. And you derived it, from “why is snow in the desert surprising?” all the way to F.cross_entropy. Next we build our first full model, linear regression, and use the gradient from Section 2 to lower a real loss from start to finish.
