Information Theory

By the end of this page you will have derived, not been told, why cross-entropy is the loss function for classification. Every step is shown; nothing is asserted.

26 min read You'll learn: information & surprise · entropy · cross-entropy · KL divergence · why cross-entropy is THE classification loss

Every time a model makes a prediction, we need a single number that says how wrong it was. That number is the loss, and for classification the loss is almost always cross-entropy. Most courses hand you the formula and move on. We are going to build it from nothing — starting with a question a child could ask, “why is some news surprising and other news boring?” — and end up, honestly and step by step, at the exact loss function that trains every image classifier and every language model on Earth.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Information is surprise, and surprise is measured in the length of the message you'd need to announce an event. Entropy is your average surprise, cross-entropy is your average surprise when you trained on the wrong beliefs, and the gap between them — the wasted effort — is exactly what a classifier learns to shrink.

1Information: surprise, measured

Intuition first

Start with a plain observation about the news. A headline is “big news” exactly when it tells you something you did not expect. “Dog bites man” is not a story — dogs bite, it happens all the time, you learned almost nothing. “Man bites dog” is a story — it is rare, and precisely because it is rare it carries a lot of information. So information is not about how important an event feels; it is about how unlikely it was. The rarer the event, the more you learn when it happens.

☂ Everyday parallel

A friend who says “the sun rose this morning” has told you nothing — probability ≈ 1, zero surprise. A friend who says “it snowed in the desert” has told you a lot — tiny probability, huge surprise. Same number of words spoken, wildly different amount of information delivered. Information is the gap between what you expected and what happened.

Now the math

We want a number — call it information content, or surprise — that takes the probability $p(x)$ of an event $x$ and grows as that probability shrinks. Let's not pull a formula out of a hat; let's demand the properties any honest measure of surprise must have, then see what function is forced on us.

  1. Certain events are boring. If $p(x)=1$, the surprise must be exactly 0. You learn nothing from what was guaranteed.
  2. Rarer means more surprising. As $p(x)$ goes down, surprise must go up. The measure decreases with probability.
  3. Independent surprises add up. If two unrelated things happen — a coin lands heads and a die rolls a six — the total surprise should be the surprise of the first plus the surprise of the second. (Two independent events. “Independent” means the outcome of one tells you nothing about the other.)

That third rule is the strict one. For independent events, probabilities multiply: $p(\text{both}) = p(x)\,p(y)$. But we want the surprises to add. We need a function that turns multiplication on the inside into addition on the outside. There is exactly one family of functions that does that — the logarithm, because $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$. So the surprise must be a log of the probability. To make it decrease as probability rises (rule 2) and be non-negative, we put a minus sign in front:

$$ I(x) \;=\; -\log p(x) \;=\; \log\frac{1}{p(x)} $$

In words: the information in an event is “minus the log of how likely it was.” Because probabilities are between 0 and 1, their log is negative, so the minus sign makes I(x) positive. A sure thing (p = 1) gives $-\log 1 = 0$ — no surprise. A one-in-a-million event gives a big positive number. And the two equal forms show the same idea two ways: surprise is the log of “one over the probability,” i.e. the log of how many equally-likely possibilities you just ruled out.

Definition

The information content (also called surprise or surprisal) of an outcome $x$ is $I(x) = -\log p(x)$. When the log is base 2 the unit is bits; when it is the natural log (base $e$) the unit is nats. One bit is the surprise of a single fair coin flip.

Why is one fair coin flip worth exactly one bit? Plug in $p=\tfrac12$ with base-2 log: $-\log_2 \tfrac12 = \log_2 2 = 1$. One yes/no answer, one bit. Eight independent coin flips carry $8 \times 1 = 8$ bits — which is why a byte (8 bits) can name exactly $2^8 = 256$ equally likely things.

The same thing in PyTorch

surprise in bits and nats

import torch

# a rare event vs a common one
p_rare   = torch.tensor(1/1000)   # one-in-a-thousand
p_common = torch.tensor(0.9)      # happens most of the time

# base-2 log -> answer in BITS
print(-torch.log2(p_rare))    # tensor(9.9658)  ~10 bits of surprise
print(-torch.log2(p_common))  # tensor(0.1520)  barely a fifth of a bit

# natural log -> answer in NATS (what PyTorch losses use internally)
print(-torch.log(p_rare))     # tensor(6.9078)  same event, different unit
Check yourself A weather app says tomorrow's rain has probability 1/8. In bits, how surprised should you be if it rains — and why does that number feel like “how many yes/no questions”? Reveal

-log₂(1/8) = log₂(8) = 3 bits. The reason 3 feels like three yes/no questions: with equally-likely options, each yes/no question cuts the possibilities in half. One-in-eight is the same as “which of 8 boxes?”, and you can pin down one of 8 boxes with exactly 3 halvings (8 → 4 → 2 → 1). Surprise in bits is the number of ideal yes/no questions the event's outcome answers.

2Entropy: your average surprise

Intuition first

Surprise is defined for a single outcome. But usually we care about a whole distribution — a source that keeps producing outcomes, like a coin you keep flipping or a language that keeps emitting words. Entropy is the answer to: “on average, how surprised will I be per outcome from this source?” It is the mean surprise, weighted by how often each outcome actually happens.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of the game 20 Questions. Entropy is the average number of yes/no questions you need to guess the answer. If I'm thinking of a fair coin's result, one question settles it — entropy 1. If I'm thinking of one of 1,024 equally likely names, you need about 10 questions — entropy 10. A predictable source (I always pick “heads”) needs zero questions; you already know. Entropy is uncertainty measured in questions.

Now the math

To average the surprise, we weight each outcome's surprise $-\log p(x)$ by its own probability $p(x)$ and add them all up. That weighted average is written with the expectation symbol $\mathbb{E}$ (“expected value” = long-run average):

$$ H(p) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log p(x) $$

In words: go through every possible outcome x; take its surprise $-\log p(x)$; multiply by how often it happens, p(x); sum over all outcomes. Common outcomes are surprising rarely but counted often; rare outcomes are very surprising but counted seldom. Entropy balances the two into one number: the surprise you should expect per draw.

Let's actually compute it for a coin that lands heads with probability $p$ (and tails with $1-p$). There are only two outcomes, so the sum has two terms:

$$ H(p) \;=\; -\,p\log_2 p \;-\; (1-p)\log_2(1-p) $$

Now read off the extremes, which is where the meaning lives:

Coin$p$ (heads)Entropy $H$ (bits)What it means
Two-headed (rigged)10No uncertainty — you always know the result.
Heavily biased0.10.47Usually tails; little uncertainty.
Fair0.51Maximum uncertainty — a full bit per flip.
Two-tailed (rigged)00No uncertainty again — always tails.

Notice the shape: entropy is zero at both ends (a rigged coin holds no surprise) and largest exactly in the middle, at $p=0.5$. Uncertainty peaks when the outcomes are equally likely and you have the least idea what's coming.

We can show that the peak sits at $p=0.5$ rather than just eyeball the table. Using the natural log for the calculus and differentiating $H(p) = -p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p)$ with respect to $p$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dH}{dp} &= -\big(\ln p + 1\big) \;-\; \big(-\ln(1-p) - 1\big) \\[2pt] &= -\ln p - 1 + \ln(1-p) + 1 \\[2pt] &= \ln\frac{1-p}{p}. \end{aligned} $$

In words: the slope of the entropy curve is the log of “tails-odds over heads-odds.” Set the slope to 0 to find the top of the hill: $\ln\frac{1-p}{p}=0$ means $\frac{1-p}{p}=1$, i.e. $1-p=p$, i.e. $p=0.5$. The single flat point of the curve is dead centre — proving the fair coin is the most uncertain coin there is.

Definition

Bits vs nats. Entropy is the same quantity whichever log you use; only the unit changes. Base-2 log gives bits (natural for “yes/no questions” and file sizes); natural log gives nats. They differ only by a constant factor ($1\text{ nat} \approx 1.44\text{ bits}$). Machine learning code uses nats, because the natural log is the one calculus likes.

The same thing in PyTorch

entropy of a coin across all biases

import torch

def coin_entropy_bits(p):
    # clamp keeps log(0) from blowing up; 0*log0 is defined as 0
    p = torch.clamp(p, 1e-9, 1 - 1e-9)
    return -(p * torch.log2(p) + (1 - p) * torch.log2(1 - p))

for p in [0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 1.0]:
    print(f"p={p:.1f}  H={coin_entropy_bits(torch.tensor(p)):.3f} bits")
# p=0.0  H=0.000   p=0.1  H=0.469   p=0.5  H=1.000
# p=0.9  H=0.469   p=1.0  H=0.000    -> peak in the middle
Check yourself A fair six-sided die has entropy log₂(6) ≈ 2.585 bits. Why is that more than the fair coin's 1 bit, and what would drop the die's entropy below that? Reveal

The die has six equally likely outcomes instead of two, so you need more yes/no questions to pin one down — about 2.585 on average — hence higher entropy. Entropy is maximised when all outcomes are equally likely: for k equal options it is exactly log₂(k). To lower the die's entropy you'd make it unfair — say it lands on 6 half the time — because now you can guess “6?” first and often be right, so fewer questions are needed on average. Predictability always lowers entropy.

3Cross-entropy: surprise under the wrong beliefs

Intuition first

Entropy assumed you knew the true probabilities $p$. But a model doesn't know them — it only has a guess, a predicted distribution we'll call $q$. Cross-entropy asks the honest question: if the world really runs on $p$, but I plan for it as if it runs on $q$, how surprised will I be on average? It is your average surprise when your beliefs are $q$ but reality is $p$.

☂ Everyday parallel

You travel to a country and pack the wrong phrasebook — it's for a neighbouring language. You can still get by: many phrases overlap, so you're understood often enough. But every mismatch costs you extra effort — repeating yourself, pointing, guessing. The true language is $p$; your phrasebook is $q$. Cross-entropy is the total effort you spend speaking reality through the wrong book. Pack a better-matched book (a better $q$) and the daily effort drops.

Now the math

The recipe is the same as entropy — average the surprise, weighted by how often each outcome happens — with one change. The outcomes still happen with the true frequency $p(x)$ (reality decides how often things occur). But the surprise you actually feel is computed from your belief $q(x)$, because that's the probability you assigned. So we weight by $p$ but take the log of $q$:

$$ H(p, q) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[-\log q(x)\big] \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log q(x) $$

In words: outcomes arrive at their real rate p(x), but each one startles you by $-\log q(x)$ — the surprise your model assigned it. If your guess q matched reality p perfectly, this would collapse back into ordinary entropy $H(p)$. Any mismatch makes it bigger. Cross-entropy is never smaller than entropy — a fact we prove in the next section.

◆ Read the two arguments carefully

The order matters: $H(p,q)$ weights by the first argument $p$ (what's true) and takes the log of the second argument $q$ (what you believe). Swap them and you get a different number. Keep saying it to yourself: “weighted by truth, surprised by belief.”

The same thing in PyTorch

cross-entropy between a true and a predicted distribution

import torch

# true distribution p over 3 classes, and two guesses q
p     = torch.tensor([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])   # reality
q_ok  = torch.tensor([0.6, 0.3, 0.1])   # a decent guess
q_bad = torch.tensor([0.1, 0.2, 0.7])   # confidently wrong

def cross_entropy(p, q):
    return -(p * torch.log(q)).sum()    # nats

print(cross_entropy(p, p))      # tensor(0.8018)  = H(p), the floor
print(cross_entropy(p, q_ok))   # tensor(0.8286)  a little above the floor
print(cross_entropy(p, q_bad))  # tensor(1.9694)  far above -> bad model
Check yourself In the code above, cross_entropy(p, p) gave the smallest value of the three. Why can no guess q ever beat using the true p itself? Reveal

Because the least-surprising code for a source is the one built from its true probabilities — that's what entropy H(p) is, the theoretical minimum average surprise. Any other belief q assigns at least one outcome a probability that's too low, and you pay for that with extra surprise when it happens. So H(p, q) ≥ H(p) always, with equality only when q = p. The exact proof of this inequality is the whole point of the next section.

4KL divergence: the extra cost of being wrong

Intuition first

Cross-entropy $H(p,q)$ is your total surprise with a wrong belief. Entropy $H(p)$ is the unavoidable surprise even with a perfect belief. The difference is the part you could have avoided — the pure waste caused by believing $q$ instead of $p$. That waste is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, written $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)$.

☂ Everyday parallel

Back to the phrasebook. Cross-entropy is the total effort you spend communicating. Entropy is the effort a fluent local still spends — language is never effortless, some work is irreducible. KL divergence is the extra effort you burn purely because your phrasebook is wrong: the repeats, the pointing, the confused looks. Get the right book and KL drops to zero; you're down to the irreducible effort and not a jot more.

Now the math

By definition it is the gap between cross-entropy and entropy. Subtract, and the two sums combine termwise into a single clean sum:

$$ \begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) &= H(p,q) - H(p) \\[2pt] &= \Big(-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)\Big) - \Big(-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\Big) \\[2pt] &= \sum_x p(x)\,\big(\log p(x) - \log q(x)\big) \\[2pt] &= \sum_x p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}. \end{aligned} $$

In words: for each outcome, look at the ratio of what's true to what you believed, $p(x)/q(x)$; take its log; weight by how often the outcome happens, p(x); sum up. Where your belief matched reality the ratio is 1 and its log is 0 — no penalty. Wherever you were wrong the ratio strays from 1 and adds a positive cost.

Why KL divergence is never negative (Gibbs' inequality)

We keep claiming “cross-entropy ≥ entropy” and “the extra cost is never negative.” Let's actually prove it, because the whole story hangs on it. The claim is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\ge 0$, known as Gibbs' inequality. The clean proof uses one fact about the logarithm and one fact about averages.

The fact about the logarithm: $\log$ is a concave function — it bulges upward, so any straight chord between two points on the curve lies below the curve. A concrete consequence we'll use is the simple bound $\log t \le t - 1$ for all $t\gt 0$ (the line $t-1$ is the tangent to $\log$ at $t=1$, and a concave curve never rises above its tangent). Now watch:

$$ \begin{aligned} -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) &= \sum_x p(x)\,\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} &&\text{(flip the ratio; the minus sign flips the log)}\\[4pt] &\le \sum_x p(x)\left(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1\right) &&\text{(apply } \log t \le t-1 \text{ with } t=\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)})\\[4pt] &= \sum_x \big(q(x) - p(x)\big) \\[4pt] &= \sum_x q(x) - \sum_x p(x) \;=\; 1 - 1 \;=\; 0. \end{aligned} $$

In words: we showed $-D_{\mathrm{KL}} \le 0$, which is the same as $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$. The last line used the only thing we know for sure about probability distributions: they each sum to 1. So the extra cost of a wrong belief is always zero or positive — never a bonus. And equality ($D_{\mathrm{KL}}=0$) holds only when the bound $\log t = t-1$ is tight for every term, which happens only at $t=1$, i.e. only when $q(x)=p(x)$ everywhere. Belief matches reality exactly, or you pay.

◆ This is the pillar

Gibbs' inequality is why minimising cross-entropy is a sensible goal. Since $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)$ and $H(p)$ is a fixed constant the model cannot change, pushing cross-entropy down is exactly pushing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ down — dragging your predicted distribution $q$ toward the truth $p$. The lowest it can go is zero, reached only when $q=p$.

▲ Watch out: KL is not a distance

KL divergence is asymmetric: in general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)$. The cost of using the wrong phrasebook depends on which language is actually being spoken. So never call KL a “distance” — a real distance is symmetric, and this is not. It's a directed measure of “extra surprise if the truth is $p$ and you planned for $q$.”

The same thing in PyTorch

KL is the gap, and it is asymmetric

import torch

p = torch.tensor([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])
q = torch.tensor([0.4, 0.4, 0.2])

def H(p, q=None):                       # entropy if q is None, else cross-entropy
    q = p if q is None else q
    return -(p * torch.log(q)).sum()

kl_pq = H(p, q) - H(p)                  # extra cost if truth is p, belief is q
kl_qp = H(q, p) - H(q)                  # the reversed question

print(kl_pq)   # tensor(0.1838)
print(kl_qp)   # tensor(0.1920)  -> different number: KL is asymmetric
print(kl_pq >= 0, kl_qp >= 0)  # tensor(True) tensor(True) -> Gibbs holds
Check yourself If a model's predicted distribution q exactly equals the truth p, what are the values of H(p,q), D_KL(p‖q), and the training loss — and can the loss ever reach zero for a genuinely uncertain source? Reveal

When q = p: D_KL(p‖q) = 0 (no wasted effort), so H(p,q) = H(p) + 0 = H(p) — cross-entropy bottoms out at the source's own entropy. The loss here is the cross-entropy, so its floor is H(p), not zero. For a genuinely uncertain source (H(p) > 0) the loss can never hit zero no matter how perfect the model — some surprise is irreducible. It reaches zero only when the truth itself is certain (one-hot), which is exactly the classification case we turn to now.

5The punchline: why cross-entropy is THE classification loss

Intuition first

Everything above was general. Now specialise it to classification — the task of putting an input into one of several categories (is this image a cat, a dog, or a bird?). Here the truth is not fuzzy: each training example belongs to exactly one class. That single fact collapses cross-entropy into something startlingly simple, and reveals that “minimise cross-entropy” and “make the correct answer as likely as possible” are the very same instruction.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine grading a multiple-choice test where only one answer is correct. You don't care how confident the student was about the wrong options — you only care how much probability they put on the right one. Full marks for “100% sure, and correct.” Harsh penalty for “only 5% on the correct answer.” Cross-entropy is exactly this grader: it ignores the wrong boxes and scores you purely on the confidence you placed on the true one.

Now the math — the collapse

In classification the true distribution $p$ for a single example is one-hot: it puts probability 1 on the correct class $c$ and 0 on every other class. That is, $p(c)=1$ and $p(x)=0$ for $x\ne c$. (“One-hot” = a vector that is all zeros except a single 1 marking the right answer.) Substitute that into the cross-entropy sum and watch every term but one vanish:

$$ \begin{aligned} H(p, q) &= -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log q(x) \\[2pt] &= -\Big[\,p(c)\log q(c) \;+\!\!\sum_{x\ne c} p(x)\log q(x)\Big] \\[2pt] &= -\Big[\,1\cdot\log q(c) \;+\!\!\sum_{x\ne c} 0\cdot\log q(x)\Big] \\[2pt] &= -\log q(c). \end{aligned} $$

In words: because the truth is 1 for the correct class and 0 everywhere else, every wrong-class term is multiplied by 0 and disappears. What survives is a single term: minus the log of the probability your model assigned to the correct class. That's it. The whole cross-entropy of a labelled example is just $-\log q(\text{correct class})$ — the surprise of the right answer under your model.

◆ Cross-entropy = negative log-likelihood

$-\log q(c)$ is precisely the negative log-likelihood of the correct label. The likelihood is how probable the model thinks the true answer is; the loss is minus its log. So minimising cross-entropy is maximising the likelihood of the correct answers — the very principle of Maximum Likelihood Estimation we met in Section 3. Two ideas that looked unrelated — “shortest code” from information theory and “most probable model” from statistics — turn out to be the same loss.

Over a whole dataset of $N$ independent examples, the likelihoods multiply, so the log-likelihoods add, and the training loss is the average negative log-likelihood — one cross-entropy term per example:

$$ L \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log q_i(c_i) $$

In words: for each training example $i$ take the model's probability on that example's true class $c_i$, take its negative log (the surprise), and average over all N examples. Drive this down and you are, example by example, making the model less surprised by the right answers.

Where do the probabilities $q$ come from? The softmax

A neural network's last layer doesn't output probabilities — it outputs a vector of raw, unbounded real numbers called logits (one score per class; they can be negative, huge, anything). To turn logits $z_1,\dots,z_K$ into a valid probability distribution $q$, we pass them through the softmax function — exponentiate each score (making it positive) and divide by the total (making the whole set sum to 1):

$$ q_k \;=\; \operatorname{softmax}(z)_k \;=\; \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} $$

In words: $e^{z_k}$ makes every score positive and blows up the gaps so the largest logit dominates; dividing by the sum normalises them into probabilities that add to 1. The class with the biggest logit gets the biggest probability. Softmax is the bridge from “raw network scores” to “a distribution $q$ you can feed to cross-entropy.”

◆ Softmax + cross-entropy: the standard pairing

Feed the network's logits through softmax to get $q$, then score $q$ with cross-entropy $-\log q(c)$. This softmax-then-cross-entropy pair is the default output of virtually every classifier. It's so common — and so much more numerically stable when fused — that PyTorch bundles both into a single function, nn.CrossEntropyLoss, which takes the raw logits directly. You almost never apply softmax by hand before the loss; the loss does it for you, safely.

The same thing in PyTorch

from raw logits to the classification loss, two equivalent ways

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# a batch of 2 examples, 3 classes; these are raw LOGITS from a network
logits = torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.5, -1.0],    # example 0
                       [0.1, 0.2,  3.0]])   # example 1
targets = torch.tensor([0, 2])              # true classes: 0 and 2 (one-hot, given as index)

# --- the manual way: softmax -> pick correct class -> -log -> average ---
q = F.softmax(logits, dim=1)                # logits -> probabilities per row
p_correct = q[range(2), targets]            # q assigned to each true class
manual = (-torch.log(p_correct)).mean()     # mean negative log-likelihood
print(manual)                               # tensor(0.1755)

# --- the idiomatic way: one fused, numerically-stable call on RAW logits ---
builtin = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)  # softmax + NLL, fused
print(builtin)                              # tensor(0.1755)  -> identical
▲ The classic double-softmax bug

nn.CrossEntropyLoss / F.cross_entropy apply softmax internally. If you also call softmax on your model's output first, you softmax twice — squashing the logits toward uniform, so the model trains slowly and badly. Rule: feed raw logits to the loss, never pre-softmaxed probabilities.

Check yourself Your classifier puts probability 0.9 on the correct class for one image and 0.1 on the correct class for another. Which image contributes more to the loss, by how much (in nats), and what does that say about how cross-entropy treats confident mistakes? Reveal

Loss per example is -log(q_correct). For the first: -log(0.9) ≈ 0.105 nats. For the second: -log(0.1) ≈ 2.303 nats. The second image contributes about 2.303 / 0.105 ≈ 22× more loss. Because -log shoots toward infinity as the correct-class probability approaches 0, cross-entropy punishes confident wrongness savagely: putting almost no probability on the true answer is enormously costly, which is exactly what pushes the model to stop being confidently wrong. This same loss, applied to “predict the next word,” is what trains language models — see Section 19.

6What to carry forward

  • Information / surprise is $-\log p(x)$: rare events carry more, and the log makes independent surprises add up.
  • Entropy $H(p) = -\sum p\log p$ is your average surprise — uncertainty in “yes/no questions,” maximal when outcomes are equally likely.
  • Cross-entropy $H(p,q) = -\sum p\log q$ is your average surprise when reality is $p$ but you believed $q$: weighted by truth, surprised by belief.
  • KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \ge 0$ is the pure extra cost of a wrong belief (Gibbs' inequality, proved via $\log t \le t-1$) — and it is asymmetric, so not a distance.
  • The punchline: for one-hot labels cross-entropy collapses to $-\log q(\text{correct class})$ = negative log-likelihood, so minimising it is maximum likelihood (Section 3). Softmax turns logits into $q$; cross-entropy reappears in its binary form in Section 6, and the full softmax + cross-entropy pair in Section 11 and Section 19.

You now hold the loss function that trains nearly every classifier ever built — and you derived it, from “why is snow in the desert surprising?” all the way to F.cross_entropy.
