Linear Algebra for ML

A neural network is a stack of vectors pushed through matrices. Learn to read that sentence, and you can read most of ML.

28 min read
You'll learn
  • vectors
  • dot products
  • matrices as transformations
  • norms
  • eigenvalues

Every piece of data you feed a model first becomes a list of numbers. This is true for an image, a sentence, or a row in a spreadsheet. Linear algebra is the set of rules for working with those lists efficiently. It is the grammar of machine learning. The good news: you already have the intuition. We only need to attach the notation to it.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Data becomes vectors. Operations on data become matrices. Learning is the search for the right numbers to put inside those matrices. The rest of this lesson fills in the details.

1Vectors: a point, and an arrow

Intuition first

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. That is the plain definition. The more useful picture: a vector is an arrow in space. It points from the origin (the center, position zero) to some spot. The numbers tell you how far to walk along each direction to reach that spot.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a grocery order: 2 apples, 1 loaf of bread, 3 coffees. As a vector, that is $[2, 1, 3]$. Each slot always means the same thing (slot 1 is always apples), and the order never changes. A vector is a labeled shopping list where everyone agrees on the order, so the labels can be left out.

We write a vector as a column of numbers and give it a bold, lowercase name:

$$ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3} $$

In words: “$\mathbf{x}$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$.” The symbol $\mathbb{R}^3$ means “a list of 3 real numbers.” The small 3 is the dimension: how many numbers are in the list. An image with 784 pixels is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{784}$.

Now the math

You can do two things to vectors. You can add them (slot by slot), and you can scale them (multiply every slot by one number, called a scalar):

$$ \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1+b_1 \\ a_2+b_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\,\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} c\,a_1 \\ c\,a_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: adding means walking along one arrow, then along the other, tip to tail. Scaling by $c$ makes the arrow $c$ times as long. If $c$ is negative, the arrow also flips around. A scalar is a single number, as opposed to a list.

Check yourself Take $\mathbf{a}=[3,1]$ and $\mathbf{b}=[1,2]$ from Figure 1.1. What is $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$, and where is that arrow in the picture? Reveal

Subtract slot by slot: $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} = [3-1,\ 1-2] = [2,-1]$. In the picture, it is the arrow that goes from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{a}$. You can check this: $\mathbf{b} + (\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}$. So a difference of two vectors is “how to get from one point to the other.” We use this later to measure how far a prediction is from the truth.

2The dot product: how aligned are two arrows?

Intuition first

The dot product takes two vectors and returns one number. That number measures how much the two arrows point the same way. A large positive number means they point in similar directions. Zero means they are at right angles. A negative number means they point in opposite directions. This one operation appears inside almost every model you will build.

☂ Everyday parallel

A dating app lists five topics: hiking, jazz, cats, cooking, football. Each person is a vector with $+1$ for “likes” and $-1$ for “dislikes” in each slot. To compare two people, multiply their matching slots. The product is $+1$ where they agree and $-1$ where they disagree. Add the five products and you get one score. That score is the dot product. Agree on everything: $+5$. Disagree on everything: $-5$. Agree on half: near 0.

Now the math

Multiply the vectors slot by slot, then add up all the results:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\,b_i \;=\; a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + \dots + a_nb_n $$

In words: the big $\Sigma$ (“sigma”) means “add up the following as $i$ counts from 1 to $n$.” So $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ pairs up the slots, multiplies each pair, and sums the products. The output is always a single number, however long the vectors are.

A second, geometric formula gives the same number. It shows why the dot product measures alignment:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta $$

In words: the dot product equals (length of $\mathbf{a}$) × (length of $\mathbf{b}$) × the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between them. The cosine is 1 when the angle is 0° and drops to 0 at 90°. So the dot product is largest when the arrows are parallel, and zero when they are perpendicular.

Why do the two formulas agree? Look at the arrow $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$, which joins the two tips. We can write its squared length in two ways. We define length properly in Section 4. For now, all we need is this: the squared length of a vector is its dot product with itself. So the first way is to expand that product:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = (\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})\cdot(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}) = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} $$

Second, the three arrows $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$ form a triangle. The law of cosines from school geometry says:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\,\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta $$

In words: both lines describe the same length. Everything matches except the last term, so those last terms must be equal: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$.

Definition

Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. “Orthogonal” is the mathematician's word for at a right angle. It means the two arrows share no direction: moving along one does not move you along the other at all. (Careful: two data features whose vectors are orthogonal are not automatically “unrelated” in the statistical sense. That needs more conditions.)

The same thing in PyTorch

dot product, two ways

import torch

a = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
b = torch.tensor([4.0, 5.0, 6.0])

# element-wise multiply, then sum: the definition
print((a * b).sum())        # tensor(32.)   = 1*4 + 2*5 + 3*6

# the built-in function most code uses
print(torch.dot(a, b))      # tensor(32.)

In words: a * b multiplies matching slots and gives a new vector $[4, 10, 18]$. .sum() adds those up. torch.dot does both steps in one call.

Check yourself Let $\mathbf{a}=[1,2]$ and $\mathbf{b}=[2,-1]$. What is $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$? Now double $\mathbf{b}$. What happens to the dot product, and what happens to the angle? Reveal

$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = (1)(2) + (2)(-1) = 0$, so the arrows are orthogonal. Doubling $\mathbf{b}$ gives $[4,-2]$, and the dot product is still 0: the angle stays 90°. For arrows that are not at right angles, doubling $\mathbf{b}$ doubles the dot product, but the angle does not change. So the dot product mixes two things: length and alignment. To measure alignment alone, divide by both lengths. The result, $\cos\theta$, is called cosine similarity.

3Matrices as transformations

Intuition first

A matrix is a grid of numbers, like a stack of vectors. The key insight is this: a matrix is a machine that takes a vector in and returns a different vector out. It can rotate, stretch, squash and reflect space. When you multiply a matrix by a vector, you ask the machine: “where does this point end up after the transformation?”

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of the “rotate” and “stretch” tools in a photo editor. “Rotate 90°” sends every point of the picture to a new place, by one fixed rule. “Stretch horizontally by 2” doubles every point's distance from the left edge, again by one fixed rule. A matrix is such a rule. Give it a different picture and the same rule runs. (A slider like “brightness” is different: it adds the same amount to every pixel. A shift like that is not a matrix. It is the job of the bias $\mathbf{b}$ that we meet below.)

Now the math

A matrix has rows and columns. We name it with a bold capital and write its shape as (rows × columns):

$$ \mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2} $$

To transform a vector, we use matrix–vector multiplication. The rule: each output slot is the dot product of one row of the matrix with the input vector.

$$ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11}x_1 + w_{12}x_2 \\ w_{21}x_1 + w_{22}x_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: the top output number is (row 1) · $\mathbf{x}$. The bottom output number is (row 2) · $\mathbf{x}$. So a matrix is several dot products stacked up, and you already understand dot products. Row 1 of $\mathbf{W}$ asks one question about $\mathbf{x}$, row 2 asks another, and the answers stack into the output vector.

There is a second way to read the same product. Put $\mathbf{x}=[1,0]$ in: the output is the first column of $\mathbf{W}$. Put $[0,1]$ in: the output is the second column. So the columns of a matrix tell you where the two unit arrows land. Because the rule is linear, the whole grid follows them:

◆ Why this matters for deep learning

A single layer of a neural network is $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$. It transforms the input with a matrix $\mathbf{W}$, then shifts it by a vector $\mathbf{b}$ (the bias). Stack a few of these with a nonlinear step between them and you have a deep network. We build neurons, layers and nonlinearity properly in Section 9. When we say a model “learns,” we mean it searches for the numbers to put inside $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

Matrix × matrix = do one transformation, then the next

Multiplying two matrices means composing their transformations: apply the right one first, then the left one. This is why order matters: $\mathbf{AB}\neq\mathbf{BA}$ in general. Rotating and then stretching is not the same as stretching and then rotating. The shape rule is the one thing you must memorize, because shape mismatches are among the most common bugs in ML code:

The shape rule

To multiply $(m\times n)\times(n\times p)$, the inner numbers must match (both $n$). The result has the shape of the outer numbers: $(m\times p)$. Read it as: “the middle two cancel, the outer two survive.”

The same thing in PyTorch

a matrix transforming a vector, and the shape rule

import torch

W = torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.0],     # stretch x by 2, leave y alone
                  [0.0, 1.0]])
x = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0])

y = W @ x                 # '@' is matrix multiplication in Python
print(y)                  # tensor([6., 4.])  -> x doubled, y unchanged

# shapes decide everything. Print them when confused:
A = torch.randn(2, 3)     # (2 x 3)
B = torch.randn(3, 5)     # (3 x 5)   inner 3s match
print((A @ B).shape)      # torch.Size([2, 5])  -> outer numbers survive

In words: the first part applies a stretch to one vector. The second part multiplies two random matrices only to show the shapes. The values inside A and B do not matter here.

▲ Watch out

RuntimeError: mat1 and mat2 shapes cannot be multiplied (2x3 and 5x3). The inner numbers (3 and 5) do not match. Most of the time the fix is to transpose one matrix (swap its rows and columns with .T) so the inner numbers line up. When in doubt, print(tensor.shape) on every line.

Check yourself You have a batch of 64 images, each flattened to 784 numbers, stored as a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ of shape (64, 784). A layer has weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ of shape (10, 784), one row per output, as in PyTorch's nn.Linear. Does X @ W work? What should you write instead, and what is the output shape? Reveal

X @ W fails: (64, 784) × (10, 784) has inner numbers 784 and 10. Transpose $\mathbf{W}$ first: $\mathbf{W}^\top$ has shape (784, 10). Now (64, 784) × (784, 10) works, and the outer numbers survive: the output X @ W.T has shape (64, 10). That is 10 scores for each of the 64 images. This is the last layer of a digit classifier, and it is how the book writes a batched layer: $\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^\top+\mathbf{b}$.

4Norms: measuring the size of a vector

Intuition first

A norm answers a simple question: “how big is this vector?” It turns a whole list of numbers into one measure of length. We need this all the time. For example, we measure how far a prediction is from the truth, or how large a model's weights have grown.

☂ Everyday parallel

There are two ways to measure the distance from your home to a friend's apartment. The L2 way is the straight-line distance, what a ruler on a map shows. The L1 way is the “taxicab” distance: you can only drive along the street grid, so you add the blocks east and the blocks north. Same start, same end, two different and valid meanings of “how far.” ML uses both on purpose.

Now the math

The L2 norm (the default, also called “Euclidean length”) is the Pythagorean theorem in any number of dimensions. Square each slot, add them, take the square root:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^{2}} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2} $$

Note that the sum under the root is $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}$. So $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2^2 = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}$, which is the fact we used in Section 2. The L1 norm adds up the sizes (absolute values) of the slots, with no squares and no root:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| = |x_1| + |x_2| + \dots + |x_n| $$

In words: L2 punishes one big value hard, because it squares it. So it prefers many small values to one large one. L1 treats all values the same way. As you will see in Section 7, L1 tends to push some weights all the way to zero. This difference is the reason we have two kinds of regularization (a technique that keeps models simple). Remember it for Section 7.

The same thing in PyTorch

L2 and L1 norms of one vector

import torch
x = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0])

print(torch.linalg.vector_norm(x))          # tensor(5.)   sqrt(3^2 + 4^2) = sqrt(25)
print(torch.linalg.vector_norm(x, ord=1))   # tensor(7.)   |3| + |4|

In words: torch.linalg.vector_norm computes the L2 norm by default. The argument ord=1 asks for the L1 norm instead.

Check yourself The vectors $[3,4]$ and $[5,0]$ both have L2 norm 5. Predict their L1 norms. Which one does L1 consider “smaller,” and what does that tell you about the kind of vector L1 prefers? Reveal

$\lVert[3,4]\rVert_1 = 7$ and $\lVert[5,0]\rVert_1 = 5$. L2 sees them as equal, but L1 calls $[5,0]$ smaller. The vector with a zero in it wins. Suppose you penalize the L1 norm of the weights. Then the cheapest way to lower the penalty is often to set some weights to zero. That is the effect Section 7 builds on.

5Eigenvalues: the directions a matrix only stretches

Intuition first

A matrix stretches and turns space. For most input directions, the arrow comes out pointing somewhere new. But many matrices have a few special directions that they do not turn. Along those directions the matrix only stretches or shrinks the arrow. It keeps pointing along the same line. These special directions are eigenvectors. The factor by which each one is stretched is its eigenvalue. (“Eigen” is German for “own”: these are the matrix's own directions.)

☂ Everyday parallel

Stretch a rubber sheet with a drawing on it: pull the left and right edges apart. Most arrows drawn on the sheet change direction as it stretches. But an arrow that points left–right stays left–right; it only gets longer. An arrow that points up–down also keeps its direction. Those two directions are the eigenvectors of the stretch, and “how much longer” is each eigenvalue.

Now the math

The defining equation is short and says what we described in words:

$$ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} $$

In words: apply the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ to its special vector $\mathbf{v}$. You get back the same vector $\mathbf{v}$, only scaled by the number $\lambda$ (lambda). The matrix may turn other arrows, but on $\mathbf{v}$ it acts like plain multiplication by a number. No new direction, only a stretch by $\lambda$.

Not every matrix has such directions among ordinary arrows. A 2-D rotation by 90° turns every arrow, so no real direction stays on its own line. Its eigenvalues are complex numbers, which you will see in the code below. Symmetric matrices (a matrix equal to its own transpose) always have real eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Why care, before we have built a model? Because eigenvalues explain a lot of ML behavior you will meet later:

  • PCA (Principal Component Analysis, a way to compress data) uses the eigenvectors of your data's covariance matrix. They are the directions along which the data varies most, so you keep those and drop the rest. We will not prove this here.
  • Exploding and vanishing gradients (training failures we meet in Section 12 and Section 17) happen when the same matrix is applied many times. Along an eigenvector, each application multiplies the length by $\lambda$. If $|\lambda|$ is above 1, the length grows without limit; below 1, it fades to nothing.

The same thing in PyTorch

eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix, and a rotation that has none

import torch

A = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0],
                  [1.0, 2.0]])          # the matrix from Figure 1.4

x = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0])
print(A @ x)                           # tensor([2., 1.])  turned: not an eigenvector
v = torch.tensor([1.0, 1.0])
print(A @ v)                           # tensor([3., 3.])  = 3 * v -> eigenvalue 3

vals, vecs = torch.linalg.eigh(A)      # eigh: for symmetric matrices
print(vals)                            # tensor([1., 3.])  the two stretch factors
print(vecs[:, 1])                      # tensor([0.7071, 0.7071])  v, scaled to length 1

R = torch.tensor([[0.0, -1.0],
                  [1.0,  0.0]])         # rotation by 90 degrees
print(torch.linalg.eigvals(R))         # tensor([0.+1.j, 0.-1.j])  complex: no real direction survives

In words: eigh returns the eigenvalues in increasing order and the eigenvectors as the columns of vecs. Column 1 belongs to eigenvalue 3 and points along $[1,1]$. The other eigenvector, $[1,-1]$ scaled to length 1, has eigenvalue 1: $\mathbf{A}$ leaves it unchanged. For the rotation, eigvals returns the imaginary numbers $\pm i$ (the j in the output means $i$).

Check yourself A matrix has an eigenvalue of 0.5 along some direction. You apply the matrix 20 times to a vector along that direction. What happens to the vector's length? What if the eigenvalue were 2? Reveal

Each application multiplies the length by 0.5. After 20 applications the length is $0.5^{20} \approx 0.00000095$ times the start, which is almost zero. The vector vanishes. With eigenvalue 2 you get $2^{20} \approx 1{,}000{,}000$ times the start: the vector explodes. This is the vanishing and exploding gradient problem that makes deep and recurrent networks hard to train. It is why training methods try to keep these factors close to 1.

6What to carry forward

  • Vectors are lists of numbers that you can picture as arrows. Data becomes vectors.
  • The dot product multiplies matching slots and sums them. It returns one number that measures alignment: $\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta$.
  • A matrix is a reusable transformation. Matrix–vector multiplication is several dot products stacked, and the columns show where the unit arrows land. One neural layer is $\mathbf{Wx}+\mathbf{b}$.
  • The shape rule: inner dimensions must match, outer dimensions survive. Print shapes to find bugs.
  • Norms measure vector size (L2 = straight line, L1 = taxicab). Eigenvalues are the stretch factors along the directions a matrix does not turn.

Next we learn how to change those numbers on purpose. That needs calculus, and the central tool of training: the gradient.
