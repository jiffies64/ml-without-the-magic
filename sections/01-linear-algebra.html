Linear Algebra for ML

A neural network is, underneath, a stack of vectors being pushed through matrices. Learn to read that sentence and you can read almost anything in ML.

28 min read
You'll learn
  • vectors
  • dot products
  • matrices as transformations
  • norms
  • eigenvalues

Every piece of data you will ever feed a model — an image, a sentence, a row in a spreadsheet — first becomes a list of numbers. Linear algebra is simply the set of rules for working with those lists efficiently. It is the grammar of machine learning, and the good news is you already have the intuition; we just need to attach the notation to it.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Data becomes vectors. Operations on data become matrices. Learning is the search for the right numbers to put inside those matrices. That's the whole game — everything below is detail.

1Vectors: a point, and an arrow

Intuition first

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers. That's the boring definition. The useful one: a vector is an arrow in space that points from the origin (the center, position zero) to some spot. The numbers tell you how far to walk along each direction to reach that spot.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a grocery order: 2 apples, 1 loaf of bread, 3 coffees. Written as a vector that's [2, 1, 3]. Each slot always means the same thing (slot 1 is always apples), and the order never changes. A vector is nothing scarier than a labelled shopping list where you've agreed to drop the labels and just remember the order.

We write a vector as a column of numbers and give it a bold, lowercase name:

$$ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3} $$

In words: “x is a vector living in ℝ³” — that symbol just means “a list of 3 real numbers.” The little 3 is the dimension: how many numbers are in the list. An image with 784 pixels is a vector in ℝ⁷⁸⁴.

Now the math

Two things you can do to vectors, and both are exactly what they look like. You can add them (slot by slot) and you can scale them (multiply every slot by one number, called a scalar):

$$ \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1+b_1 \\ a_2+b_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\,\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} c\,a_1 \\ c\,a_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: adding walks one arrow, then the other, tip to tail. Scaling by c stretches the arrow to c times its length (and flips it if c is negative). A scalar is just a plain single number, as opposed to a list.

2The dot product: how aligned are two arrows?

Intuition first

The dot product takes two vectors and returns a single number that measures how much they point the same way. Big positive number: they point in similar directions. Zero: they're at right angles (totally unrelated). Negative: they point in opposing directions. This one operation is doing quiet work inside almost every model you'll ever build.

☂ Everyday parallel

A dating app scores your profile against someone else's: +3 for shared love of hiking, +2 for both liking jazz, −1 because one of you hates cats. Add those up and you get a single compatibility score. That summed-up score is a dot product — multiply matching slots, then add everything together. A high score means “aligned”; a score near zero means “no strong relationship either way.”

Now the math

Multiply the vectors slot by slot, then add up all the results:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\,b_i \;=\; a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + \dots + a_nb_n $$

In words: the big Σ (“sigma”) just says “add up the following as i counts from 1 to n.” So a·b = (pair up the slots, multiply each pair, sum the products). The output is always a single number, no matter how long the vectors are.

There is a second, geometric formula for the exact same number, and it's the one that reveals why the dot product measures alignment:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta $$

In words: the dot product equals (length of a) × (length of b) × the cosine of the angle θ between them. Because cos is 1 when the angle is 0° and drops to 0 at 90°, the dot product is largest when the arrows are parallel and exactly zero when they are perpendicular. That's the alignment meter, made precise.

Definition

Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. “Orthogonal” is the mathematician's word for at a right angle, i.e. carrying no shared direction. It shows up constantly — it's the precise way to say “these two features are unrelated.”

The same thing in PyTorch

dot product, two ways

import torch

a = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
b = torch.tensor([4.0, 5.0, 6.0])

# element-wise multiply, then sum — literally the definition
print((a * b).sum())        # tensor(32.)   = 1*4 + 2*5 + 3*6

# the idiomatic one-liner every codebase uses
print(torch.dot(a, b))      # tensor(32.)
Check yourself Vectors u = [1, 0] and v = [0, 1]: what is u · v, and what does the result tell you geometrically? Reveal

u · v = (1)(0) + (0)(1) = 0. A dot product of zero means the two arrows are orthogonal — at a perfect right angle. And indeed, [1,0] points along the x-axis while [0,1] points straight up the y-axis; they share no direction at all. This is exactly why [1,0] and [0,1] are called the standard basis of the 2D plane: two independent directions you can mix to reach any point.

3Matrices as transformations

Intuition first

A matrix is a grid of numbers — a stack of vectors, if you like. But the real insight, the one that makes deep learning click, is this: a matrix is a machine that takes a vector in and returns a different vector out. It rotates, stretches, squishes, and reflects space. When you multiply a matrix by a vector, you are asking the machine “where does this point end up after the transformation?”

☂ Everyday parallel

A photo-editing slider is a transformation. “Increase brightness” takes every pixel and maps it to a new value. “Rotate 90°” takes every point and sends it somewhere new, in a consistent, predictable way. A matrix is exactly that: one fixed rule applied to every input vector. Feed it a different picture, same rule runs — that reusable rule is the whole point.

Now the math

A matrix has rows and columns; we name it with a bold capital and write its shape as (rows × columns):

$$ \mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2} $$

To transform a vector, we do matrix–vector multiplication. The rule: each output slot is the dot product of one row of the matrix with the input vector.

$$ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11}x_1 + w_{12}x_2 \\ w_{21}x_1 + w_{22}x_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

In words: the top output number is (row 1) · (x). The bottom output number is (row 2) · (x). So a matrix is just several dot products stacked up — and you already understand dot products. Row 1 of W asks one question about x; row 2 asks another; the answers stack into the output vector.

◆ Why this is the whole of deep learning

A single layer of a neural network is exactly $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ — transform the input with a matrix W, then shift it by a vector b (the bias). Stack a few of these with a bit of nonlinearity between them and you have a deep network — we build neurons, layers, and nonlinearity properly in Section 9. When we say a model “learns,” we mean it is searching for the numbers to write inside W and b.

Matrix × matrix = do one transformation, then the next

Multiplying two matrices means composing their transformations: apply the right one first, then the left one. This is why order matters ($\mathbf{AB}\neq\mathbf{BA}$ in general — rotating then stretching is not the same as stretching then rotating). The shape rule is the one thing you must memorize, because 90% of real ML bugs are shape mismatches:

The shape rule

To multiply $(m\times n)\times(n\times p)$ the inner numbers must match (both n). The result has the shape of the outer numbers: $(m\times p)$. Read it as: “the middle two cancel, the outer two survive.”

The same thing in PyTorch

a matrix transforming a vector

import torch

W = torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.0],     # stretch x by 2, leave y alone
                  [0.0, 1.0]])
x = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0])

y = W @ x                 # '@' is matrix multiplication in Python
print(y)                  # tensor([6., 4.])  -> x doubled, y unchanged

# shapes are everything. print them when confused:
A = torch.randn(2, 3)     # (2 x 3)
B = torch.randn(3, 5)     # (3 x 5)   inner 3s match
print((A @ B).shape)      # torch.Size([2, 5])  -> outer numbers survive
▲ The bug you will hit first

RuntimeError: mat1 and mat2 shapes cannot be multiplied (2x3 and 5x3). The inner numbers (3 and 5) don't match. Ninety percent of the time the fix is to transpose one matrix — flip its rows and columns with .T — so the inner dimensions line up. When in doubt, print(tensor.shape) on every line.

Check yourself You have a batch of 64 images, each flattened to a 784-length vector, stored as a matrix X of shape (64, 784). You want to pass them through a layer with weight matrix W. What shape must W be to produce 10 outputs per image, and what's the output shape? Reveal

We compute X @ W with X of shape (64, 784). For the inner numbers to match, W must start with 784: its shape is (784, 10). By the shape rule, (64, 784) × (784, 10) → the outer numbers survive, so the output is (64, 10): 10 numbers for each of the 64 images. This is exactly the shape of a linear layer that turns pixels into 10 class scores — you just designed the last layer of a digit classifier.

4Norms: measuring the size of a vector

Intuition first

A norm answers a simple question: “how big is this vector?” It collapses a whole list of numbers into a single measure of length or magnitude. We need this constantly — to measure how far a prediction is from the truth, or how large a model's weights have grown.

☂ Everyday parallel

Two ways to measure the distance from your home to a friend's flat. The L2 way is the straight-line “as the crow flies” distance — what a ruler on a map shows. The L1 way is the “taxicab” distance — you can only drive along the street grid, so you add up the blocks east plus the blocks north. Same start and end, two honest but different notions of “how far,” and ML uses both on purpose.

Now the math

The L2 norm (the default, “Euclidean length”) is the Pythagorean theorem in any number of dimensions — square each slot, add them, take the square root:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^{2}} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2} $$

The L1 norm just adds up the sizes (absolute values) of the slots — no squaring, no square root:

$$ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| = |x_1| + |x_2| + \dots + |x_n| $$

In words: L2 punishes one big value harshly (because it squares it), so it prefers many small values over one large one. L1 treats all values evenly and, as you'll see in Section 7, has a knack for pushing weights all the way to exactly zero. That single difference is the entire reason we have two flavours of regularization (a technique for keeping models simple), so keep it in your pocket.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch
x = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0])

print(torch.norm(x, p=2))   # tensor(5.)   sqrt(3^2 + 4^2) = sqrt(25)
print(torch.norm(x, p=1))   # tensor(7.)   |3| + |4|

5Eigenvalues: the directions a matrix leaves alone

Intuition first

We said a matrix stretches and rotates space. For most input directions, the arrow comes out pointing somewhere new. But almost every matrix has a few special directions that it doesn't rotate — it only stretches or shrinks them, leaving them pointing the same way. Those special directions are eigenvectors, and the amount each one gets stretched is its eigenvalue. (“Eigen” is German for “own” or “characteristic” — these are the matrix's own favoured directions.)

☂ Everyday parallel

Spin a globe. Almost every city on the surface swings around to a new position. But the two poles stay put — they sit exactly on the axis of rotation. That axis is the globe's eigenvector: the one direction the “spin” transformation leaves pointing the same way. Eigenvectors are the poles of whatever a matrix does.

Now the math

The defining equation is short and says exactly what we described in words:

$$ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} $$

In words: “applying the matrix A to its special vector v gives back the same vector v, merely scaled by the number λ (lambda).” The transformation A — which normally rotates things — acts on v like plain multiplication by a number. No new direction, just a stretch by λ.

Why should you care, before we've even built a model? Because eigenvalues quietly explain a lot of ML behaviour you'll meet later:

  • PCA (Principal Component Analysis, a way to compress data) finds the eigenvectors of your data — the directions along which the data varies most, so you can keep those and throw the rest away.
  • Exploding and vanishing gradients (a training failure we'll hit in Section 17) happen when repeated matrix multiplications have eigenvalues bigger or smaller than 1 — the same stretch applied over and over blows up or fades to nothing.

Seeing it in PyTorch

import torch

A = torch.tensor([[2.0, 0.0],
                  [0.0, 3.0]])          # a pure stretch: x by 2, y by 3

vals = torch.linalg.eigvals(A).real
print(vals)                            # tensor([2., 3.])  the two stretch factors

# check the definition on the x-axis direction v = [1, 0]:
v = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0])
print(A @ v)                           # tensor([2., 0.]) == 2 * v   -> eigenvalue 2
Check yourself A matrix has an eigenvalue of 0.5 along some direction. If you apply that matrix to a vector pointing in that direction, over and over, 20 times — what happens to the vector's length? Reveal

Each application multiplies the length by 0.5, so after 20 applications the length is 0.5²⁰ ≈ 0.00000095 of what it started as — practically zero. The vector vanishes. Flip the eigenvalue to 2 instead and you'd get 2²⁰ ≈ 1,000,000× — it explodes. This is precisely the vanishing/exploding-gradient problem that makes deep and recurrent networks hard to train, and it's why eigenvalues near 1 are so prized. You just previewed one of the deepest issues in the whole course.

6What to carry forward

  • Vectors are lists of numbers you can picture as arrows; data becomes vectors.
  • The dot product multiplies matching slots and sums them — an alignment meter that returns one number.
  • A matrix is a reusable transformation; matrix–vector multiply is just several dot products stacked. One neural layer is $\mathbf{Wx}+\mathbf{b}$.
  • The shape rule: inner dimensions must match, outer dimensions survive. Print shapes to kill bugs.
  • Norms measure vector size (L2 = straight line, L1 = taxicab); eigenvalues are the stretch factors along a matrix's favoured directions.

Next we'll learn how to change those numbers on purpose — which means calculus, and the single most important idea in the whole course: the gradient.
