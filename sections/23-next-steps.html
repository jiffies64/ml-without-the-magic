Where to Go Next

You have gone from a vector to a working Mixture of Experts. This last section gives you a map for what comes next: what to read, what to build, and how to keep learning without getting lost in hype.

There is no final exam for machine learning. The field moves every week, and nobody reads everything, not even the people whose names are on the papers. What you need instead is three things. First, a short list of the papers that changed how the field builds models. Second, a habit of building small projects that force real understanding. Third, a way to filter the constant noise. This section gives you all three, and then says goodbye.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

You do not need to read everything. You need a path (papers that build on each other, in order) and a habit (read a little, build a little, repeat). Both last longer than any single paper you learn from.

1A reading list, in order

Intuition first

Below are papers that shaped the field, grouped by theme. Inside each group they are in date order, so each one can use the ideas before it. Read them after you finish the course, one every week or two, not all at once. Use the three-pass method from Section 21 on each of them.

☂ Everyday parallel

Nobody learns the piano by opening a book of Chopin on day one. You learn scales, then simple songs, then harder ones, and later you improvise. Reading order matters as much as reading itself. The Transformer paper is your Chopin. It will make far more sense once you have done the scales below.

Foundations

  1. Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986. “Learning representations by back-propagating errors.” The paper that made training multi-layer networks practical. You built this by hand in Section 10.
  2. Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012. “ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks” (AlexNet). Showed that deep nets, GPUs, and enough data beat hand-engineered computer vision by a wide margin.
  3. Kingma & Ba, 2014. “Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization.” The default optimizer for most models since. You met it in Section 12.
  4. Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015. “Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift.” Normalizing activations between layers made much deeper networks trainable.
  5. He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, 2015. “Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition” (ResNet). The skip connection: a layer can learn nothing and still pass its input through unchanged. This is why networks hundreds of layers deep can train.
  6. Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016. “Layer Normalization.” The normalization that transformers use. It is short, and it helps before you read the Transformer and GPT-2 papers closely.

Sequence to attention

  1. Vaswani et al., 2017. “Attention Is All You Need.” Replaces recurrence (Section 17) with attention alone. This is the architecture behind modern language models, covered in Section 18.
  2. Su et al., 2021. “RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding” (RoPE). Encodes position by rotating query and key vectors. Most recent open language models use it instead of the original position encoding.

Large language models

  1. Radford et al., 2019. “Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners” (GPT-2). Shows that one next-token objective, scaled up, does far more than predict text. You built the core of this model in Section 19.
  2. Brown et al., 2020. “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners” (GPT-3). Scales GPT-2's recipe from 1.5 billion to 175 billion parameters (about 117×). The model can then learn a new task from a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates.
  3. Hoffmann et al., 2022. “Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models” (Chinchilla). Shows that many large models of that time were undertrained for their size. For a fixed compute budget, a smaller model on more data beats a bigger model on less. Read it right after GPT-3 to see the field correct itself.

Efficiency and Mixture of Experts

  1. Shazeer et al., 2017. “Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer.” The modern MoE layer, with noisy top-k gating and a load-balancing loss.
  2. Lepikhin et al., 2020. “GShard: Scaling Giant Models with Conditional Computation and Automatic Sharding.” Top-2 routing with expert capacity, scaled across thousands of accelerators.
  3. Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, 2021. “Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity.” Simplifies routing to top-1. Its auxiliary loss is the one you derived in Section 22. Section 22 used top-2 with renormalized weights and no capacity limit; this paper shows the top-1 version with capacity.
  4. Dao et al., 2022. “FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness.” Computes the same attention as Section 18, but in tiles that fit in fast GPU memory. A good lesson in how the memory hierarchy, not only FLOPs, sets speed.
  5. Zhou et al., 2022. “Mixture-of-Experts with Expert Choice Routing.” Turns routing around: each expert picks its top tokens, so load is balanced by construction.
  6. Jiang et al., 2024. “Mixtral of Experts.” An openly released MoE model with 8 experts and top-2 routing in every layer. It is close to the layer you built, at a scale people run.
  7. DeepSeek-AI, 2024. “DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report.” A 671B-parameter MoE with 37B active parameters per token. It uses fine-grained and shared experts and balances load without relying on an auxiliary loss.

Fine-tuning and alignment

  1. Hu et al., 2021. “LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models.” Adapt a large pretrained model to a new task by training a small number of extra parameters instead of the whole network.
  2. Ouyang et al., 2022. “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback” (InstructGPT / RLHF). How a raw next-token predictor like GPT-3 becomes an assistant that follows instructions.
  3. Rafailov et al., 2023. “Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model” (DPO). Gets a similar result to RLHF with one simple loss on pairs of preferred and rejected answers, and no separate reward model.
Check yourself You want to understand why Mixtral runs only 2 of its 8 experts per token. Which two papers from the list should you read first, and in what order? Reveal

Read Shazeer et al. (2017) first. It defines the sparsely-gated MoE layer and top-k routing, so you learn the words (router, gate, expert, top-k) before a later paper assumes them. Then read Switch Transformer (2021). It argues that $k$ can go down to 1 and scales the idea inside a full transformer. Mixtral's “top-2 of 8” is a choice between those points. If you read it without either paper, “why 2 and not 1 or 4” has no motivation.

2Project ideas, by level

Intuition first

Reading tells you what is possible. Building is what makes it stick. Pick one project below that is slightly above your comfort level, and finish it before you start the next. Half-finished projects teach much less than one completed project.

☂ Everyday parallel

A trainer does not give a beginner the heaviest weights on day one. The trainer also does not keep a strong lifter on the lightest weights forever. Match the project to where you are now, and move up one step only when it is done.

Check yourself Judge this claim: “I reproduced the LoRA result: my fine-tuned model's loss went down.” What is missing before you can call it a reproduction? Reveal

Almost any training run makes the loss go down, so this shows only that the code runs. A reproduction needs a comparison. The paper's claim is that LoRA gets close to full fine-tuning while training far fewer parameters. So you need both runs on the same data and budget, the same evaluation metric, and the number of trained parameters for each. Then you can say whether your gap matches the paper's.

3How to stay current without the hype

Intuition first

New papers appear faster than anyone can read them. Many of the loudest announcements do not hold up under careful testing. The goal is not to read everything. The goal is a short, reliable set of places to look, and a fast filter that separates a real result from a demo video.

Where to look:

  • arXiv, the preprint server. The cs.LG and cs.CL listings are where most work appears first, often months before formal publication.
  • Top conferences: NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR. Peer review there filters the flood of preprints down to work that passed independent review.
  • Hugging Face Papers. A daily, community-voted list of new papers, often linked to their code and released models. (Papers With Code used to fill this role; it shut down in 2025 and now redirects here.)
  • A few consistently careful newsletters and blogs. Prefer the ones that link the paper and the code, not only a demo clip. A short list is worth more than a dozen noisy ones, so remove sources that waste your time.

How to check a claim with healthy doubt, in about two minutes:

  • Is there an ablation table, or only one headline number? (Section 21 explained why this matters.)
  • Is the baseline fair (same data, same compute budget, same amount of tuning), or is it a weak opponent chosen to lose?
  • Is there released code, and has anyone outside the original team reproduced the result?
  • Is the size of the claim in proportion to the evidence? “Improves accuracy by 2 points on this benchmark” is a claim. “Solves reasoning” is marketing.
▲ Watch out

The most common hype pattern is a striking demo with no baseline, no ablation, and no released code, often timed with a funding announcement. This does not mean the work is fake. It means you do not yet have enough to judge it. Treating it as settled fact at that point is the trap. Wait for the paper, or move on.

Check yourself Judge this claim: “Our new MoE model has 1 trillion parameters and beats a 70-billion-parameter dense model on 5 benchmarks, so sparse models are 14× more efficient.” Which checks from the list does it fail, and what would you ask for? Reveal

The “14×” compares total parameters, but compute per token depends on active parameters (Section 22). Ask for the active parameter count and the training compute of both models; that is the fair baseline. Ask whether both saw the same data. Five benchmarks with no ablation cannot show which change caused the gain. The claim is also larger than the evidence: “beats on 5 benchmarks” does not support a general efficiency ratio.

4What to carry forward

  • Read the list above in order: foundations, then attention, then language models, then efficiency and MoE, then fine-tuning. Each group builds on the one before.
  • Pick projects one step above where you are, and finish each one before you start the next.
  • Stay current with a short, reliable source list (arXiv, top conferences, Hugging Face Papers, a few careful newsletters). Check claims fast: ablations, fair baselines, released code, claims in proportion to evidence.
  • There is no finish line, only a better practice habit, as with any instrument worth learning.
