Where to Go Next

You've gone from a vector to a working Mixture of Experts — this last section hands you a map for everything after this course: what to read, what to build, and how to keep learning without drowning in hype.

18 min read You'll learn: a reading list in order · project ideas by level · how to stay current without the hype

There is no final exam for machine learning. The field moves every week, and nobody reads everything — not even the people whose names are on the papers. What you need instead is a short list of the papers that actually changed how the field builds things, a habit of building small projects that force real understanding, and a way to filter the constant noise. This section gives you all three, and then says goodbye.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

You don't need to read everything — you need a path (papers that build on each other, in order) and a habit (read a little, build a little, on repeat). Both outlast any single paper you learn from them.

1A reading list, in order

Intuition first

Below are the papers that actually matter, grouped by theme and ordered so each one leans on the idea before it. Read them in this order once you finish the course — not all at once, one every week or two is plenty. Use the three-pass method from Section 21 on every one of them.

☂ Everyday parallel

Nobody learns an instrument by opening a book of Chopin on day one. You learn scales, then simple songs, then harder ones, then eventually you improvise. Reading order matters as much as reading itself — the Transformer paper is your Chopin, and it will make far more sense once you've done the scales below.

Foundations

  1. Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986 — "Learning representations by back-propagating errors." The paper that made training multi-layer networks practical; you built this by hand in Section 10.
  2. Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012 — "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks" (AlexNet). Proved deep nets plus GPUs plus enough data beats hand-engineered computer vision, almost overnight.
  3. Kingma & Ba, 2014 — "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization." The optimizer nearly every model since has defaulted to; you met it in Section 12.
  4. Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015 — "Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift." Why normalizing activations between layers made much deeper networks trainable.
  5. He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, 2015 — "Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition" (ResNet). The skip connection: let a layer learn nothing and still pass its input through untouched. This one idea is why networks hundreds of layers deep can train at all.

Sequence → attention

  1. Vaswani et al., 2017 — "Attention Is All You Need." Replaces recurrence (Section 17) with attention alone; the architecture behind every modern language model, covered in Section 18.

Large language models

  1. Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016 — "Layer Normalization." The normalization variant Transformers actually use, and a small prerequisite for reading GPT-2 closely.
  2. Radford et al., 2019 — "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners" (GPT-2). Shows a single next-token-prediction objective, scaled up, does far more than predict text — the model you built the core of in Section 19.
  3. Brown et al., 2020 — "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (GPT-3). Scaling GPT-2's recipe by ~1000x parameters produces the ability to learn a new task from a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates at all.
  4. Hoffmann et al., 2022 — "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (Chinchilla). Shows most large models of that era were undertrained relative to their size — for a fixed compute budget, a smaller model on more data beats a bigger model on less. Read this right after GPT-3 to see the field correct itself in real time.

Efficiency & Mixture of Experts

  1. Shazeer et al., 2017 — "Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer." The modern MoE layer, with top-2 routing.
  2. Lepikhin et al., 2020 — "GShard: Scaling Giant Models with Conditional Computation and Automatic Sharding." Scales MoE across thousands of accelerators.
  3. Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, 2021 — "Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity." Simplifies routing to top-1 and is exactly what you implemented in Section 22.
  4. Jiang et al., 2024 — "Mixtral of Experts." A production-quality, openly released MoE model — read this to see the Switch Transformer recipe applied at a scale people actually run.

Fine-tuning & alignment

  1. Hu et al., 2021 — "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models." How to adapt a huge pretrained model to a new task by training a tiny number of extra parameters instead of the whole network.
  2. Ouyang et al., 2022 — "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (InstructGPT / RLHF). How a raw next-token predictor like GPT-3 becomes an assistant that follows instructions — the step between a language model and a product.
Check yourself You want to understand why Mixtral only needs to run 2 of its 8 experts per token. Which two papers from the list above should you read first, and why in that order? Reveal

Read Shazeer et al. (2017) first — it's where the sparsely-gated MoE layer and top-k routing are defined from scratch, so you'll understand the vocabulary (router, gate, expert, top-k) before it's assumed. Then read Switch Transformer (2021), which argues you can push k down to 1 without losing quality and scales the idea into a full Transformer. Mixtral's "top-2 of 8" is a point on the spectrum both papers already staked out — reading it cold, without either predecessor, would leave "why 2 and not 1 or 4" completely unmotivated.

3How to stay current without the hype

Intuition first

New papers appear faster than anyone can read them, and a good fraction of the loudest announcements don't hold up under scrutiny. The goal isn't to read everything — it's to have a short, reliable set of places to look, and a fast filter for separating a real result from a demo video.

☂ Everyday parallel

Learning an instrument never really ends — there's always a harder piece, a faster tempo, a style you haven't tried. You don't panic about not having "finished" music; you keep a practice habit and let curiosity pick the next piece. Machine learning is the same: there's no finish line, only a better and better practice habit.

Where to actually look:

  • arXiv (the cs.LG and cs.CL categories) — where almost everything is posted first, often months before formal publication.
  • Top conferences — NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR are where peer review filters arXiv's firehose down to work that passed independent scrutiny.
  • Papers With Code — pairs papers with their released code and leaderboards, which is a fast way to check the next bullet point below.
  • A handful of consistently careful newsletters and blogs — the kind that link the paper and the code, not just a demo clip — are worth more than a dozen noisy ones. Curate a short list and prune it aggressively.

How to evaluate a claim skeptically, in about two minutes:

  • Is there an ablation table, or just one headline number? (Section 21 covered why this matters.)
  • Is the baseline comparable — same data, same compute budget, same amount of tuning — or is it a strawman?
  • Is there released code, and has anyone outside the original team reproduced the result?
  • Is the claim's size proportional to the evidence — "improves accuracy by 2 points on this benchmark" is a claim; "solves reasoning" is marketing.
▲ Watch out

The most common hype pattern is a striking demo with no baseline, no ablation, and no released code — often timed around a funding announcement. It doesn't mean the work is fake; it means you don't yet have enough to evaluate it, and treating it as settled fact before that is exactly the trap. Wait for the paper, or move on.

4What to carry forward

  • Read the list above in order — foundations, then attention, then LLMs, then efficiency and MoE, then fine-tuning — each leans on the one before it.
  • Pick projects one rung above where you are, and finish each one before starting the next: micrograd and a char-level GPT, then LoRA fine-tuning and a from-scratch optimizer, then extending the MoE or reproducing a paper cold.
  • Stay current with a short, reliable source list (arXiv, top conferences, Papers With Code, a few careful newsletters) and a fast skepticism checklist: ablations, fair baselines, released code, proportional claims.
  • There's no finish line — only a better practice habit, same as with any instrument worth learning.

You started this course looking at a vector — an arrow, a list of numbers — and you end it having built, from scratch, a router deciding which of several expert networks should handle a token inside a Transformer. Every step in between was earned, not skipped: you derived backprop by hand, wrote an MLP in raw NumPy, debugged real shape mismatches, and read a research paper the way an engineer does. That is the entire distance from zero to hero. Go build something with it.
