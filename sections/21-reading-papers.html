Reading a Paper Like an Engineer

You do not need to read every word of a research paper to get what is useful from it. You need a method: a few minutes to decide, an hour to understand, and more time only for the papers you build on.

32 min read
You'll learn
  • the three-pass reading method
  • what to extract from any paper
  • dissecting an MoE / Switch Transformer paper
  • habits that make it stick

Many people read a paper the way they read a novel. They start at the top, read every sentence in order, and hope that understanding arrives by the last page. It usually does not, and it is slow. You can spend two hours on a minor paragraph before you even know if the paper is relevant. Engineers read papers differently: in passes. Each pass goes deeper than the last and has one clear goal. This section gives you that method. Then it applies the method to the papers behind our capstone project.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Do not read a paper once, from start to finish. Read it in passes of increasing depth: skim, then structure, then reproduce. Stop as soon as the current pass has given you what you need.

1The three-pass method

Intuition first

This method is close to the one computer scientist S. Keshav described in “How to Read a Paper” (2007). Many working engineers arrive at something similar on their own. The idea: make three passes over the same document. Each pass asks a different question and takes more time than the last. You may stop after pass one if the paper does not matter to you.

☂ Everyday parallel

Learning a new city. First you look at a map for a few minutes: where is the center, where is the river, is this the right city at all (pass one)? Then you walk the main streets and find the one square everything connects to (pass two). Only for the city where you will live do you learn the side streets by finding your own way (pass three). Nobody learns every side street of every city they look at on a map.

  1. Pass 1: skim (about 5 minutes). Read only the title, the abstract (the short summary at the top of the paper), every figure with its caption, and the conclusion. Skip the method section entirely. Goal: decide what the paper claims and whether it is relevant to you. You may not be able to state its one-sentence claim after this pass. Then the paper is either poorly written, or you are not its intended reader. Both are useful to know before you spend more time.
  2. Pass 2: structure and the key equation (30–60 minutes). Now read the body, in order, but do not follow every citation or check every derivation. Note the structure: what problem section 1 sets up, what section 3 proposes, what section 4 measures. Find the one equation that the paper's claim depends on. Usually there is a single formula that everything else exists to justify. Also find the main architecture or results figure. Redraw that figure from memory on paper. If you cannot, you have not understood it yet.
  3. Pass 3: reproduce (hours to days, only for papers you build on). Derive the key equation yourself, line by line. Then implement the smallest unit of the method in code. Not the whole system: only the one component that carries the paper's idea. This pass turns “I read it” into “I understand it”. Code and math show you at once where your understanding has gaps; a quick read-through does not.
Check yourself You finish a 5-minute pass-1 skim and still cannot state the paper's main claim in one sentence. What should you do next? Reveal

Do not start a full pass 2 and hope it becomes clear. First look again at the pass-1 material, only for the claim. Reread the last sentences of the abstract and the first paragraph of the conclusion; the claim is usually stated plainly there. If it is still unclear, the paper may not suit you right now. Check whether it assumes background you do not have, or whether it is not the paper you need. Deciding this in 5 minutes is the purpose of pass one. It is much cheaper than finding out after an hour of pass two.

Check yourself A search gives you 20 papers on a topic, and you will build on about one of them. With 5, 45, and 600 minutes for the three passes, roughly how long does the funnel take if 4 papers reach pass 2? How does that compare with a full pass 3 on all 20? Reveal

Pass 1 on all 20: 100 minutes. Pass 2 on 4: 180 minutes. Pass 3 on 1: 600 minutes. That is about 880 minutes, or roughly 15 hours. A deep reading of all 20 papers would take 20 × 600 = 12,000 minutes, about 200 hours. The early passes are cheap filters that protect your time for the one paper that matters.

2What to extract from any paper

Intuition first

In almost any field, a research paper answers the same six questions. If you write down clear answers to these six, you have the paper's engineering value. You may never need the rest of the text again.

  • The problem. What did not work before, in one sentence? Not “language models are hard”, but something testable, such as “dense models need proportionally more compute for every increase in parameters.”
  • The key idea, in one sentence. If you cannot compress the contribution to one sentence, you have not found it yet. You are still describing the paper's title.
  • The central equation. The formula without which the rest of the paper would make no sense. Usually there is only one.
  • The architecture diagram. The picture that shows data flowing through the proposed system. If the paper has no diagram, draw one yourself from the method section. This shows you anything you skipped over.
  • The ablations: what they tell you the idea is for.
  • What they do not tell you: what the idea costs.
Definition

An ablation is an experiment where the authors remove or replace one piece of their system and measure performance again. It shows whether that piece was necessary. (“Ablate” means to cut away.)

☂ Everyday parallel

A driver wants to know whether new tires make her car faster. She drives the same lap, on the same day, with the old tires and then with the new ones, and changes nothing else. The difference in lap time is the effect of the tires. That second lap is her ablation: it is evidence, not a claim. A paper's ablation table does the same thing. It trains the same model several times, each time with one part removed, so you can see which parts matter.

▲ Watch out

Papers report what worked. They rarely report what it cost to get there. How many failed runs came before the reported one? How much hyperparameter tuning was needed? What hardware, and how much time, did the “efficient” method use? Which results did not reproduce on a different dataset? Read the “Limitations” section if there is one; it is often the most candid paragraph in the paper. If there is none, assume the costs exist and were left out. Look for them in the appendix or in the released code.

Check yourself A paper proposes a new component X and reports one number: “model with X gets 91% accuracy.” There is no ablation table. What is missing, and why does it matter? Reveal

The missing number is “model without X, everything else the same”. Without that baseline, 91% tells you nothing about whether X caused it. A larger learning rate or three extra epochs might have given 91% anyway. An ablation is not an optional extra; it is the evidence for the paper's claim. Treat a single number with no matched baseline with doubt, until you find the ablation or run it yourself.

3Dissecting the MoE / Switch Transformer papers

Intuition first

A Mixture of Experts (MoE) model replaces one big feed-forward layer with many smaller feed-forward layers, called experts. A small decision-maker, the router, picks which one or two experts handle each token. The goal is to grow the model's total number of parameters (its capacity to store knowledge) without growing the compute for each token. Each token visits only a few experts, not one very large layer.

☂ Everyday parallel

A large call center has dozens of specialists: billing, technical support, returns, legal. A caller never talks to all of them. A receptionist (the router) listens to the first sentence of the call and sends the caller to the one or two specialists (the experts) most likely to help. The center can add many specialists, while each call still takes the time of only one or two people. The router learns to make the receptionist's decision.

Two papers define most of the vocabulary you will meet here. The first is Shazeer et al., 2017, “Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer”. It introduced the modern MoE layer with noisy top-k gating. The router adds learned noise to its scores and keeps a small number $k$ of experts per token. Its experiments use values such as 2 and 4. The second is Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, 2021, “Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity”. It simplifies routing to one expert per token and uses it inside a Transformer. In between, GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) used top-2 routing in a large translation Transformer.

Definition

The router is a small learned layer, usually one linear layer plus a softmax. It looks at a token's vector and outputs a probability for every expert: “how well does this token suit expert 1, expert 2, …?” These probabilities decide the routing, and they also weight each chosen expert's output.

Now the math

The one equation worth deriving yourself from the Switch Transformer paper is the auxiliary load-balancing loss. It is added to the normal training loss. It stops the router from sending almost every token to the same one or two experts:

$$ L_{\text{aux}} = \lambda_{\text{aux}} \cdot E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \, P_i $$

In words: $E$ is the number of experts. $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens in the batch that were sent to expert $i$, so $\sum_i f_i = 1$. $P_i$ is the average probability the router gave to expert $i$ over the batch. $\lambda_{\text{aux}}$ is a small weight (the paper uses 0.01; it calls it $\alpha$). The factor $E$ makes the balanced value the same for any number of experts, as we show next.

Two cases make the formula concrete. With $E = 4$ experts and perfectly even routing, $f_i = P_i = 0.25$ for every expert:

$$ \sum_i f_i P_i = 4 \times (0.25 \times 0.25) = 0.25, \qquad L_{\text{aux}} = \lambda_{\text{aux}} \times 4 \times 0.25 = \lambda_{\text{aux}} $$

If instead every token goes to expert 1 ($f_1 = P_1 = 1$, the rest zero):

$$ \sum_i f_i P_i = 1, \qquad L_{\text{aux}} = \lambda_{\text{aux}} \times 4 \times 1 = 4\,\lambda_{\text{aux}} $$

In words: the balanced case costs $\lambda_{\text{aux}}$ and the fully collapsed case costs $E$ times more. When $f$ and $P$ agree, balance is the best case. With $f = P$ the sum is $\sum_i f_i^2$, and because the $f_i$ add up to 1, $\sum_i f_i^2 \ge 1/E$ (the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality), with equality only when every $f_i = 1/E$.

How does minimizing this loss change the router? The fractions $f_i$ come from a hard choice (which expert had the top score), so they have no gradient. The gradient flows only through the probabilities $P_i$:

$$ \frac{\partial L_{\text{aux}}}{\partial P_i} = \lambda_{\text{aux}} \, E \, f_i $$

In words: the more tokens expert $i$ received ($f_i$ large), the harder gradient descent pushes its router probability $P_i$ down. Experts that received few tokens are pushed down only a little, so they gain probability in comparison. Over many steps this moves tokens from busy experts toward idle ones.

Definition

The capacity factor sets how many tokens each expert may process in one batch. With $T$ tokens in the batch, $k$ experts per token, $E$ experts, and capacity factor $c$, each expert may take $C$ tokens:

$$ C = \frac{T \cdot k}{E} \times c $$

A factor of 1.0 gives each expert exactly its fair share, with no extra room. Tokens that arrive after an expert is full overflow: they skip that expert and pass through unchanged via the residual connection. A higher factor (say 1.25) gives extra room for uneven routing. The cost is reserved compute that is sometimes not used.

Definition

Top-k routing sends each token to its $k$ highest-scoring experts. Their outputs are combined, weighted by the router's probabilities. GShard uses top-2. Earlier work had assumed that a token must go to at least two experts for the router to learn well. Switch Transformer tested top-1 (one expert per token) and found that quality stayed good. Top-1 also reduces expert compute and the communication between devices.

Concrete questions to ask when you open one of these papers:

  • How many experts ($E$), and how does the compute per token compare with a dense model with the same total number of parameters?
  • What is $k$ (top-1, top-2, more), and what is the argument for that choice?
  • What is the capacity factor, and what happens to tokens that overflow?
  • How is $\lambda_{\text{aux}}$ chosen, and what happens in their ablation when it is set to zero?
  • Do they report a dropped-token rate or routing collapse? Either is a sign that load balancing was not fully solved.
  • Is the router trained end to end with the rest of the model, or does it use a fixed rule (for example, hashing tokens to experts)?

In Section 22 we implement exactly this from scratch in PyTorch: a router, a small set of expert feed-forward networks, and this load-balancing loss. Keep these six questions in mind; the next section answers each one in code.

Check yourself With $E = 4$ experts and $\lambda_{\text{aux}} = 0.01$, routing gives $f = P = (0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1)$. Compute the auxiliary loss and compare it with the balanced case. Reveal

$\sum_i f_i P_i = 0.7^2 + 3 \times 0.1^2 = 0.49 + 0.03 = 0.52$. So $L_{\text{aux}} = 0.01 \times 4 \times 0.52 = 0.0208$. The balanced case gives $L_{\text{aux}} = \lambda_{\text{aux}} = 0.01$, so this uneven routing costs about twice as much. The gradient on $P_1$ is $0.01 \times 4 \times 0.7 = 0.028$, seven times the gradient on each other expert. So training pushes expert 1's probability down the most.

Check yourself A batch has $T = 1024$ tokens, $E = 8$ experts, top-1 routing, and capacity factor 1.25. What is each expert's capacity? If 200 tokens choose expert 3, what happens? Reveal

$C = 1024 \cdot 1 / 8 \times 1.25 = 160$ tokens. Expert 3 processes 160 of its 200 tokens. The other 40 overflow: they skip the expert and continue unchanged through the residual connection. They are not sent to another expert and not tried again. The balancing loss exists to make such overflows rare.

4Practical habits that make it stick

Intuition first

The method above tells you how to read. These three habits help you keep what you learned.

  • Read the code alongside the paper. Many major papers release code, or a faithful reimplementation exists (search for the paper's title plus “github”). When the text says “we route tokens to their top-1 expert”, find the three lines of code that do it. Code cannot be vague the way prose can.
  • Reproduce the smallest unit, not the whole system. You do not need to train a trillion-parameter Switch Transformer to understand it. You need to implement one router that chooses among four tiny experts on made-up data. If that ten-line version behaves as the paper predicts, you understand the mechanism.
  • Keep a short note per paper: one line for the key idea, the central equation copied by hand (not pasted), and one open question you still cannot answer. Look at the open questions again after you read the next paper in the area. Papers often answer each other's questions.
☂ Everyday parallel

A musician who cannot play one fast passage does not play the whole piece again and again. She practices those four bars alone, slowly, until she knows why her fingers get stuck. Then she puts them back into the piece. Do the same here: build the router alone, on toy data, before you build the model it belongs to.

The same thing in PyTorch

the smallest reproducible unit: a top-1 router and its balancing loss

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
E = 4          # number of experts
d_model = 8

router = torch.nn.Linear(d_model, E)     # the whole "decision-maker"
tokens = torch.randn(6, d_model)         # 6 toy tokens, nothing real

logits = router(tokens)                  # (6, 4): one score per expert, per token
probs = torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
chosen = probs.argmax(dim=-1)            # (6,): top-1 routing decision
print(chosen)                            # tensor([1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 1])

f = torch.bincount(chosen, minlength=E) / len(chosen)   # fraction of tokens per expert
P = probs.mean(dim=0)                                    # mean router probability per expert
lam_aux = 0.01
aux = lam_aux * E * (f * P).sum()
print(f)                                 # tensor([0.0000, 0.5000, 0.3333, 0.1667])
print(round(aux.item(), 4))              # 0.0102

In words: this is the core routing decision and the balancing loss, nothing more. Expert 1 gets half the tokens and expert 0 gets none, yet the loss is only a little above the balanced value 0.01. The reason: the untrained router's probabilities $P$ are still almost even (about 0.23 to 0.27 each). Section 22 adds the experts, the output weighting, the capacity limit, and training.

Check yourself In the code above, f is computed from argmax. If you call aux.backward(), which tensor does the gradient reach the router through, and why? Reveal

Only through P. argmax and bincount return integer results with no gradient, so f acts as a constant. P is the mean of the softmax output, which depends smoothly on the router weights. This is the equation $\partial L_{\text{aux}} / \partial P_i = \lambda_{\text{aux}} E f_i$ from above, now in code.

5What to carry forward

  • Three passes, increasing depth: skim for relevance, read the structure and the one key equation, then reproduce the smallest unit to understand it.
  • Extract six things from any paper: the problem, the key idea in one sentence, the central equation, the architecture diagram, the ablations, and what it does not tell you.
  • An ablation shows a component matters by removing it and measuring again. A single number with no matched baseline shows nothing.
  • MoE vocabulary: a router scores each token against the $E$ experts. Top-k routing sends it to $k$ of them (top-1 in Switch Transformer, top-2 in GShard). The capacity factor limits how many tokens each expert takes. The load-balancing loss $\lambda_{\text{aux}} E \sum_i f_i P_i$ penalizes uneven routing.

You now have the method and the vocabulary. In Section 22 we stop reading about a Mixture of Experts and build one.
