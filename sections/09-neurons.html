Neurons, Layers, Activations

A neuron is a weighted vote passed through a switch — and without that switch, depth is a lie.

28 min read
You'll learn
  • the artificial neuron
  • layers as matrices
  • activation functions
  • why nonlinearity is essential

Everything you have built so far — vectors, matrices, dot products — was preparation for this one small object: the neuron. A neuron takes a handful of numbers, weighs them, adds them up, and then passes the result through a little switch. Wire a few thousand of these together and you have a neural network. This section builds the neuron, stacks neurons into layers, and then proves the single most important fact about them: the switch is not optional. Remove it and a hundred layers collapse into one.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

One neuron is $\text{activation}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b)$ — a weighted sum passed through a nonlinear switch. A layer runs many neurons at once, which is just a matrix multiply. And the switch — the activation — is the only thing that lets stacking layers buy you real power. That's the whole section; everything below is why.

1The artificial neuron: a weighted vote through a switch

Intuition first

A neuron makes one small decision. It looks at several input numbers, decides how much each one matters, adds up its weighted opinion, and then squashes that total through a switch that decides how strongly to “fire.” Big weighted total → strong output. Small or negative total → weak or zero output. That's it. The cleverness of a network is not in any one neuron; it's in wiring many simple ones together.

☂ Everyday parallel

Deciding whether to go outside is a weighted vote. The weather matters a lot to you (big weight), whether you have plans matters somewhat (medium weight), your energy level matters a little (small weight). You tally up the weighted evidence — and you only actually get off the couch once that tally crosses some personal threshold. Below the threshold you stay in; above it you go. A neuron is exactly that: weigh the factors, sum them, fire only past a point.

Now the math

Write the inputs as a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ (a list of n numbers). Give the neuron a matching list of weights $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and a single extra number b, the bias. The neuron computes:

$$ y = \text{activation}\!\left( \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b \right) = \text{activation}\!\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b \right) $$

In words: the piece $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$ is just the dot product from Section 1 — multiply each input by its weight and add the results, giving one number (“how much do I care, in total?”). Then add the bias b to shift that number up or down, and finally push the whole thing through the activation — the switch — to get the neuron's output y.

Definition

A weight $w_i$ says how much input $x_i$ matters, and in which direction — a large positive weight means “this input pushes the output up,” a negative weight means “this input pushes it down.” The bias b is a constant offset added to every input; it sets the neuron's default lean, i.e. how big the weighted sum must be before the neuron starts to fire. The activation is a fixed nonlinear function — the switch — applied to that sum.

The raw number before the activation, $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$, shows up so often it has a name: the pre-activation (some texts call it the logit when it is the final score before a probability). Keep the two apart in your head: z is the weighted vote, y = activation(z) is the vote after the switch.

The same thing in PyTorch

one neuron by hand

import torch

x = torch.tensor([0.6, -1.2, 2.0])     # three inputs
w = torch.tensor([1.5,  0.3, -0.8])    # a weight per input
b = torch.tensor(0.1)                  # one bias

z = torch.dot(w, x) + b                # pre-activation: the weighted vote
y = torch.relu(z)                      # push it through the switch (ReLU)

print(z)     # tensor(-1.05)   0.6*1.5 + (-1.2)*0.3 + 2.0*(-0.8) + 0.1
print(y)     # tensor(0.)      negative vote -> neuron stays silent
Check yourself If every weight in a neuron were 0, what would its output be for any input, and what does that tell you about the bias? Reveal

With all weights zero, $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} = 0$ no matter what x is, so the pre-activation is just b and the output is a constant $\text{activation}(b)$ — the neuron ignores its inputs entirely. That's the point of the bias: it's the neuron's output when the weighted evidence is silent, its baseline lean. Weights decide how the inputs move the output; the bias decides where the output sits when the inputs say nothing.

2Layers: many neurons at once is one matrix multiply

Intuition first

One neuron gives you one number. Usually you want many — one neuron asking “does this look like a wheel?”, another “does this look like a window?”, another “is there fur?”. A layer is simply a row of neurons, all looking at the same input at the same time, each with its own weights and bias, each producing its own number. Their outputs stack into a new vector, which becomes the input to the next layer.

☂ Everyday parallel

A loan application lands on a desk with a panel of reviewers. One reviewer cares mostly about income, another mostly about credit history, another about existing debt. They all read the same application but weigh it differently, and each writes down a score. The panel's set of scores is richer than any single reviewer's opinion. A layer is that panel: same input to everyone, different weights per member, a vector of scores out.

Now the math

Here is the beautiful part. Stacking neurons doesn't need any new machinery — it is exactly the matrix–vector multiply from Section 1. Put each neuron's weight vector as a row of a matrix W, collect the biases into a vector b, and one line does the whole layer:

$$ \mathbf{h} = \text{activation}\!\left( \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \right) $$

In words: row i of W is the weights of neuron i, so $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ computes every neuron's weighted vote in a single multiply (one dot product per row). Add the bias vector, apply the activation to every slot, and out comes h — the layer's output vector, one number per neuron.

The shapes are the whole story, so let's pin them down. Suppose the input has n features and the layer has m neurons:

$$ \underbrace{\mathbf{W}}_{m \times n}\; \underbrace{\mathbf{x}}_{n \times 1} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbf{b}}_{m \times 1} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbf{h}}_{m \times 1} $$

In words: W has one row per neuron (m rows) and one column per input feature (n columns). By the shape rule — inner numbers match, outer numbers survive — an $(m\times n)$ matrix times an $(n\times 1)$ vector gives an $(m\times 1)$ vector. So a layer maps n numbers in to m numbers out; you pick m when you design the network.

In practice you rarely push one example through at a time. You send a whole batch (a bundle of examples processed together for speed). Stack N input vectors as the rows of a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ of shape $(N \times n)$, and the layer becomes $\mathbf{H} = \text{activation}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^\top + \mathbf{b})$, producing an $(N \times m)$ output — one row of answers per example. (This row-of-examples convention is why PyTorch's Linear layer stores its weight as $(m \times n)$ and multiplies by $\mathbf{W}^\top$ under the hood.)

Check yourself A layer takes a 784-length image vector and has 128 neurons. What are the shapes of W, b, and the output h for a single image? Reveal

The layer maps $n = 784$ inputs to $m = 128$ neurons. So W is (128, 784) — one row per neuron, one column per input feature — the bias b is (128,) — one per neuron — and the output h is (128,). The 784 “cancels” in the multiply and the 128 survives. You've just sized the first hidden layer of an image classifier.

3Activation functions: the shape of the switch

Intuition first

The activation is the switch each neuron passes its weighted vote through. Different switches have different personalities — some are soft and gentle, some are hard on/off, some let strong signals through untouched. The choice matters because it decides two things: what range the output can take, and — crucially for learning — how the gradient (the slope that tells training which way to nudge the weights, from backprop) behaves. A switch whose slope goes flat is a switch through which learning cannot flow.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a dimmer versus a light switch. A dimmer (like sigmoid or tanh) eases smoothly from off to fully on, but near the two ends it barely responds — twist it a lot and almost nothing changes. A plain light switch is hard on/off. And a ReLU is like a diode: it blocks anything negative completely, but passes positive signal straight through at full strength, no dimming. Each feels different to “tune,” and that tuning feel is the gradient.

Now the math

The sigmoid squashes any number into the range (0, 1) — handy when you want an output you can read as a probability:

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \qquad \text{range } (0, 1) $$

In words: huge positive z gives an output near 1, huge negative z gives near 0, and $z = 0$ gives exactly 0.5. The catch: at both far ends the curve flattens out, so its slope (gradient) shrinks to nearly zero — this is called saturation, and it starves learning.

The tanh is a rescaled sigmoid centred on zero, with range (−1, 1):

$$ \tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} \qquad \text{range } (-1, 1) $$

In words: same S-shape as sigmoid but symmetric around zero, so its outputs are centred (a negative input gives a negative output). That centering makes it a bit nicer to train than sigmoid — but it still saturates and flattens at the two ends.

The ReLU (“rectified linear unit”) is almost embarrassingly simple: keep the input if it's positive, otherwise output zero.

$$ \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) \qquad \text{range } [0, \infty) $$

In words: for any positive z the output is just z and the slope is exactly 1 — the gradient passes through undamped, so learning never stalls on the positive side. For negative z the output is 0 and the slope is 0 (the neuron is “off”). No expensive $e^{x}$, no saturation for positive inputs — that combination is why ReLU became the default hidden-layer activation.

ReLU has one flaw: a neuron stuck in the negative zone has zero gradient and may never wake up — a dead neuron. Leaky ReLU fixes this by letting a small trickle through on the negative side instead of hard zero:

$$ \text{LeakyReLU}(z) = \max(\alpha z,\; z), \qquad \alpha \approx 0.01 \qquad \text{range } (-\infty, \infty) $$

In words: positive inputs behave exactly like ReLU (slope 1); negative inputs get a gentle slope α (say 0.01) instead of 0, so the neuron always keeps a faint gradient and can recover instead of dying. α (alpha) is just the small leak factor.

Finally, worth knowing by name: GELU (“Gaussian Error Linear Unit”) is a smooth, curved cousin of ReLU that bends gently through zero instead of kinking sharply. It is the default activation inside modern transformer models (the architecture behind today's large language models). You don't need its formula yet — just recognise the name when it appears later.

The same thing in PyTorch

every switch on the same inputs

import torch

z = torch.tensor([-2.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 2.0])

print(torch.sigmoid(z))                    # (0,1):  [0.12, 0.38, 0.50, 0.62, 0.88]
print(torch.tanh(z))                       # (-1,1): [-0.96, -0.46, 0.00, 0.46, 0.96]
print(torch.relu(z))                       # [0,inf):[0.00, 0.00, 0.00, 0.50, 2.00]
print(torch.nn.functional.leaky_relu(z))   # negatives leak: [-0.02, -0.005, 0, 0.5, 2]
▲ Watch out

Don't reach for sigmoid or tanh in deep hidden layers by default. Because they flatten at the ends, stacking many of them makes gradients shrink layer after layer until the early layers barely learn — the vanishing gradient problem. Sigmoid still has a proper home: the final layer of a binary classifier, where you genuinely want a 0-to-1 probability. For the hidden layers in between, start with ReLU.

Check yourself A neuron uses ReLU and its pre-activation is z = −3.0. What is its output, and what is the gradient flowing back through it during training? Reveal

Since $z = -3.0 \lt 0$, $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, -3) = 0$ — the neuron is silent. And ReLU's slope for negative inputs is 0, so no gradient flows back through this neuron on this example: it learns nothing from it. If the neuron sits in the negative zone for every example, it's a dead neuron that never recovers — the exact problem Leaky ReLU's small negative slope is designed to prevent.

4Why nonlinearity is not optional

Intuition first

Here is the claim, and it is worth proving because it justifies the entire existence of activation functions: if you stack layers with no activation between them, the whole stack is exactly equal to a single layer. A hundred plain matrix multiplies in a row have no more power than one. The switch is the only thing that makes depth mean anything.

☂ Everyday parallel

Lay straight rulers end to end, as many as you like. Every ruler is straight, so the whole chain is still one straight line — you never bend it into a curve, no matter how many rulers you add. A linear layer is a straight ruler. To draw a curve you need a bend somewhere, and the activation is the only place a bend can enter. No bend, no curve; no nonlinearity, no real depth.

Now the math

Take two layers with no activation. The first maps $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{h}$, the second maps $\mathbf{h}$ to $\mathbf{y}$:

$$ \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_2 $$

Now substitute the first equation into the second and simplify, one honest step at a time:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_2 \\[2pt] &= \mathbf{W}_2\big(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1\big) + \mathbf{b}_2 &&\text{(plug in } \mathbf{h}\text{)} \\[2pt] &= \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2 &&\text{(multiply through)} \\[2pt] &= \underbrace{\big(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1\big)}_{\mathbf{W}'}\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{\big(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2\big)}_{\mathbf{b}'} &&\text{(group the constants)} \\[2pt] &= \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}' \end{aligned} $$

In words: the two weight matrices multiply together into one new matrix $\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1$, and the two biases fold into one new bias $\mathbf{b}' = \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2$. The result, $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}'$, is a single linear layer. The two-layer network and this one-layer network compute the identical function.

◆ The punchline

Nothing in that derivation used the number two. Chain 100 activation-free layers and the same collapse happens 99 times over: the product $\mathbf{W}_{100}\cdots\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1$ is just some matrix $\mathbf{W}'$, and all the biases fold into one $\mathbf{b}'$. A 100-layer linear network is exactly as expressive as one layer — it can only ever draw straight lines (technically, flat planes). All those extra parameters buy you nothing. The activation is what breaks the collapse: because $\text{activation}(\mathbf{W}_2\,\text{activation}(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x}))$ can't be flattened into one matrix, each real layer adds genuine new power.

So what can a network do once you put the nonlinearity back? Far more than you might guess. The universal approximation theorem states that a network with just one hidden layer — as long as it has a nonlinear activation and enough neurons — can approximate any continuous function to any accuracy you want. “Approximate” means get as close as you like everywhere; “continuous function” means any smooth input-to-output rule without sudden jumps. In plain terms: the bend the activation provides, repeated across enough neurons, can be assembled into any curve at all. That theorem is why neural networks are worth building — and it is silent, worthless, the moment you delete the activation.

The same thing in PyTorch

watch two linear layers collapse into one

import torch

W1 = torch.randn(4, 3); b1 = torch.randn(4)
W2 = torch.randn(2, 4); b2 = torch.randn(2)
x  = torch.randn(3)

# two "layers" with NO activation between them
y_two = W2 @ (W1 @ x + b1) + b2

# the single equivalent layer, built from the proof
W_eq = W2 @ W1                 # (2x4)(4x3) -> (2x3)
b_eq = W2 @ b1 + b2            # folded bias
y_one = W_eq @ x + b_eq

print(torch.allclose(y_two, y_one))   # True  -> same function, one layer
Check yourself A colleague builds a 10-layer network but forgets to add any activation functions. During training the loss drops a little then plateaus, and the model does no better than plain linear regression. Why? Reveal

Without activations, all ten layers collapse into a single linear map $\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}'$ by the proof above. The model literally is a linear model, so it can never beat one — the nine extra layers add parameters but zero expressive power. It plateaus wherever a straight-line fit plateaus. The fix is one line per layer: wrap each hidden layer's output in a nonlinearity like ReLU, and the collapse can no longer happen.

5A real layer in PyTorch

Intuition first

You now have every piece: weights, bias, matrix multiply, activation. PyTorch bundles the weighted sum and bias into nn.Linear and keeps the activation separate, so you compose them yourself. Let's run a batch through one hidden layer with ReLU and watch the shapes flow — the shapes are the proof that everything above is wired correctly.

☂ Everyday parallel

It's a two-stage assembly line. Station one (Linear) takes each incoming part, weighs and combines it, and hands on a rough result. Station two (ReLU) is quality control: anything below the bar is set to zero, the rest passes through unchanged. Same two stations run on every item on the belt (every example in the batch), in lockstep.

The same thing in PyTorch

a hidden layer's forward pass, with shapes

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

batch_size, in_features, out_features = 32, 784, 128

layer = nn.Linear(in_features, out_features)   # holds W (128x784) and b (128,)
relu  = nn.ReLU()

x = torch.randn(batch_size, in_features)       # a batch: 32 examples of 784 numbers
print("input :", x.shape)                      # torch.Size([32, 784])

z = layer(x)                                   # weighted sum + bias:  x @ W.T + b
print("linear:", z.shape)                      # torch.Size([32, 128])

h = relu(z)                                    # apply the switch element-wise
print("relu  :", h.shape)                      # torch.Size([32, 128])

# proof the activation actually clipped the negatives:
print("min after relu:", h.min().item())       # 0.0  -> nothing negative survives

In words: the batch goes in as $(32, 784)$, Linear turns each 784-number example into 128 numbers giving $(32, 128)$, and ReLU keeps that shape while zeroing every negative entry. Each row is one example's journey through the layer; the batch dimension (32) just rides along untouched.

▲ Watch out

The in_features you pass to nn.Linear must equal the last dimension of your input, and the next layer's in_features must equal this layer's out_features. Mismatch and PyTorch throws a shape error the instant you call the layer. When a network won't run, print the shape after every layer — the break is always where two numbers that should match, don't.

6What to carry forward

  • A neuron is $\text{activation}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b)$ — a weighted vote (weights say how much each input matters) plus a bias (baseline lean), pushed through a nonlinear switch.
  • A layer runs many neurons at once and is just one matrix multiply: $\mathbf{h} = \text{activation}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$, mapping n inputs to m outputs.
  • Activations differ by output range and gradient behaviour. ReLU ($\max(0,z)$) is the default hidden-layer switch: cheap and non-saturating for positive inputs. Sigmoid/tanh saturate; Leaky ReLU avoids dead neurons; GELU is the transformer default.
  • The proof: stacked linear layers with no activation collapse to one layer — $\mathbf{W}'\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}'$. Nonlinearity is what makes depth real, and with it an MLP can approximate any continuous function.

We have a network that can compute a forward pass. But its weights are random, so its answers are nonsense. Next we learn how to fix those weights — how the gradient flows back through every neuron and every activation to teach the whole stack at once. That's backpropagation, and it's where the network finally starts to learn.
