Sequence Models: RNNs & LSTMs

A recurrent network reads one step at a time and carries a memory forward — and the very thing that makes that possible is also what makes it so hard to train over long stretches.

24 min read You'll learn: RNNs & hidden state · unrolling · vanishing/exploding gradients · LSTMs · why we moved on

Convolutional networks in Section 16 assume their input is a grid where nearby pixels matter, but position order isn't inherently meaningful in any particular direction. Text, audio, and time series are different: they're sequences, where order carries the meaning. “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” use the same words in a different order to say something completely different. Recurrent networks are the first architecture built specifically to respect that.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

A recurrent network reads a sequence one element at a time and keeps a running hidden state — a summary of everything seen so far — updating it at every step. That single reused update rule, applied over and over, is both the source of its power and, as you'll see, the source of its biggest weakness.

1RNNs: a hidden state that carries memory forward

Intuition first

A recurrent neural network (RNN) processes a sequence step by step — one word, one audio sample, one time-tick at a time. At each step it looks at the current input and a running summary of everything it has seen before (the hidden state), and produces an updated summary to carry into the next step.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of reading a sentence word by word, out loud, to someone who can't see the page. By the time you reach the word “bank” in “I walked along the river bank,” you already have a running sense of the sentence's meaning from the words before it — that running sense is what lets you interpret “bank” as a riverbank, not a place to deposit money. You don't re-read the whole sentence from scratch at every word; you carry a summary forward and update it. That carried summary is the hidden state.

Now the math

At each time step t, the network takes the current input x_t and the previous hidden state h_{t-1}, and produces the new hidden state h_t:

$$ \mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\!\left( \mathbf{W} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b} \right) $$

In words: W is a matrix that transforms the current input (exactly the $\mathbf{Wx}$ from Section 1); U is a matrix that transforms the previous hidden state, folding in what's been remembered so far; b is a bias vector; and tanh squashes the result into a bounded range so the hidden state can't grow without limit. Crucially, the same W, U, and b are reused at every single time step — one small set of weights processes a sequence of any length, just like one convolution kernel is reused at every position in Section 16.

Unrolling and backprop-through-time

To picture what's happening — and to train the network — it helps to draw the RNN “unrolled”: one copy of the same update rule per time step, laid out left to right, each feeding its hidden state into the next.

$$ x_1 \to h_1 \to h_2 \to h_3 \to \dots \to h_T $$

In words: it looks like a very deep feedforward network — except every copy shares the exact same weights W and U. Training uses ordinary backpropagation (see Section 12) applied to this unrolled picture, which is why it's called backpropagation through time: the error at the last step has to flow backward through every earlier time step, through the same weight matrix, again and again, to reach the beginning of the sequence.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

rnn = nn.RNN(input_size=8, hidden_size=16, batch_first=True)

x = torch.randn(4, 10, 8)          # (batch=4, sequence length=10, features=8)
h0 = torch.zeros(1, 4, 16)         # initial hidden state, all zeros

outputs, h_last = rnn(x, h0)
print(outputs.shape)   # torch.Size([4, 10, 16]) -> hidden state at every one of the 10 steps
print(h_last.shape)    # torch.Size([1, 4, 16])  -> just the final hidden state
Check yourself Why can the same RNN, with a fixed number of parameters, process both a 5-word sentence and a 500-word sentence? Reveal

Because the parameters (W, U, b) don't describe the whole sequence — they describe a single update step, applied once per token regardless of how many tokens there are. A 500-word sentence just runs that same small update 500 times instead of 5, reusing identical weights each time. This is the sequence analogue of a convolution kernel being reused at every pixel position rather than needing one new kernel per image size.

2The failure mode: vanishing and exploding gradients

Intuition first

Here's the problem with reusing the same weight matrix at every step: backpropagation through time has to multiply by that same matrix once per time step, all the way back to the start of the sequence. Multiplying by the same thing over and over doesn't stay neutral — it compounds, the same way repeatedly applying a stretch or a shrink compounds.

☂ Everyday parallel

Whisper a sentence down a long line of people, and by the tenth person it's already garbled — small distortions compound with every relay. Now imagine instead that each person is instructed to say everything 5% louder than they heard it: after twenty people, the message is a deafening roar. Repeated small changes, applied over and over along a long chain, either fade to nothing or blow up — there's rarely a stable middle.

Now the math

Recall from Section 1 that a matrix's eigenvalues are the stretch factors along its favoured directions, and that applying the same matrix repeatedly raises those stretch factors to a power. Backpropagation through time does exactly this to the recurrent weight matrix U: the gradient flowing back T steps involves a term that looks roughly like U multiplied by itself T times.

$$ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_0} \;\approx\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} U^\top \,\cdot\, \text{(local terms)} $$

In words: the gradient connecting the last hidden state back to the first has to pass through T copies of (roughly) the same matrix, multiplied together. If U's dominant eigenvalue is less than 1, that product shrinks toward zero as T grows — the vanishing gradient problem, identical in spirit to the eigenvalue example in Section 1 and the depth problem in Section 16. If it's greater than 1, the product blows up — the exploding gradient problem instead. Either way, the network's early time steps get almost no usable training signal, which means it struggles to learn any dependency that spans a long stretch of the sequence (see Section 12 for how this plays out during training).

▲ Watch out

This is exactly why plain RNNs are bad at tasks like “does the subject introduced at the start of a long paragraph agree with the verb at the end?” — by the time the gradient (or, at inference time, the hidden state itself) has been pushed through dozens of update steps, information about that early subject has often faded to nearly nothing.

Check yourself If the recurrent weight matrix's dominant eigenvalue is 0.9, roughly what fraction of the original gradient survives after 50 time steps, and what does that imply about learning long-range dependencies? Reveal

0.9^50 ≈ 0.005 — about half a percent of the original signal survives. Any dependency that requires connecting something 50 steps back to the current step gets a gradient signal roughly 200 times weaker than a dependency just one step back, so the network effectively can't learn it: the training signal needed to strengthen that long-range connection is drowned out before it arrives. This is the concrete reason plain RNNs are said to have a "short memory" in practice, even though the hidden state is, in principle, carrying information forward from every step.

3LSTMs: gates that choose what to remember

Intuition first

The LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) is an RNN variant designed directly against the vanishing-gradient problem. Instead of squashing everything through one tanh update every step, it keeps a separate memory cell and uses three learned gates — small networks that output a number between 0 and 1 — to control, at every step, what gets forgotten, what gets added, and what gets read out.

☂ Everyday parallel

Picture keeping a notebook while reading a long report. At every page you make three deliberate decisions: what old notes to cross out because they're no longer relevant (the forget gate), what new fact to write down (the input gate), and how much of what's in the notebook to actually read back out right now to use (the output gate). The notebook itself — the memory cell — persists across pages largely untouched except where you deliberately edit it, instead of being rewritten from scratch every single page.

Definition

A gate, in an LSTM, is a small learned layer (a sigmoid, so its output is always between 0 and 1) that acts as a per-element “volume knob”: near 0 blocks information from passing, near 1 lets it through unchanged. The forget, input, and output gates are three independent such knobs, each learned to serve a different job.

We won't derive the full LSTM update here — it's three gates plus a candidate memory update, and the algebra is more bookkeeping than new insight. The one thing worth carrying forward: the memory cell is updated additively (old memory times the forget gate, plus new information times the input gate), which is structurally similar to the $x + F(x)$ skip connection from Section 16 — an additive path that lets gradients flow back through many steps without being forced through a repeated multiplication every time. This is what eases (though doesn't fully solve) the vanishing-gradient problem from the section above.

The same thing in PyTorch

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=8, hidden_size=16, batch_first=True)

x = torch.randn(4, 10, 8)           # (batch=4, sequence length=10, features=8)
h0 = torch.zeros(1, 4, 16)
c0 = torch.zeros(1, 4, 16)          # LSTMs carry a memory cell alongside the hidden state

outputs, (h_last, c_last) = lstm(x, (h0, c0))
print(outputs.shape)  # torch.Size([4, 10, 16])
print(c_last.shape)   # torch.Size([1, 4, 16])  -> the memory cell, separate from the hidden state
Check yourself A plain RNN updates its entire hidden state by squashing it through tanh every step. Why does an LSTM's additive memory-cell update ease the vanishing-gradient problem better than that? Reveal

A plain RNN's hidden state is rewritten at every step by passing it through a matrix multiply and a squashing nonlinearity — the gradient backward through that step always has to go through that multiplication, which compounds over many steps exactly as shown above. An LSTM's memory cell is updated additively (forget-gated old memory plus input-gated new information), so there's a path for the gradient that doesn't require repeated multiplication by the same matrix — much like the identity path in a residual connection. The gates still have to learn good values, so LSTMs don't eliminate the problem, but the additive path gives gradients a much easier route over long sequences than a plain RNN offers.

4Why we moved on: the case for attention

Intuition first

LSTMs eased the gradient problem, but they left a different limitation untouched: an RNN, by construction, must process a sequence one step at a time — step 50 needs the hidden state from step 49, which needs step 48, all the way back to the start. You cannot compute step 50 before step 49 is done.

☂ Everyday parallel

It's the difference between one person reading a book aloud, page by page, versus handing every page to a different reader in a room and letting them all read simultaneously, then comparing notes. The second approach only works if readers can directly reference any other page whenever they need to — not just whatever the previous reader happened to pass along.

Now the math

This sequential dependency has a very concrete cost: on modern hardware (GPUs), which are built to do enormous numbers of independent calculations at once, an RNN can't use that parallelism across the time dimension — step 50's calculation must wait for step 49's to finish, no matter how many idle processors are sitting around. Training on long sequences becomes slow simply from this forced serial order, on top of the fact that even LSTMs still degrade on very long-range dependencies.

◆ The opening for attention

What if, instead of forcing information to pass through every intermediate step to get from position 3 to position 300, a model could let position 300 look directly at position 3 — no relay required, and no inherent obstacle to computing many positions at once? That's the idea Section 18 builds on: attention mechanisms and the transformer architecture, which replace the sequential hidden state with a way for every position to directly attend to every other position.

5What to carry forward

  • An RNN reads a sequence step by step, keeping a hidden state that's updated by the same reused weights ($\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b})$) at every step.
  • Unrolling turns the recurrence into a deep chain of identical steps; training it is backpropagation through time.
  • Because the same weight matrix is applied repeatedly, gradients over long sequences vanish or explode depending on that matrix's eigenvalues — the same phenomenon as Section 1's eigenvalues and Section 16's deep-network gradient problem.
  • LSTMs add gates and an additive memory cell to ease (not eliminate) that problem, at the cost of more parameters and computation per step.
  • RNNs (and LSTMs) are inherently sequential — they can't be parallelized across time steps — which, together with their still-limited long-range memory, is exactly what motivates attention and transformers next.
