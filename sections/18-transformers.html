Attention & Transformers

The architecture behind every modern LLM — derived, not diagrammed, with every matrix shape spelled out. By the end you could rebuild the core of GPT from memory.

42 min read You'll learn: scaled dot-product attention (from scratch) · multi-head attention · positional encodings · the full transformer block, dimension by dimension

Every large language model you have heard of — GPT, Claude, Llama, Gemini — is a tall stack of one repeated part: the transformer block. And the beating heart of that block is a single operation called attention. If you truly understand attention, the rest of modern AI stops being magic and becomes engineering. So we are going to build it here from nothing but the dot product you already know, and we will write down the shape of every single matrix as we go — because in this subject, the shapes are the understanding.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Attention lets every position in a sequence look directly at every other position and pull in the information it needs — all at once, in parallel. That single capability is what replaced the slow, forgetful recurrent networks of Section 17 and unlocked models with billions of parameters. Everything below is the detail of how that lookup is computed.

1Why we needed something new

Intuition first

In Section 17 we met recurrent neural networks (RNNs): models that read a sentence one word at a time, carrying a running summary — a hidden state (a fixed-size memory vector) — from each word to the next. They work, but they fight two problems at once.

  • Long-range forgetting. To connect the last word of a paragraph to the first, the information must survive being squeezed through every step in between. It fades — the vanishing-gradient problem we previewed in Section 1.
  • No parallelism. Word 50 cannot be processed until word 49 is done, because it needs the hidden state coming out of word 49. Reading is strictly sequential, so it cannot be spread across the thousands of cores in a modern GPU. Training is slow.

We want the impossible-sounding combination: direct connections between far-apart words (so nothing has to survive a long relay) and the ability to process every word at the same time (so training is fast). Attention delivers both, and it does so with an idea that turns out to be surprisingly simple.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine reading a mystery novel two ways. The RNN way: you may only keep a single sticky-note of memory, and you must rewrite it after every sentence — by the last chapter the early clues are smudged beyond reading. The attention way: the whole book lies open on a huge table, and to understand any sentence you may glance directly at any other sentence, however far back, instantly. No relay, no fading, and you can have a room full of readers each studying a different sentence at the same moment.

Check yourself In the sentence “The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big,” what does “it” refer to, and why is that hard for an RNN? Reveal

“It” means the trophy (change “big” to “small” and it flips to the suitcase — the meaning depends on distant words). To resolve it, the model must link “it” back to “trophy,” which sits many words earlier. An RNN can only reach that word through the whole chain of hidden states in between, where the signal weakens. Attention lets “it” look straight at “trophy” in one hop, no matter the distance. This exact example is the classic test of long-range reference.

2Scaled dot-product attention, from scratch

Intuition first

Here is the whole mechanism in one picture, and it is a picture of a lookup. Every position asks a question, every position advertises what it is about, and every position offers some content. A position collects its answer by taking a blend of everyone's content, weighted by how well their advertisement matches its question. That is attention. Three roles do all the work, and they have names:

  • The query ($\mathbf{q}$) — what this position is looking for. (“I'm a pronoun; I need the noun I refer to.”)
  • The key ($\mathbf{k}$) — a label each position advertises about itself. (“I'm a concrete singular noun.”)
  • The value ($\mathbf{v}$) — the actual content a position hands over once it is selected. (“Here is my meaning: trophy.”)
☂ Everyday parallel

A library search. Your query is the phrase you type into the catalogue. Each book on the shelf has a spine label — its key — and its actual pages — its value. A normal computer picks the single book whose label matches best and hands you exactly that one. Attention is the soft version: instead of one winner, it returns a smooth blend — 70% of this book's pages, 20% of that one's, 10% of a third — weighted by how well each spine label matched what you asked for. It is a soft dictionary lookup: a dictionary returns one value for an exact key, attention returns a weighted mixture of all values, graded by key similarity.

Now the math

We start from an input we already have: a sequence of vectors, one per position (per word), stacked into a matrix. Call it $\mathbf{X}$.

$$ \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}} $$

In words: X has one row per position. T is the sequence length — how many words (or tokens) we are processing. dmodel is the model dimension — the length of the vector we use to represent each word (in the original transformer, 512). So X is T rows, each row a dmodel-long vector.

Now the one trick: we do not use $\mathbf{X}$ directly as query, key, and value. We project it into three different spaces with three learned weight matrices. This lets the model decide, on its own, what makes a good question, a good label, and good content — they need not be the same.

$$ \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q, \qquad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K, \qquad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V $$

Track every shape with the shape rule from Section 1 (inner dimensions cancel, outer survive):

ObjectShapeWhat it is
$\mathbf{W}_Q$$d_{\text{model}} \times d_k$learned: how to build a query
$\mathbf{W}_K$$d_{\text{model}} \times d_k$learned: how to build a key
$\mathbf{W}_V$$d_{\text{model}} \times d_v$learned: how to build a value
$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q$$T \times d_k$one query per position
$\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K$$T \times d_k$one key per position
$\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V$$T \times d_v$one value per position

In words: dk is the length of each query and key (they must match, because we are about to compare them); dv is the length of each value. Query and key live in the same space so they can be measured against each other; the value can be any size. Very often $d_k = d_v$, and we'll assume that from now on to keep the picture clean.

Step 1 — scores: how well does each query match each key?

To compare query $i$ with key $j$ we use the dot product from Section 1 — our alignment meter. A big dot product means “this query and this key point the same way; strong match.” We want every query against every key, and that is exactly one matrix multiply:

$$ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top} \qquad \text{shape } (T \times d_k)(d_k \times T) = (T \times T) $$

In words: K⊤ is K transposed (rows and columns swapped), so the shared dk lines up and cancels. Entry $S_{ij}$ is the dot product of query i with key j — a single number saying “how much should position i attend to position j.” The result is a square L×L table: every position scored against every position. This one matrix is where the “look at everything at once” promise is kept.

Step 2 — the scaling, and why it is not optional

We do not use $\mathbf{S}$ raw. We divide it by $\sqrt{d_k}$. This looks like a fudge factor; it is actually forced on us by statistics. Let us derive it, because it is the kind of detail this course refuses to hand-wave.

Suppose the entries of a query $\mathbf{q}$ and a key $\mathbf{k}$ are independent random numbers, each with mean $0$ and variance $1$ (a fair starting assumption for freshly initialised weights). Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ products:

$$ \mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i\,k_i $$

Take one term $q_i k_i$. Because $q_i$ and $k_i$ are independent with mean 0:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[q_i k_i] &= \mathbb{E}[q_i]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0, \\ \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) &= \mathbb{E}[q_i^2 k_i^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[q_i k_i]\big)^2 = \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] - 0 = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 . \end{aligned} $$

In words: each little product has average 0 and variance 1. (We used $\mathbb{E}[q_i^2]=\operatorname{Var}(q_i)=1$, since the mean is 0.) 𝔼 is “expected value” — the long-run average. Var is variance — how spread out the values are.

Now add up $d_k$ of these independent terms. Variances of independent things simply add:

$$ \operatorname{Var}\!\big(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}\big) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{typical size} \approx \sqrt{d_k}. $$

In words: the raw score has variance dk, so its typical magnitude grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. With $d_k = 64$ the scores swing by roughly ±8 instead of ±1 — eight times too hot.

Why does an oversized score hurt? Because the next step is a softmax (defined below), and softmax of large numbers saturates: one entry rockets to nearly 1, the rest collapse to nearly 0. At that extreme the function is almost flat, so its gradient (the slope the network learns from — see Section 2) is almost zero. A zero gradient means no learning signal. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ rescales the variance back to 1, keeping scores in the gentle, high-gradient middle of the softmax:

$$ \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{1}{d_k}\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \frac{d_k}{d_k} = 1 . $$

In words: dividing a random quantity by a constant divides its variance by that constant squared. So dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ divides the variance by dk, pulling it from dk back to 1. That is the entire reason for the “scaled” in scaled dot-product attention.

▲ Watch out

Drop the $\sqrt{d_k}$ and small models still train — just worse. But scale up dk and the unscaled version quietly stops learning: the softmax locks into a near–one-hot spike from the first step, the gradient vanishes, and the loss plateaus. It looks like a mysterious optimisation failure; it is really this missing division.

Step 3 — softmax: turn scores into a blend that sums to 1

The scaled scores are still arbitrary real numbers — some negative, none adding up to anything tidy. We want weights: all positive, summing to 1, so they describe a genuine blend. The softmax function does exactly that, applied to each row independently.

Definition

The softmax of a list of numbers exponentiates each (making it positive and amplifying the largest), then divides by the total so the results sum to 1: $\displaystyle \text{softmax}(z)_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_m e^{z_m}}$. It turns any list of scores into a set of probabilities. We met it in Section 4; here it converts match-scores into attention weights.

$$ \mathbf{A} = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \qquad \text{shape } (T \times T),\ \text{each row sums to } 1. $$

In words: row i of A is position i's attention distribution — how much of its answer it will draw from each of the T positions. “Softmax over each row” matters: we normalise across the keys a single query is comparing itself to, so each query spends a total attention budget of exactly 1.

Step 4 — the weighted blend of values

Finally, each position builds its output by mixing all the values with its attention weights. Another matrix multiply:

$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \underbrace{\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)}_{\mathbf{A}\,:\,(L\times T)} \underbrace{\vphantom{\frac{1}{1}}\mathbf{V}}_{(L\times d_v)} \qquad \text{shape } (T \times d_v). $$

In words: multiply the L×L weight table by the L×dv value stack; the shared T cancels and out comes an T×dv result — one new vector per position. Row i of the output is $\sum_j A_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j$: a weighted average of every value, using position i's weights. Positions it matched strongly contribute most; the rest barely register.

◆ The whole formula in one line

$\displaystyle \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}$. Read left to right it is: compare every query to every key (QKᵀ), calm the numbers (÷√d_k), turn them into a blend (softmax), then mix the values by that blend (×V). Four steps, one line, and it is the engine of the entire field.

A concrete walk-through: seq = 3, dk = 4

Let us make it real with tiny numbers. Three positions ($L=3$), query/key/value length four ($d_k=d_v=4$). Then:

  • $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ are each $3 \times 4$ — three rows, four numbers per row.
  • $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ is $(3\times 4)(4\times 3) = 3 \times 3$ — the shared 4 cancels. Nine scores: every position against every position.
  • Divide all nine by $\sqrt{4}=2$.
  • Softmax each of the 3 rows → a $3\times 3$ table whose rows each sum to 1. Say position 1's row comes out $[\,0.7,\ 0.2,\ 0.1\,]$: it will take 70% of its answer from position 1, 20% from position 2, 10% from position 3.
  • Multiply $(3\times 3)(3\times 4) = 3 \times 4$. The shared 3 cancels; out come three new 4-long vectors — one refreshed representation per position. Position 1's output is $0.7\,\mathbf{v}_1 + 0.2\,\mathbf{v}_2 + 0.1\,\mathbf{v}_3$.

Notice the output has the same shape as the value input ($3\times 4$). Attention refreshes each position's vector by letting it gather context from the others; it does not change how many positions there are. That shape-preserving property is what lets us stack these blocks dozens deep.

The same thing in PyTorch

scaled dot-product attention — the four steps, verbatim

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V):
    # Q, K, V: [..., seq, d_k]   (the leading dims can be batch, heads, etc.)
    d_k = Q.size(-1)                                  # length of each query/key
    scores  = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1)                 # [..., seq, seq]  QKᵀ: every query · every key
    scores  = scores / d_k ** 0.5                     # [..., seq, seq]  the √d_k scaling
    weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)               # [..., seq, seq]  each ROW sums to 1
    return weights @ V                                # [..., seq, d_v]  blend the values

# tiny sanity check: seq = 3, d_k = 4
Q = torch.randn(3, 4)
K = torch.randn(3, 4)
V = torch.randn(3, 4)
out = scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V)
print(out.shape)                                      # torch.Size([3, 4])  -> same as V

In words: transpose(-2, -1) swaps the last two axes to form K⊤. dim=-1 makes softmax normalise across keys (the last axis), which is exactly “over each row.” The leading ... is deliberate — the same function will handle batches and heads untouched in a moment.

Check yourself If a query's scaled scores against three keys are [5, 5, 5] (a perfect tie), what attention weights does softmax give, and what is the resulting output? Reveal

Softmax of three equal numbers is uniform: [1/3, 1/3, 1/3] (the constant cancels top and bottom). The output is the plain average of the three values, $\tfrac13(\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2+\mathbf{v}_3)$. This is the honest behaviour when a query has no reason to prefer anyone: it listens to everyone equally. Attention only becomes selective when the scores actually differ — which is why the learned $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K$ matter so much.

3Multi-head attention: several lookups at once

Intuition first

One attention operation produces one blend per position — one kind of relationship. But a word relates to its neighbours in many ways at once: grammar (which noun does this verb agree with?), position (what came just before?), meaning (what does this pronoun refer to?). Asking a single query to capture all of those at once is asking too much. So we run several attentions in parallel — each called a head — each with its own $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V$, and let each specialise in a different kind of relationship. Then we stitch their answers back together.

☂ Everyday parallel

A panel of experts reviewing the same job application. The grammar expert checks one thing, the experience expert another, the culture-fit expert a third. Each reads the same application but attends to different parts of it. Afterwards a manager collects all their notes and merges them into one decision. Multi-head attention is that panel: several specialists looking at the same sentence through different lenses, then a final matrix combining their findings.

Now the math

We fix a total budget $d_{\text{model}}$ and split it across $h$ heads. Each head gets a slice of size:

$$ d_k = \frac{d_{\text{model}}}{h} $$

In words: with $d_{\text{model}}=512$ and $h=8$ heads, each head works in a small $d_k=64$-dimensional space. Eight cheap 64-wide lookups cost about the same as one expensive 512-wide lookup — we get variety for free, not at extra cost.

For each head $i = 1 \dots h$ we run ordinary scaled dot-product attention in its slice:

$$ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}\big(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)},\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)},\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)}\big) \qquad \text{shape } (T \times d_k). $$

Each head's own projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)},\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)},\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)}$ have shape $d_{\text{model}} \times d_k$. So each head outputs an $T \times d_k$ matrix. We lay all $h$ of them side by side (concatenate — glue along the width):

$$ \text{concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) \qquad \text{shape } (T \times h\,d_k) = (T \times d_{\text{model}}). $$

In words: because $h \cdot d_k = d_{\text{model}}$ by construction, gluing the heads back together restores the full width. We are exactly where we started, size-wise — but the vector now carries h different views blended in.

One last step. The concatenated heads sit in separate lanes; nothing has yet let a discovery in head 3 talk to a discovery in head 7. A final learned matrix $\mathbf{W}_O$ mixes them:

$$ \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) = \text{concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,\mathbf{W}_O, \qquad \mathbf{W}_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}}. $$

In words: $\mathbf{W}_O$ takes the stacked-up head outputs and blends them into one coherent dmodel-wide vector per position. It is the “manager” in the panel analogy — the layer that turns separate expert notes into a single decision. Final output shape: $(T \times d_{\text{model}})$, same as the input, so the block stacks cleanly.

◆ Why heads earn their keep

When researchers inspect trained transformers, different heads really do specialise: some track the previous word, some link verbs to their subjects, some connect a pronoun to the noun it refers to. No one programmed these roles — the split into heads simply gave the model room to discover several relationship types instead of averaging them all into one muddy signal.

Check yourself A model has d_model = 768 and h = 12 heads. What is d_k? And what shape is each head's output for a sequence of length 100? Reveal

$d_k = 768 / 12 = 64$. Each head runs attention in a 64-wide space and outputs shape (100, 64). Concatenating all 12 heads gives $(100, 12\times 64) = (100, 768)$, and $\mathbf{W}_O$ of shape (768, 768) maps that back to (100, 768). These are the actual numbers inside BERT-base and GPT-2 small (two landmark early transformers) — you just sized a real model.

4Positional encodings: attention can't tell order

Intuition first

Attention has a hidden flaw, and it is a big one. Look again at how a position builds its output: $\sum_j A_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j$ — a sum over the other positions. Addition doesn't care about order. Shuffle the input words and you get the same set of outputs, just shuffled the same way. Attention treats a sentence as a bag of words. But “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are very different sentences. We must hand the model some sense of position, because the mechanism itself has none.

☂ Everyday parallel

Hand a chef a basket of loose ingredients with no recipe steps and no order — flour, eggs, sugar, heat. They can see what they have but not in what sequence to use it, and sequence is the whole difference between a cake and a mess. Positional encodings are the little numbered stickers we put on each ingredient — “I'm step 1, I'm step 2” — so order is no longer lost.

Now the math — first, the flaw, precisely

Let $\mathbf{P}$ be a permutation matrix — an identity matrix with its rows shuffled — so $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}$ reorders the input positions. Because attention computes $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$ row by row and then softmax-blends, feeding it $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}$ gives:

$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{P}\,\text{Attention}(\mathbf{X}). $$

In words: reorder the inputs and you get exactly the same outputs, in the reordered order — nothing else changes. This property is called permutation invariance (a permutation is just a reshuffle). It is perfect for a set of items with no natural order, and disastrous for language, where order is meaning. The fix is not to change attention — it is to make each word's vector already contain its position before attention ever sees it.

So we build a position vector $\mathbf{PE}_{\text{pos}}$ for each slot $\text{pos} = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and simply add it to the word's vector. The original transformer uses a fixed pattern of sines and cosines at many frequencies:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i)} &= \sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right), \\[4pt] \mathbf{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i+1)} &= \cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right). \end{aligned} $$

In words: pos is which slot in the sentence (0, 1, 2, …); i indexes the dimension inside the vector. Even dimensions use a sine, odd dimensions the matching cosine. The $10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}$ in the denominator makes the wavelength stretch as i grows: early dimensions wiggle fast, later dimensions wiggle slowly.

◆ Why sines at many frequencies

Think of a bank of clocks with different hand speeds — seconds, minutes, hours, days. Read all their hands together and you can pin down a unique moment in time. The fast hand distinguishes nearby moments; the slow hand distinguishes far-apart ones. The sinusoidal encoding is exactly this: each pair of dimensions is a clock at its own speed, and a position's full set of hand-readings is a fingerprint no other position shares. A bonus falls out of the trig: the code for position $\text{pos}+k$ is a fixed linear function of the code for $\text{pos}$, so the model can learn to reason about relative offsets like “three words back.”

A common alternative is the learned positional embedding: instead of the fixed sine formula, keep a lookup table with one trainable vector per position and let training decide what each should be. GPT and BERT use this. It is simpler and works as well over the lengths seen in training; the sinusoidal version has the edge that it extends smoothly to positions longer than any seen during training, since the formula is defined for every $\text{pos}$. Either way the shape is the same ($T \times d_{\text{model}}$) and the move is the same: add it to the input.

The same thing in PyTorch

sinusoidal positional encodings, added to the input

import torch, math

def positional_encoding(seq_len, d_model):
    pos = torch.arange(seq_len).unsqueeze(1)              # [L, 1]  slot index 0,1,2,...
    i   = torch.arange(0, d_model, 2)                     # [d/2]   even dimension indices
    freq = 1.0 / (10000 ** (i / d_model))                # [d/2]   one frequency per dim-pair
    pe = torch.zeros(seq_len, d_model)                   # [L, d_model]
    pe[:, 0::2] = torch.sin(pos * freq)                  # even dims -> sine
    pe[:, 1::2] = torch.cos(pos * freq)                  # odd  dims -> cosine
    return pe                                            # [L, d_model]

X  = torch.randn(10, 512)                                # 10 word vectors, d_model = 512
X  = X + positional_encoding(10, 512)                    # [10, 512]  position now baked in
print(X.shape)                                           # torch.Size([10, 512])

In words: we add, not concatenate — the position rides along inside the existing dmodel width, so nothing downstream changes shape. After this line, two identical words in different slots have different vectors, and permutation invariance is broken exactly where we wanted it broken.

Check yourself Without positional encodings, would a transformer give different outputs for “dog bites man” and “man bites dog”? Reveal

No — and that is the whole problem. With the same three word-vectors in a different order, permutation invariance guarantees the same set of output vectors, merely reordered. The model literally could not tell who bit whom. Adding positional encodings gives “dog” in slot 0 a different vector from “dog” in slot 2, so the two sentences finally produce genuinely different representations. Position is not a nice-to-have here; it is what makes language modelling possible at all.

5The full transformer block, dimension by dimension

Intuition first

We now have the two moving parts. Multi-head attention lets positions share information across the sequence — the “mixing across words” step. But it is mostly a weighted average; it does no heavy per-word computation on its own. So each block pairs it with a feed-forward network (FFN) — a small two-layer network applied to each position separately — that does the “think hard about this one word” step. Wrap both in two safety devices — residual connections and LayerNorm — and you have the complete transformer block. Stack it $N$ times and you have a transformer.

☂ Everyday parallel

A team working a problem in rounds. Each round has two phases: a meeting, where everyone shares what they know and updates their view (that's attention — mixing information between people), and desk work, where each person goes off alone and processes what they just heard (that's the FFN — per-position computation). Do many such rounds and a rich shared understanding builds up. A transformer is that team; the block is one round.

Now the math — the two support devices

Residual connections. Instead of replacing the input with a sub-layer's output, we add the output back to the input: $\mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})$. This is the same skip-connection trick that made very deep image networks trainable in Section 16. Two reasons it matters here: it gives each sub-layer an easy “do nothing” option (output zero and the input passes straight through), and it hands the gradient a clean shortcut straight back through the addition — so signal reaches the earliest layers without fading. Shapes must match for the add, which is why every sub-layer preserves $d_{\text{model}}$.

LayerNorm. Before each sub-layer we normalise each position's vector: subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation, then rescale with two learned numbers. This keeps the numbers flowing through the network at a steady scale — not exploding, not vanishing — which is the difference between training that converges and training that diverges. We treat it properly in Section 20; here just hold that it is a per-position “volume normaliser” that keeps every layer's input well-behaved.

The feed-forward network. Two linear layers with a nonlinearity between, applied identically to every position:

$$ \text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \max(0,\ \mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_1 + \mathbf{b}_1)\,\mathbf{W}_2 + \mathbf{b}_2 $$

with the deliberate widen-then-shrink shape:

StepShape in → outMatrix
expand$d_{\text{model}} \to 4\,d_{\text{model}}$$\mathbf{W}_1 : d_{\text{model}} \times 4d_{\text{model}}$
nonlinearity$4\,d_{\text{model}} \to 4\,d_{\text{model}}$ReLU / GELU (no weights)
project back$4\,d_{\text{model}} \to d_{\text{model}}$$\mathbf{W}_2 : 4d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$

In words: the FFN blows each position's vector up to four times the width, runs a nonlinearity ($\max(0,\cdot)$ is ReLU — “keep positives, zero out negatives”), then squeezes it back down. The wide middle gives the network room to compute richer features per word; the factor of 4 is the standard choice from the original paper. Crucially the FFN acts on each position independently — it does no mixing between positions. All the cross-word talking already happened in attention.

Assembling the block (pre-norm order)

Modern transformers put the LayerNorm before each sub-layer (“pre-norm” — it trains more stably than the original “post-norm”). The full block is four lines:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{MultiHead}\big(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{FFN}\big(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned} $$

In words: normalise, mix across words with attention, add the result back in. Then normalise again, think per word with the FFN, add that back in. Input shape $(T \times d_{\text{model}})$; output shape $(T \times d_{\text{model}})$ — identical, on purpose, so the next block can consume it with no adjustment.

◆ The shape never changes — that's the point

Every sub-layer takes $(T \times d_{\text{model}})$ in and returns $(T \times d_{\text{model}})$ out. Attention preserves it, the FFN preserves it, the residual add requires it, LayerNorm preserves it. That invariance is what lets you stack 12, 48, or 96 identical blocks like LEGO bricks — the entire depth of a large model is this one shape, repeated.

The same thing in PyTorch

multi-head attention and a full transformer block — shapes on every line

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class MultiHeadAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        assert d_model % n_heads == 0                    # heads must divide the width evenly
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_k = d_model // n_heads                    # slice size per head
        # one big Linear does all heads at once (cheaper than n_heads separate ones)
        self.W_q = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_k = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_v = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_o = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)           # the final mixing matrix W_O

    def forward(self, x):                                # x: [B, L, d_model]
        B, L, _ = x.shape
        # project, then split the width into heads: [B, L, d_model] -> [B, n_heads, L, d_k]
        def split(t):
            return t.view(B, L, self.n_heads, self.d_k).transpose(1, 2)
        Q, K, V = split(self.W_q(x)), split(self.W_k(x)), split(self.W_v(x))   # [B, n_heads, L, d_k]

        scores  = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1) / self.d_k ** 0.5   # [B, n_heads, L, L]
        weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)                   # [B, n_heads, L, L]  rows sum to 1
        out     = weights @ V                                 # [B, n_heads, L, d_k]

        # concat heads back together: [B, n_heads, L, d_k] -> [B, L, d_model]
        out = out.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, L, self.n_heads * self.d_k)
        return self.W_o(out)                                  # [B, L, d_model]


class TransformerBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, d_ff=None):
        super().__init__()
        d_ff = d_ff or 4 * d_model                       # the standard 4x wide middle
        self.attn  = MultiHeadAttention(d_model, n_heads)
        self.norm1 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.norm2 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.ffn = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d_model, d_ff),                    # [B, L, d_model] -> [B, L, d_ff]
            nn.GELU(),                                   # smooth cousin of ReLU
            nn.Linear(d_ff, d_model),                    # [B, L, d_ff] -> [B, L, d_model]
        )

    def forward(self, x):                                # x: [B, L, d_model]
        x = x + self.attn(self.norm1(x))                 # pre-norm attention + residual add
        x = x + self.ffn(self.norm2(x))                  # pre-norm FFN + residual add
        return x                                         # [B, L, d_model]  -> same shape in and out


# smoke test: batch 2, sequence 10, width 512, 8 heads
block = TransformerBlock(d_model=512, n_heads=8)
x = torch.randn(2, 10, 512)
print(block(x).shape)                                    # torch.Size([2, 10, 512])

In words: the batch dimension B rides along in front the whole way — our earlier scaled_dot_product_attention used a leading ... for exactly this reason. Notice the code contains no loop over heads: reshaping to [B, n_heads, L, d_k] lets one batched matrix multiply do all heads at once. That is the parallelism we promised at the start of this section, cashed out.

◆ Where this goes next

This block is the encoder form: every position may look at every other. To turn a transformer into a text generator, we add one line — a causal mask that stops each position from peeking at words it hasn't produced yet. That single change is the subject of Section 19. And when models grow huge, the FFN is often replaced by a mixture of experts — many FFNs with a router picking a few per token — which is Section 22. Only the feed-forward sub-layer changes there; the masked self-attention works exactly as it does here.

Check yourself In the block, why must every sub-layer output the shape [B, L, d_model] — what specifically breaks if the FFN returned [B, L, 2*d_model] instead? Reveal

The residual add x = x + ffn(...) requires both sides to have the same shape — you cannot add a [B, L, d_model] tensor to a [B, L, 2*d_model] one; PyTorch would raise a shape error. More broadly, the next block also expects d_model width, so a changed width would break the whole stack. That is why the FFN widens to 4*d_model in the middle but must project back to d_model at the end — the residual pathway keeps the width pinned from input to output.

6What to carry forward

  • Attention is a soft lookup. Queries ask, keys advertise, values are the content; each position gets a weighted blend of all values, graded by query–key match.
  • The formula: $\text{softmax}\!\big(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k}\big)\mathbf{V}$ — compare, calm, blend, mix. Shapes: $(L\times d_k)(d_k\times T)\to(L\times T)$, then $(L\times T)(L\times d_v)\to(L\times d_v)$.
  • The $\sqrt{d_k}$ is derived, not decorative: raw scores have variance $d_k$; dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ restores variance 1 and keeps softmax out of its flat, no-gradient zone.
  • Multi-head splits $d_{\text{model}}$ into $h$ heads of size $d_{\text{model}}/h$, runs attention in each, concatenates, and mixes with $\mathbf{W}_O$ — many relationship types for the price of one.
  • Attention is permutation-invariant, so we add positional encodings (sinusoidal or learned) to inject order.
  • The block is: LayerNorm → multi-head attention → residual add → LayerNorm → FFN ($d_{\text{model}}\to 4d_{\text{model}}\to d_{\text{model}}$) → residual add. Shape in equals shape out, so blocks stack without limit.

You now hold the complete core of every modern LLM. Next we add a causal mask and turn this machine into something that writes — that's Section 19, language modelling.
