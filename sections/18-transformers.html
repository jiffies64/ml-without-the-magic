Attention & Transformers

The architecture behind modern large language models, derived step by step, with the shape of every matrix written out.

45 min read
You'll learn

Most large language models you have heard of (GPT, Claude, Llama, Gemini) are a tall stack of one repeated part: the transformer block. The central operation inside that block is called attention. When you understand attention, the rest of a modern language model becomes engineering, not magic. So we will build it here from the dot product you already know. We will write down the shape of every matrix as we go, because in this subject the shapes are the understanding.

◆ The one idea to hold onto

Attention lets every position in a sequence look directly at every other position and take the information it needs. All positions do this at the same time, in parallel. This is what replaced the slow, forgetful recurrent networks of Section 17. Everything below is the detail of how that lookup is computed.

1Why we needed something new

Intuition first

In Section 17 we met recurrent neural networks (RNNs). They read a sentence one word at a time and carry a running summary, the hidden state (a fixed-size memory vector), from each word to the next. They work, but they have two problems.

  • Long-range forgetting. To connect the last word of a paragraph to the first, the information must survive every step in between. It fades. This is the vanishing-gradient problem from Section 17.
  • No parallelism. Word 50 cannot be processed until word 49 is done, because it needs the hidden state from word 49. So the work cannot be spread across the thousands of cores in a modern GPU, and training is slow.

We want two things at once. First, direct connections between far-apart words, so nothing has to survive a long relay. Second, the ability to process every word at the same time, so training is fast. Attention gives both, with an idea that is surprisingly simple.

☂ Everyday parallel

Imagine reading a mystery novel in two ways. The RNN way: you may keep only one small note of memory, and you must rewrite it after every sentence. By the last chapter, the early clues are lost. The attention way: the whole book lies open on a large table. To understand any sentence, you may look directly at any other sentence, however far back. Nothing fades on the way. And a room full of readers can each study a different sentence at the same time.

Check yourself In “The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big,” what does “it” refer to? Is the hard part here the distance between the words, or something else? Reveal

“It” means the trophy. Change “big” to “small” and it means the suitcase. The distance is short: “trophy” is only 7 words before “it”. The hard part is knowledge: you must know that a big thing does not fit into a smaller one. Sentences like this are called Winograd schemas, and they test common sense, not memory span. Attention helps in one way: “it” can compare itself with both “trophy” and “suitcase” directly, in one step. Choosing the right one still depends on what the model learned in training. For a truly long-range example, imagine the trophy was named three paragraphs earlier. An RNN would have to carry it through hundreds of steps; attention still reaches it in one.

2Scaled dot-product attention, from scratch

Intuition first

Here is the whole mechanism in one picture, and it is a picture of a lookup. Every position asks a question. Every position also shows a label that says what it is about, and every position offers some content. A position builds its answer as a blend of everyone's content. The blend is weighted by how well each label matches its question. That is attention. Three roles do all the work:

  • The query ($\mathbf{q}$): what this position is looking for. (“I'm a pronoun; I need the noun I refer to.”)
  • The key ($\mathbf{k}$): a label each position shows about itself. (“I'm a concrete singular noun.”)
  • The value ($\mathbf{v}$): the content a position hands over when it is selected. (“Here is my meaning: trophy.”)
☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a library search. Your query is the phrase you type into the catalog. Each book has a spine label, its key, and its pages, its value. A normal search picks the one book whose label matches best and gives you that book. Attention is the soft version. It returns a blend: 70% of this book's pages, 20% of that one's, 10% of a third. The shares depend on how well each label matched your query. So attention is a soft dictionary lookup. A dictionary returns one value for an exact key. Attention returns a weighted mix of all values, graded by how similar each key is to the query.

Now the math

We start from an input we already have: a sequence of vectors, one per position (per word). We stack them as the rows of a matrix $\mathbf{X}$.

$$ \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}} $$

In words: $\mathbf{X}$ has one row per position. $T$ is the sequence length: how many words (or tokens) we process. $d_{\text{model}}$ is the model dimension: the length of the vector for each word (512 in the original transformer). So $\mathbf{X}$ has $T$ rows, and each row is a vector of length $d_{\text{model}}$.

Now the one trick. We do not use $\mathbf{X}$ directly as query, key and value. We project it into three different spaces with three learned weight matrices. Then the model can learn what makes a good question, a good label, and good content. The three need not be the same.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q^{\top} \\ \mathbf{K} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K^{\top} \\ \mathbf{V} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V^{\top} \end{aligned} $$

In words: as in the rest of this course, we store a weight matrix with shape (out, in), like nn.Linear. For a stack of row vectors $\mathbf{X}$, the layer is $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\top}$ (see Section 14). Each row of $\mathbf{Q}$ is one position's query, and so on.

Track every shape with the shape rule from Section 1 (inner dimensions cancel, outer dimensions stay):

ObjectShapeWhat it is
$\mathbf{W}_Q$$d_k \times d_{\text{model}}$learned: how to build a query
$\mathbf{W}_K$$d_k \times d_{\text{model}}$learned: how to build a key
$\mathbf{W}_V$$d_v \times d_{\text{model}}$learned: how to build a value
$\mathbf{Q}$$T \times d_k$one query per position
$\mathbf{K}$$T \times d_k$one key per position
$\mathbf{V}$$T \times d_v$one value per position

In words: $d_k$ is the length of each query and each key. They must have the same length, because we are about to compare them. $d_v$ is the length of each value, and it can be any size. Very often $d_k = d_v$, and we will assume that from now on to keep the picture clean.

Step 1: scores (how well does each query match each key?)

To compare query $i$ with key $j$, we use the dot product from Section 1, our alignment meter. A large dot product means “this query and this key point the same way: a strong match.” We want every query against every key, and that is one matrix multiply:

$$ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top} $$ $$ (T \times d_k)(d_k \times T) = (T \times T) $$

In words: $\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ is $\mathbf{K}$ transposed (rows and columns swapped), so the shared $d_k$ lines up and cancels. Entry $S_{ij}$ is the dot product of query $i$ with key $j$. It is one number that says how much position $i$ should attend to position $j$. The result is a square $T \times T$ table: every position scored against every position. This one matrix is where the promise “look at everything at once” is kept.

Step 2: the scaling, and why we need it

We do not use $\mathbf{S}$ as it is. We divide it by $\sqrt{d_k}$. This looks like a fudge factor, but statistics forces it on us. Let's derive it.

Suppose the entries of a query $\mathbf{q}$ and a key $\mathbf{k}$ are independent random numbers, each with mean $0$ and variance $1$. (This is a fair assumption for freshly initialized weights.) Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ products:

$$ \mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i\,k_i $$

Take one term $q_i k_i$. Because $q_i$ and $k_i$ are independent with mean 0:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[q_i k_i] &= \mathbb{E}[q_i]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i] = 0 \\[4pt] \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) &= \mathbb{E}[q_i^2 k_i^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[q_i k_i]\big)^2 \\ &= \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] - 0 = 1 \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\mathbb{E}$ is the expected value (the long-run average). $\operatorname{Var}$ is the variance (how spread out the values are). Each small product has average 0 and variance 1. We used $\mathbb{E}[q_i^2]=\operatorname{Var}(q_i)=1$, which holds because the mean is 0.

Now add up $d_k$ of these independent terms. Variances of independent terms add:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k $$

In words: the raw score has variance $d_k$, so its standard deviation (typical size) is $\sqrt{d_k}$. With $d_k = 64$ the scores spread about ±8 instead of ±1: eight times too large.

Why does a large score hurt? The next step is a softmax (defined below). Softmax of large numbers saturates: one weight goes to nearly 1 and the others go to nearly 0. Look at the softmax derivative. With weights $a_j = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_j$:

$$ \frac{\partial a_j}{\partial z_m} = a_j\,\big(\delta_{jm} - a_m\big) $$

In words: $\delta_{jm}$ is 1 when $j = m$ and 0 otherwise. If one weight is near 1 and all others are near 0, every term is near 0. For $j = m$ it is $a_j(1-a_j)$, which is $1 \cdot 0$ or $0 \cdot 1$. For $j \ne m$ it is $-a_j a_m$, and at least one factor is near 0. So the gradient (the slope the network learns from; see Section 2) is almost zero, and there is almost no learning signal for the scores.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to 1. This keeps the scores in the middle range of the softmax, where the gradient is large:

$$ \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k})}{d_k} = 1 $$

In words: dividing a random quantity by a constant divides its variance by that constant squared. So dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ divides the variance by $d_k$, from $d_k$ back to 1. That is the reason for the word “scaled” in scaled dot-product attention.

▲ Watch out

If you drop the $\sqrt{d_k}$, a small model with small $d_k$ may still train, only worse. The larger $d_k$ is, the larger the unscaled scores are at the start. Then the softmax starts close to one-hot, its gradients are tiny, and training is slow or gets stuck. This is the reason the original transformer paper (Vaswani et al., 2017) gives for the scaling. The code at the end of this section measures the effect.

Step 3: softmax (turn scores into a blend that sums to 1)

The scaled scores are still any real numbers. Some are negative, and they do not add up to anything useful. We want weights: all positive and summing to 1, so they describe a real blend. The softmax function does this. We apply it to each row separately.

Definition

The softmax of a list of numbers $z_1, \dots, z_n$ takes the exponential of each one (this makes it positive and makes the largest one stand out). Then it divides by the total, so the results sum to 1:

$$ \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_m e^{z_m}} $$

It turns any list of scores into probabilities. We met it in Section 4. Here it turns match scores into attention weights.

$$ \mathbf{A} = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) $$

In words: $\mathbf{A}$ has shape $T \times T$, and each row sums to 1. Row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ is position $i$'s attention distribution: how much of its answer it takes from each of the $T$ positions. “Softmax over each row” matters. We normalize across the keys that one query is compared with, so each query spends a total attention budget of exactly 1.

Step 4: the weighted blend of values

Finally, each position builds its output by mixing all the values with its attention weights. This is another matrix multiply:

$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \underbrace{\mathbf{A}}_{T \times T}\;\underbrace{\mathbf{V}}_{T \times d_v} $$

In words: multiply the $T \times T$ weight table by the $T \times d_v$ stack of values. The shared $T$ cancels, and the result is $T \times d_v$: one new vector per position. Row $i$ of the output is $\sum_j A_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j$, a weighted average of all the values, using position $i$'s weights. Positions it matched strongly contribute most; the rest contribute little.

◆ The whole formula in one line
$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) \\ &\quad = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V} \end{aligned} $$

Read it left to right. Compare every query with every key ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$). Bring the numbers back to a normal size (divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$). Turn them into a blend (softmax). Then mix the values with that blend (multiply by $\mathbf{V}$). Four steps in one line.

A worked example: T = 3, dk = 4

Let's make it concrete with small numbers. Take three positions ($T=3$) and queries, keys and values of length four ($d_k=d_v=4$). Then:

  • $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are each $3 \times 4$: three rows, four numbers per row.
  • $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ is $(3\times 4)(4\times 3) = 3 \times 3$. The shared 4 cancels. That gives nine scores: every position against every position.
  • Divide all nine by $\sqrt{4}=2$.
  • Softmax each of the 3 rows. The result is a $3\times 3$ table whose rows each sum to 1. Say row 1 is $[\,0.7,\ 0.2,\ 0.1\,]$. Then position 1 takes 70% of its answer from position 1, 20% from position 2, and 10% from position 3.
  • Multiply: $(3\times 3)(3\times 4) = 3 \times 4$. The shared 3 cancels, and we get three new vectors of length 4, one for each position. Position 1's output is $0.7\,\mathbf{v}_1 + 0.2\,\mathbf{v}_2 + 0.1\,\mathbf{v}_3$.

Notice that the output has the same shape as the value input ($3\times 4$). Attention updates each position's vector with context from the other positions. It does not change the number of positions. This is what lets us stack these blocks dozens deep.

The same thing in PyTorch

scaled dot-product attention in four lines, and a test of the √d_k argument

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V):
    # Q, K: [..., T, d_k]   V: [..., T, d_v]   (leading dims can be batch, heads, ...)
    d_k = Q.size(-1)                         # length of each query/key
    scores  = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1)        # [..., T, T]    QKᵀ: every query · every key
    scores  = scores / d_k ** 0.5            # [..., T, T]    the √d_k scaling
    weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)      # [..., T, T]    each ROW sums to 1
    return weights @ V                       # [..., T, d_v]  blend the values

torch.manual_seed(0)
Q, K, V = torch.randn(3, 4), torch.randn(3, 4), torch.randn(3, 4)   # T = 3, d_k = d_v = 4
out = scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V)
print(out.shape)                                                    # torch.Size([3, 4])
print(torch.allclose(out, F.scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V), atol=1e-6))  # True

# test the √d_k argument: random unit-variance q, k with d_k = 512
d_k = 512
q, k = torch.randn(10_000, d_k), torch.randn(10_000, d_k)
print((q * k).sum(-1).var().item())                   # 517.6...  about d_k
print(((q * k).sum(-1) / d_k ** 0.5).var().item())    # 1.01...   about 1

# what that does to softmax: one query against 10 keys
z = torch.randn(10, d_k) @ torch.randn(d_k)            # 10 raw scores, spread about ±22
J = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian
for name, s in [("unscaled", z), ("scaled", z / d_k ** 0.5)]:
    w = F.softmax(s, dim=-1)
    g = J(lambda t: F.softmax(t, dim=-1), s).norm()     # size of all ∂w_j/∂s_m
    print(f"{name:8s} largest weight {w.max():.3f}   Jacobian norm {g:.1e}")
# unscaled largest weight 1.000   Jacobian norm 9.1e-10
# scaled   largest weight 0.368   Jacobian norm 3.4e-01

In words: transpose(-2, -1) swaps the last two axes to form $\mathbf{K}^{\top}$. dim=-1 makes softmax normalize across the keys (the last axis), which is “over each row”. The leading ... is on purpose: the same function will handle batches and heads later. Our function gives the same result as PyTorch's built-in F.scaled_dot_product_attention. In the last experiment, the unscaled softmax is one-hot and its derivative is almost zero. The exact numbers change with the seed, but in our runs the unscaled Jacobian was always the smaller one, often by many orders of magnitude.

Check yourself A query's scaled scores against three keys are $[5, 5, 5]$ (a perfect tie). What weights does softmax give, and what is the output? What changes if the scores are $[5, 5, 9]$? Reveal

Softmax of three equal numbers is uniform: $[1/3, 1/3, 1/3]$. (The factor $e^5$ cancels above and below the line.) The output is the plain average of the three values, $\tfrac13(\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2+\mathbf{v}_3)$. A query with no reason to prefer any position listens to all of them equally. With $[5, 5, 9]$, only the differences matter: the weights are $e^0 : e^0 : e^4 = 1 : 1 : 54.6$, so about $[0.018, 0.018, 0.965]$. The output is almost $\mathbf{v}_3$. Attention becomes selective only when the scores differ, which is why the learned $\mathbf{W}_Q$ and $\mathbf{W}_K$ matter so much.

Check yourself A model uses $d_k = 256$ and forgets the scaling. At the start of training, what is the typical size of a score? Predict what the attention rows look like. Reveal

The variance of a raw score is $d_k = 256$, so the typical size is $\sqrt{256} = 16$. Two scores in the same row often differ by 10 or more, and $e^{10} \approx 22{,}000$. So most rows are close to one-hot: nearly all the weight on one key. The softmax derivative is then close to zero, and the scores get almost no gradient. With the scaling, the typical size is 1 and the rows are smooth blends.

3Multi-head attention: several lookups at once

Intuition first

One attention operation gives one blend per position, so it captures one kind of relationship. But a word relates to its neighbors in many ways at once. There is grammar (which noun does this verb agree with?), position (what came just before?), and meaning (what does this pronoun refer to?). One query cannot capture all of these at once. So we run several attentions in parallel. Each one is called a head, and each has its own $\mathbf{W}_Q$, $\mathbf{W}_K$ and $\mathbf{W}_V$. Each head can specialize in a different kind of relationship. Then we join their answers back together.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of marking up a text with several colored pens, one reading at a time. On the first reading you mark only which verb goes with which subject. On the second, you mark only which pronoun points to which name. On the third, you mark only dates and places. Each reading looks at the same text but asks a different question. At the end you combine all the marks into one set of notes. Each head is one reading with its own question ($\mathbf{W}_Q$, $\mathbf{W}_K$, $\mathbf{W}_V$). Combining the notes is the final matrix $\mathbf{W}_O$.

Now the math

We fix a total width $d_{\text{model}}$ and split it across $h$ heads. Each head gets a slice of size:

$$ d_k = \frac{d_{\text{model}}}{h} $$

In words: with $d_{\text{model}}=512$ and $h=8$ heads, each head works in a small space of size $d_k=64$. Eight 64-wide lookups cost about the same as one 512-wide lookup. So we get several kinds of relationship at about the same cost as one.

For each head $i = 1, \dots, h$, we run ordinary scaled dot-product attention in its own slice:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{head}_i &= \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}_i, \mathbf{K}_i, \mathbf{V}_i) \\[4pt] \mathbf{Q}_i &= \mathbf{X}\big(\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)}\big)^{\!\top}, \ \text{etc.} \end{aligned} $$

In words: each head has its own projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)}$, $\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)}$ and $\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)}$, each of shape $d_k \times d_{\text{model}}$. So each head outputs a $T \times d_k$ matrix.

We lay all $h$ head outputs side by side. This is called concatenation: joining matrices along their width.

$$ \text{concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) $$ $$ \text{shape: } (T \times h\,d_k) = (T \times d_{\text{model}}) $$

In words: because $h \cdot d_k = d_{\text{model}}$ by construction, joining the heads gives back the full width. Size-wise, we are where we started. But each vector now holds $h$ different views.

One last step. After concatenation, each head's result is in its own separate lanes. Nothing has yet let a finding of head 3 combine with a finding of head 7. A final learned matrix $\mathbf{W}_O$ mixes them:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) \\ &\quad = \text{concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,\mathbf{W}_O^{\top} \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\mathbf{W}_O$ has shape $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$. It takes the joined head outputs and blends them into one $d_{\text{model}}$-wide vector per position. In the colored-pen picture, it combines the separate sets of marks into one set of notes. The final output shape is $T \times d_{\text{model}}$, the same as the input, so blocks can be stacked.

◆ Why heads are worth it

When researchers inspect trained transformers, some heads do show clear roles. Some track the previous word. Some link verbs to their subjects. Some connect a pronoun to the noun it refers to. No one programmed these roles. The split into heads gave the model room to learn several kinds of relationship, instead of averaging them all into one unclear signal. (Many other heads have no role that is easy to name.)

Check yourself A model has $d_{\text{model}} = 768$ and $h = 12$ heads. What is $d_k$, and what shape is one head's output for 100 tokens? Then: how many parameters does the attention layer have (the four matrices with biases)? Does that number change if you use 24 heads instead? Reveal

$d_k = 768 / 12 = 64$, and each head outputs shape (100, 64). Joining the 12 heads gives (100, 768), and $\mathbf{W}_O$ maps that to (100, 768). These are the sizes inside BERT-base and GPT-2 small, two well-known early transformers. The four matrices ($\mathbf{W}_Q$, $\mathbf{W}_K$, $\mathbf{W}_V$, $\mathbf{W}_O$) are each 768 × 768 plus a bias of 768, so the layer has $4 \times (768^2 + 768) = 2{,}362{,}368$ parameters. With 24 heads the count is the same. The heads only split the same width into narrower slices ($d_k = 32$). The number of heads changes how the width is used, not how many weights there are.

4Positional encodings: attention can't tell order

Intuition first

Attention has a hidden weakness. Look again at how a position builds its output: $\sum_j A_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j$, a sum over the other positions. A sum does not care about order. Shuffle the input words, and you get the same outputs, shuffled in the same way. So attention treats a sentence as a bag of words. But “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are very different sentences. We must give the model some sense of position, because the mechanism itself has none.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of loose book pages that fell on the floor. To keep their order, a printer can print a faint page-number watermark into the paper of each page. The watermark takes no extra space: it is blended into the page, on top of the text. A careful reader can still read both. Positional encodings work like this. We add a position pattern to each word vector, in the same space, instead of attaching extra numbers at the end.

Now the math

Let $\mathbf{P}$ be a permutation matrix: an identity matrix with its rows shuffled. Then $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}$ reorders the input positions. Let's follow $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}$ through attention. Each projection acts on each row separately, so it keeps the shuffle: $(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W}_Q^{\top} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}$, and the same for $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$. Then:

$$ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{K})^{\top} &= \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\top} \\[4pt] \text{softmax}\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\big) &= \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{P}^{\top} \\[4pt] \mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\,\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} &= \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V} \end{aligned} $$

In words: the scores are shuffled in both rows and columns. Softmax works on each row, and shuffling the entries of a row only shuffles its softmax, so the weights are shuffled in the same way. In the last line, $\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{I}$, because shuffling and then unshuffling changes nothing. So:

$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{P}\,\text{Attention}(\mathbf{X}) $$

In words: reorder the inputs, and you get the same outputs in the new order. Nothing else changes. This property is called permutation equivariance. (A permutation is a reordering. “Equivariant” means the output moves with the input; “invariant” would mean the output does not change at all.) It is right for a set of items with no natural order. It is wrong for language, where order carries meaning. The fix is not to change attention. The fix is to put each word's position into its vector before attention sees it.

So we build a position vector $\mathbf{PE}_{\text{pos}}$ for each slot $\text{pos} = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and add it to the word's vector. The original transformer uses a fixed pattern of sines and cosines at many frequencies:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i)} &= \sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right) \\[4pt] \mathbf{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i+1)} &= \cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right) \end{aligned} $$

In words: $\text{pos}$ is the slot in the sentence (0, 1, 2, …), and $i$ numbers the pairs of dimensions inside the vector. Even dimensions use a sine; the odd dimension next to it uses the matching cosine. The term $10000^{\,2i/d_{\text{model}}}$ makes the wavelength longer as $i$ grows. The first dimensions change quickly from one position to the next; the last dimensions change slowly.

◆ Why sines at many frequencies

Think of a set of clocks whose hands turn at different speeds: seconds, minutes, hours, days. Read all the hands together and you can identify one exact moment. The fast hand separates nearby moments; the slow hand separates moments far apart. The sinusoidal encoding works like this. Each pair of dimensions is a clock at its own speed. The set of all hand positions is a pattern that no other position shares (over the lengths used in practice).

There is also a bonus. Write $\omega$ for the frequency of one pair. Moving from position $p$ to $p+k$ turns that pair's “hand” by a fixed angle $\theta = \omega k$. With $s_p = \sin \omega p$ and $c_p = \cos \omega p$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} s_{p+k} \\ c_{p+k} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} s_p \\ c_p \end{bmatrix} $$

This follows from the sum formulas for sine and cosine. The matrix depends only on the offset $k$, not on $p$. So the code for “$k$ positions later” is a fixed linear function of the current code. This makes it easier for the model to learn relative offsets such as “three words back.”

Other ways to give position are common:

  • Learned positional embeddings. Keep a table with one trainable vector per position, and let training decide what each should be. GPT-2 and BERT use this. It works well for the lengths seen in training, but it has no vector for longer positions.
  • Rotary position embeddings (RoPE). Instead of adding a vector to the input, RoPE rotates each query and key, pair by pair, by an angle proportional to the position. It uses the rotation above directly. The dot product of a query at $p$ and a key at $p'$ then depends only on the offset $p - p'$. Most recent open models, such as Llama, use RoPE.

The sine formula is defined for every $\text{pos}$, so it gives a vector even for positions longer than any seen in training. But in practice, models with sinusoidal encodings do not work well on sequences much longer than their training sequences (Press et al., 2021). Handling longer sequences needs other methods. For the additive methods the shape is the same ($T \times d_{\text{model}}$) and the step is the same: add it to the input.

The same thing in PyTorch

sinusoidal positional encodings, added to the input

import torch

def positional_encoding(T, d_model):
    pos  = torch.arange(T).unsqueeze(1)                  # [T, 1]      slot index 0, 1, 2, ...
    i    = torch.arange(0, d_model, 2)                   # [d/2]       even dimension indices 2i
    freq = 1.0 / (10000 ** (i / d_model))                # [d/2]       one frequency per pair
    pe = torch.zeros(T, d_model)                         # [T, d_model]
    pe[:, 0::2] = torch.sin(pos * freq)                  # even dims -> sine
    pe[:, 1::2] = torch.cos(pos * freq)                  # odd  dims -> cosine
    return pe                                            # [T, d_model]

torch.manual_seed(0)
X = torch.randn(10, 512)                                 # 10 token vectors, d_model = 512
X = X + positional_encoding(10, 512)                     # [10, 512]  position now added in
print(X.shape)                                           # torch.Size([10, 512])
print(positional_encoding(10, 512)[1, :4])               # tensor([0.8415, 0.5403, 0.8219, 0.5697])

In words: we add; we do not concatenate. The position travels inside the existing $d_{\text{model}}$ width, so nothing later changes shape. The last line shows position 1: $\sin 1 = 0.8415$ and $\cos 1 = 0.5403$ for the fastest pair, then a slightly slower pair. After the addition, the same word in two different slots has two different vectors. The permutation equivariance is broken, which is what we want.

Check yourself Without positional encodings, feed the block “dog bites man” and then “man bites dog”. Compare the output vector for “dog” in the two runs. Are they the same or different? Why? Reveal

They are exactly the same. The second input is a reordering of the first, so by permutation equivariance the outputs are the same vectors, reordered. “Dog” gets the same vector whether it is the biter or the one bitten, so the model cannot tell who bit whom. The code in Section 5 checks this property on a full block. Adding positional encodings gives “dog” in slot 0 a different input vector from “dog” in slot 2, so the two sentences now give different results. One nuance: the text-generating models of Section 19 use a causal mask, which already breaks the symmetry. Such models can learn some position information even without positional encodings (Haviv et al., 2022). The block in this lesson, with no mask, cannot.

5The full transformer block, dimension by dimension

Intuition first

We now have the two main parts. Multi-head attention lets positions share information across the sequence: the “mixing across words” step. But it is mostly a weighted average, and it does little computation on each word by itself. So each block pairs it with a feed-forward network (FFN). This is a small two-layer network applied to each position separately: the “think hard about this one word” step. Around both, we add two support devices: residual connections and LayerNorm. That is the complete transformer block. Stack it $N$ times and you have a transformer.

☂ Everyday parallel

Think of a team working on a problem in rounds. Each round has two phases. First, a meeting: everyone shares what they know and updates their view. That is attention, mixing information between people. Second, desk work: each person works alone on what they just heard. That is the FFN, computation for each position. After many rounds, the team shares a rich understanding. A transformer is that team, and one block is one round.

Now the math

Residual connections. We do not replace the input with a sub-layer's output. We add the output to the input: $\mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})$. This is the skip connection that made very deep image networks trainable in Section 16. It helps here for two reasons. It gives each sub-layer an easy “do nothing” option: output zero, and the input passes through. And it gives the gradient a direct path back through the addition, so the signal reaches the first layers without fading. The two sides of the addition must have the same shape. That is why every sub-layer keeps the width $d_{\text{model}}$.

LayerNorm. Before each sub-layer we normalize each position's vector. We subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation. Then we rescale and shift with two learned vectors, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, each of length $d_{\text{model}}$:

$$ \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\gamma} \odot \frac{\mathbf{x} - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \boldsymbol{\beta} $$

In words: $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the mean and the variance of the $d_{\text{model}}$ numbers in this one vector. $\epsilon$ is a small constant (such as $10^{-5}$) that prevents division by zero. $\odot$ is element-by-element multiplication. This keeps the numbers that flow through the network at a steady size, so they do not grow or shrink from layer to layer. Section 20 treats normalization properly.

The feed-forward network. Two linear layers with a nonlinearity between them, applied in the same way to every position. For one position's vector $\mathbf{x}$ (a column vector of length $d_{\text{model}}$):

$$ \text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}_2\,\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 $$

The shape widens and then narrows on purpose:

StepShape in → outMatrix
expand$d \to 4d$$\mathbf{W}_1$: $4d \times d$
nonlinearity$4d \to 4d$ReLU / GELU (no weights)
project back$4d \to d$$\mathbf{W}_2$: $d \times 4d$

In words: here $d$ is short for $d_{\text{model}}$. The FFN makes each position's vector four times wider. It applies a nonlinearity: $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$ keeps positive numbers and sets negative ones to zero. Then it makes the vector narrow again. The wide middle gives the network room to compute richer features for each word. The factor 4 is the standard choice from the original paper. Importantly, the FFN acts on each position independently. It does no mixing between positions; all of that happened in attention.

Assembling the block (pre-norm order)

Most modern transformers put the LayerNorm before each sub-layer. This is called “pre-norm”, and it trains more stably than the original “post-norm” order, which normalized after the addition. The full block is two lines:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{MultiHead}\big(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{FFN}\big(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned} $$

In words: normalize, mix across words with attention, and add the result to the input. Then normalize again, process each word with the FFN, and add that result too. The input shape is $T \times d_{\text{model}}$, and the output shape is the same. This is on purpose, so the next block can take the output directly.

◆ The shape never changes, and that is the point

Every sub-layer takes $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ in and gives $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ out. Attention keeps it, the FFN keeps it, LayerNorm keeps it, and the residual addition requires it. Because of this, you can stack 12, 48 or 96 identical blocks. The whole depth of a large model is this one shape, repeated.

▲ Watch out

The block in this lesson is the classic design. Recent large models change some parts. Many use RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm: it only divides by the root mean square and does not subtract the mean. Many replace the ReLU/GELU FFN with SwiGLU, which multiplies two linear projections, one passed through a smooth gate, before projecting back. And most use RoPE for position (Section 4 above). The structure is the same: pre-norm, attention, residual, pre-norm, FFN, residual.

The same thing in PyTorch

multi-head attention and a full transformer block, with shapes on every line

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class MultiHeadAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        assert d_model % n_heads == 0                    # heads must divide the width evenly
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_k = d_model // n_heads                    # slice size per head
        # one big Linear does all heads at once (cheaper than n_heads separate ones)
        self.W_q = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_k = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_v = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)
        self.W_o = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)           # the final mixing matrix W_O

    def forward(self, x):                                # x: [B, T, d_model]
        B, T, _ = x.shape
        # project, then split the width into heads: [B, T, d_model] -> [B, n_heads, T, d_k]
        def split(t):
            return t.view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.d_k).transpose(1, 2)
        Q, K, V = split(self.W_q(x)), split(self.W_k(x)), split(self.W_v(x))

        scores  = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1) / self.d_k ** 0.5   # [B, n_heads, T, T]
        weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)                   # [B, n_heads, T, T]  rows sum to 1
        out     = weights @ V                                 # [B, n_heads, T, d_k]

        # concat heads back together: [B, n_heads, T, d_k] -> [B, T, d_model]
        out = out.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, T, self.n_heads * self.d_k)
        return self.W_o(out)                                  # [B, T, d_model]


class TransformerBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, d_ff=None):
        super().__init__()
        d_ff = d_ff or 4 * d_model                       # the standard 4x wide middle
        self.attn  = MultiHeadAttention(d_model, n_heads)
        self.norm1 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.norm2 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.ffn = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d_model, d_ff),                    # [B, T, d_model] -> [B, T, d_ff]
            nn.GELU(),                                   # smooth relative of ReLU
            nn.Linear(d_ff, d_model),                    # [B, T, d_ff] -> [B, T, d_model]
        )

    def forward(self, x):                                # x: [B, T, d_model]
        x = x + self.attn(self.norm1(x))                 # pre-norm attention + residual add
        x = x + self.ffn(self.norm2(x))                  # pre-norm FFN + residual add
        return x                                         # [B, T, d_model]  same shape in and out


torch.manual_seed(0)
block = TransformerBlock(d_model=512, n_heads=8)         # batch 2, sequence 10, width 512, 8 heads
x = torch.randn(2, 10, 512)
print(block(x).shape)                                    # torch.Size([2, 10, 512])
print(sum(p.numel() for p in block.parameters()))        # 3152384

# permutation equivariance: shuffle the 10 positions, outputs shuffle the same way
perm = torch.randperm(10)
print(torch.allclose(block(x[:, perm]), block(x)[:, perm], atol=1e-5))   # True

In words: the batch dimension B travels in front the whole way. That is why our earlier scaled_dot_product_attention used a leading .... The code has no loop over heads. Reshaping to [B, n_heads, T, d_k] lets one batched matrix multiply do all heads at once: this is the parallelism promised at the start of the lesson. The parameter count is 1,050,624 for attention (four 512 × 512 matrices with biases), 2,099,712 for the FFN, and 2,048 for the two LayerNorms. The last line checks the equivariance from Section 4 on a whole block.

◆ Where this goes next

This block is the encoder form: every position may look at every other. To turn a transformer into a text generator, we add one thing: a causal mask that stops each position from looking at words that come after it. That change is the subject of Section 19. In very large models, the FFN is often replaced by a mixture of experts. This is many FFNs, with a router that picks a few of them for each token. That is Section 22. There, only the feed-forward sub-layer changes; the masked self-attention works as it does here.

Check yourself Find the bug: someone changes the FFN's last layer to nn.Linear(d_ff, 2 * d_model). What error do you get, on which line, and why? Reveal

The error comes from the residual addition x = x + self.ffn(self.norm2(x)). The left side is [B, T, d_model] and the right side is [B, T, 2*d_model]. The last dimensions (512 and 1024) are different and neither is 1, so broadcasting fails and PyTorch raises a RuntimeError about the sizes. Even if the addition were allowed, the next block expects width d_model, so the stack would break there. This is why the FFN can widen to 4*d_model in the middle but must project back to d_model at the end. The residual path fixes the width from input to output.

6What to carry forward

  • Attention is a soft lookup. Queries ask, keys label, values are the content. Each position gets a weighted blend of all values, graded by how well its query matches each key.
  • The formula is $\text{softmax}\big(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k}\big)\mathbf{V}$: compare, rescale, blend, mix. Shapes: $(T\times d_k)(d_k\times T)\to(T\times T)$, then $(T\times T)(T\times d_v)\to(T\times d_v)$.
  • The $\sqrt{d_k}$ is derived, not decoration. Raw scores have variance $d_k$. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to 1 and keeps the softmax away from its flat, near-zero-gradient region.
  • Multi-head attention splits $d_{\text{model}}$ into $h$ heads of size $d_{\text{model}}/h$. It runs attention in each head, concatenates the results, and mixes them with $\mathbf{W}_O$. You get several kinds of relationship for the parameter count of one.
  • Attention is permutation-equivariant, so we give it position: sinusoidal or learned vectors added to the input, or rotations of queries and keys (RoPE).
  • The block is: LayerNorm → multi-head attention → residual add → LayerNorm → FFN ($d_{\text{model}}\to 4d_{\text{model}}\to d_{\text{model}}$) → residual add. The shape in equals the shape out, so blocks stack.

You now know the core of a modern language model. Next we add a causal mask and turn this machine into something that writes. That is Section 19, language modeling.
